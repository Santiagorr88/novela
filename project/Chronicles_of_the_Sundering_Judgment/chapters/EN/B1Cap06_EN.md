# Chapter 6 — Whispers in the Burning Halls

## The Games of Court

Court, on an ordinary evening, ran on rivalries too small to name and too constant to ignore. Belial watched it from his customary place near the dais, half-attentive, the way he watched most of Hell's daily theater — two lesser lords trading veiled insults over a disputed border garrison, each pretending the other's presence was beneath comment while making very sure to be overheard; a scribe hurrying past with a sealed report, chased by a junior officer angling for whatever favor might be bought by delivering it first. None of it mattered, and all of it was taken, by everyone involved, with total seriousness.

He had long since stopped finding any of this entertaining. Most of what passed for ambition in this court was simply fear wearing better clothes, and he had watched too many centuries of it to feel much beyond a distant, familiar boredom. Every rivalry in this room would resolve itself the same way it always did — a favor traded, a debt called in, one lord slightly diminished and another slightly emboldened, and the whole arrangement reset within a season or two to make room for the next round of the same small war.

The doors at the far end opened before the scribe reached the dais, and whatever business had occupied the room a moment before dissolved instantly into silence.

## The Report

The survivor who stumbled through those doors did not look like a soldier anymore. His armor hung in pieces, one wing dragged rather than folded, and his voice shook on every third word. He fell to his knees before Lucifer's throne without waiting to be granted the privilege, a breach of protocol no one in the room seemed inclined to correct.

"It wasn't a battle," he said, when someone finally asked him to speak. "It was an unmaking. The sword didn't cut. It just — undid. Everything it touched simply stopped being there."

Laughter rippled through the assembled lords before he had finished the sentence. One of the border-garrison rivals called out something about angelic propaganda and morale-boosting fairy tales, and several others took up the theme, the tension of a moment ago dissolving into easy mockery. A demon lord near the front actually applauded, as though the survivor had delivered a piece of especially entertaining theater rather than a report. The survivor did not defend himself. His shoulders loosened once the court's attention moved elsewhere, and Belial took it for relief. He knelt where he was and let the laughter wash over him.

Belial did not laugh. He held very still, his eyes fixed on the trembling soldier, and let the noise around him fall away from his attention entirely, the way it always did when something genuinely warranted his focus. He had heard a hundred reports of angelic weaponry over the long war, each one exaggerated in the retelling, each one eventually reducible to ordinary steel and ordinary fire dressed up in fear. This description did not reduce. It described a logic he did not recognize, and Belial had learned, over centuries of command, to take seriously exactly the things he did not recognize.

## A Name Overheard

Foras stood near enough to be heard without raising his voice, unhurried as always, and did not bother directing his comment at anyone in particular. "Ghost stories," he said, watching the survivor's continued trembling from across the hall with something that might have been pity and might have been contempt. "Good enough to frighten those who still believe in something."

Belial did not turn toward him. "Then be glad you believe in nothing, Foras. You'll have nothing to lose when it turns out to be true."

The words carried no heat, delivered as flatly as a man commenting on the weather, and that flatness was what made the room go quiet around them for a moment. Foras inclined his head slightly, a gesture Belial chose to read as acceptance, showed no visible offense, and said nothing further. Belial's attention had already returned to the kneeling survivor, but the comment had landed somewhere he had not intended, pricking at a pride he rarely let anyone see the edges of. He filed the small irritation away and told himself, firmly, that it was beneath his notice. It was not, entirely, and he knew it.

## The Pride of Belial

Later, alone in his private chambers, Belial allowed himself the anger he had not shown in the throne room.

His walls held the accumulated trophies of a very long career — banners taken from broken legions, weapons stripped from rivals who no longer had use for them, a throne carved, deliberately and without apology, from the bones of the last commander who had seriously challenged his position. He had walked past these trophies ten thousand times without a second glance, and tonight he found himself studying each one in turn, as though counting them might settle something the report had unsettled. Every object in this room existed to remind him, and anyone who entered it, exactly what he was. He had built that reputation inch by inch, century by century, and he did not intend to watch it made irrelevant by a sword he had not even seen.

This was not, he told himself, about Heaven's war effort. Heaven's war effort had never much interested him one way or another — territories changed hands, generals rose and fell, and none of it touched the deeper contest that actually mattered to him. This was a challenge laid at his own feet, whether its bearer knew it or not — a power operating by rules Belial did not control, in a war he had spent an eternity mastering the rules of. He would not be rendered obsolete by something he did not yet understand. He intended to understand it, whatever that required.

He crossed to the throne of bone and sat, unhurried, letting the familiar weight of the room settle back around him the way it always did when his composure needed rebuilding. On any other night it would have been enough. It worked less well than usual tonight.

## An Old Wound

He remembered, unbidden, a humiliation centuries old and never quite healed — an early campaign, long before his current rank, when a rival commander had dismissed his tactics in open council and won the following battle through sheer force of numbers alone, making Belial's careful strategy look like cowardice dressed as cleverness. He remembered the specific silence that had followed that battle, the way the other officers had avoided his eyes for weeks afterward, more clearly than he remembered most victories from the same era. He had eventually surpassed that rival and buried the memory beneath centuries of victories since, and had not thought of the man's face in longer than he could easily calculate.

The shape of that old contempt returned to him now, faint but unmistakable, the way it always did whenever something threatened to make him small again. He set his jaw against it, the way he always did, and let it settle into something closer to resolve. He would not be made small again. Not by a sword, and not by whatever contempt Belial had heard beneath Foras's remark.

## A Question for the Morningstar

He found Lucifer where he most often found him — not in the throne room, not among the court's endless maneuvering, but in the vast, silent library that occupied the quietest wing of the palace, a single lamp burning over whatever page currently held his attention. Shelves rose beyond the lamp's reach in every direction, more books than any single mind could read in the span of the war, and Lucifer had, as far as Belial could tell, made real progress through a significant portion of them.

"You've heard the report by now," Belial said, without preamble. "What is it?"

Lucifer did not look up from his book. "There are old songs about such things, Belial. Older than this war — or so their singers claimed." He turned a page, unhurried. "Some notes are best left unsung."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Lucifer agreed, closing the book with unhurried care, and finally raised his eyes, mild amusement flickering across his face. "It isn't."

Belial left the library with more questions than he had entered it carrying, and beneath the questions, a suspicion he could not shake: that Lucifer knew considerably more than he was saying, and had chosen, for reasons of his own, to say nothing at all. He did not ask a third time, nor a fourth. He had learned long ago that pressing Lucifer past the point he intended to answer never produced more truth, only a more elaborate way of declining to give it.

## The Tower of the Eternals

The Tower of the Eternals stood alone in a forgotten corner of the realm, far from the capital's noise, a spiraling edifice of black iron that hummed faintly with knowledge no one had dared catalogue properly in longer than Belial cared to calculate. He went there directly from the library, Lucifer's non-answer still sitting cold in his chest.

He had visited the tower perhaps a dozen times in his long service, always for some narrow, practical purpose — a name to verify, a treaty to confirm, a rival's lineage to disprove. He had never once come here for himself, chasing a question with no clear shape, and the difference showed in how carefully he now studied the entrance, as though it might refuse him for asking something the tower was not accustomed to being asked.

The air inside was thick with dust and with something less physical than dust — old paradoxes, half-finished thoughts left hanging by scholars who had never returned to finish them. Screaming echoed faintly from the tower's lower levels, the residue of psychic wards long since triggered and never fully silenced, and Belial had long ago learned to stop flinching at it. Spiral stairs wound upward past level after level of shelving, each floor darker and more disused than the one below it, the deeper archives clearly meant to discourage exactly the kind of visitor Belial had become tonight. He walked past all of it without slowing, his eyes fixed on the highest archives, the ones sealed away from casual scholars for reasons the seals themselves no longer bothered to explain.

A withered archivist intercepted him near the seventh landing, robes hanging loose on a frame that looked as though it had not eaten in centuries, and asked, in a voice like old paper tearing, what business a commander of Belial's rank could possibly have this high in the tower. Belial did not slow his stride. "Business that isn't yours to question." The archivist withdrew without further argument, retreating back into whatever shadow he had emerged from without another word, and Belial continued climbing.

He was not here merely to find a weapon to match Solmire. He was here because Lucifer had told him, in the politest possible terms, that some truths were not his to have — and Belial had never, in all his long service, been able to leave a locked door unquestioned once he knew it existed. That particular flaw, if it was a flaw, had made him useful more often than it had made him a fool, and he saw no reason tonight should be the exception.

## First Blood for Knowledge

The tower's lowest ward required a price before it would yield its higher archives, and Belial had brought a minor demon for exactly that purpose — one of the countless expendable creatures that filled Hell's ranks without ever earning a name worth remembering. He had made this kind of trade before, more times than he could count over the long war, always with the same brisk efficiency, never once pausing to consider the creature offered as anything more than a key shaped like a body.

The ward itself was a simple thing to look at — a plain iron door set into the tower's inner wall, unremarkable except for the faint, patient hum that seemed to come from somewhere behind it rather than from the metal itself, as though it were waiting rather than merely standing shut.

The creature did not resist when Belial gestured it forward. It stood for a moment at the threshold, small and stooped and entirely unremarkable, the kind of low-ranking thing Belial had walked past a thousand times in his long service without a second glance. It simply looked at him, once, with an expression that might have been fear — a stillness Belial might have mistaken for the resignation of something that had always expected to end this way. Belial felt, briefly, something that was not quite guilt — an acknowledgment, perhaps, that even the least of Hell's soldiers carried some small flicker of a self before the ward consumed it. He had not expected to feel anything at all, and the fact that he did unsettled him more than the feeling itself. The sensation passed before he had finished naming it, replaced by the older, more familiar patience of a man waiting for a door to open.

The seal on the archive door unmade itself in a wash of pale light, and Belial stepped through without looking back at what he had left behind, already reaching for the first volume that caught his eye on the shelves beyond.
