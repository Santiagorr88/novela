# B2C22 – City of Whispering Shadows

Navarion unsettled Arin more than any battlefield he'd ever crossed, and he'd spent the better part of a week trying to name exactly why before he finally admitted the answer to himself. It wasn't danger. He knew danger the way other men knew their own reflection, could read it in a room the instant he crossed the threshold, and this campus, on its surface, offered nothing that fit the shape of it. Students crossed quiet quads between lectures. Lights burned late in library windows over papers and exams. It was, by every visible measure, one of the safest places he'd stood in the entire month since the Grey City.

That was precisely what unsettled him. A war zone told him, plainly, exactly where to point his attention. This place hid its wrongness somewhere underneath an ordinary Tuesday, and Arin found himself checking the same quiet corners three and four times, certain he'd missed something the first pass, and finding, each time, only more of the same convincing calm.

He had crossed cities eaten alive by despair before, the Grey City chief among them, and had learned that particular kind of wrongness well enough to recognize it anywhere. Navarion offered him none of that familiar signature. Students laughed in the quad at midday. Couples argued about nothing over coffee outside the student union, the ordinary, low-stakes arguments of people who had never once had to consider whether tomorrow was guaranteed to them. He found himself almost resenting how easy it all looked, a resentment he recognized, uncomfortably, as envy wearing a more acceptable disguise.

---

He walked the grounds at night, when the crowds thinned and the campus's true shape became easier to read, and catalogued what he found without yet understanding what any of it meant. A lecture hall on the humanities building's second floor held a silence that didn't sit right against his ears, a held-breath quality no empty room should have carried this consistently across three separate nights of walking past it. A faculty office in the same building kept its light burning past midnight more often than any reasonable workload should have demanded, the same window lit whether Arin passed at eleven or at two.

He noted the office's location on his second pass without particular interest — third floor, corner window, a nameplate on the door too far away to read from the quad below — and moved on toward the library instead, drawn by something closer to the trail he actually understood himself to be following. He had no reason yet to connect a professor's late hours to anything larger than an approaching deadline or a stubborn insomnia. Plenty of people worked too late in a place like this. He filed the detail away regardless, the same reflexive habit that had kept him alive across a decade of contracts where the thing that seemed irrelevant on the first pass sometimes turned out, weeks later, to have been the only detail that mattered.

He filed both details away without connecting them to anything larger, the way he filed every anomaly he hadn't yet earned the right to understand. Neither felt like a threat exactly. Both felt like something worth remembering regardless, the specific instinct of a man who had learned, across a career built on exactly this kind of patient attention, that the things which didn't quite fit were usually the things that mattered most eventually.

---

He found the concentrated source on the fourth night, after three nights of the ambient corruption blurring together into a single directionless hum he'd learned to filter out entirely. This was different — sharper, localized, distinct enough from the general noise that he stopped mid-stride the first time it registered, certain for a moment that he'd imagined it before it steadied into something he could actually follow. He traced it slowly at first, doubling back twice to confirm the direction hadn't shifted, before he let himself trust it enough to commit to a real pace.

The pull carried him past the library, past the humanities building with its held-breath silence, toward the older, half-forgotten wing where the university stored records no one had properly catalogued in decades. The corridor grew colder as he went, the ordinary hum of a functioning campus thinning out behind him until the only sound left was his own careful footsteps against old stone.

The trail went cold at a locked service door, warded in a way that told him plainly this was no accident of neglect. He tested the ward carefully, without touching it directly, and felt it push back against his attention with a resistance too deliberate to belong to ordinary campus security. Something down here wanted to stay hidden badly enough to have spent real effort ensuring it, and effort like this, in his long experience, was never spent protecting anything harmless. He crouched at the door's base a while longer, studying the ward's construction the way he might once have studied a rival's defenses before a job, cataloguing weak points he had no intention of exploiting tonight.

He didn't force the door tonight. Forcing it would announce, to whatever watched this corridor, that someone had finally noticed what it was hiding, and Arin had learned, across a month of exactly this kind of patient work, that the advantage of being unnoticed was worth considerably more than the satisfaction of moving quickly.

---

The security guard he approached the next evening carried himself with the particular weariness of a man fifteen years into a job that had stopped surprising him around year three, his uniform pressed out of long habit rather than any remaining pride in the wearing of it. Arin introduced himself as an outside investigator, a lie broad enough to survive casual scrutiny, and let the man's own restlessness do most of the work before he'd even finished the first question.

"Anything strange this week?" Arin asked. "Someone where they shouldn't be, cameras glitching, that kind of thing." He kept his tone light, professional, the particular flatness of a man asking a routine question he expected a routine answer to, and watched the guard's face for whatever the question actually shook loose.

The guard hesitated, visibly weighing whether the honest answer was worth the risk of sounding unstable. "There's a guy in sunglasses," he said finally, "who shows up and disappears near doors that are supposed to be locked. I haven't reported it, because it sounds insane, and I've gone fifteen years here without saying anything insane out loud."

"It's not insane," Arin said, offering nothing further to explain why he believed it so readily.

The guard studied him a moment, some of his composure visibly slipping. "That's not exactly reassuring, coming from an investigator."

"I didn't say it to reassure you." Arin let the silence that followed sit exactly as long as it needed to. "I said it because you deserved an honest answer more than a comfortable one."

The guard didn't respond to that, and Arin left him standing there considerably more unsettled than he'd found him, which had never once been the intention but rarely failed to be the result. He walked away with a clearer picture now than he'd had an hour earlier — sunglasses in the dark, a talent for doors that shouldn't have opened, moving through a campus that had apparently already learned to look away from exactly the sort of thing it should have been reporting. That kind of camouflage didn't happen by accident. Someone, or something, had been working this particular stretch of ground for long enough to make its presence feel ordinary.

---

He spotted the demon two nights later, wearing the guard's own uniform with the particular sloppy confidence of something that had never once expected to be recognized for what it actually was. Low-ranked, unremarkable, exactly the kind of disguised infiltrator this operation would have posted to watch a location this valuable without drawing attention to how valuable it actually was.

He didn't move on it. Every trained instinct in him wanted to end the threat cleanly and immediately, the same instinct that had served him through a decade of contracts where hesitation had only ever cost lives. He held it in check instead, patient in a way that cost him something to maintain, because a public execution on a campus this crowded would draw exactly the kind of attention that would burn whatever advantage his month of careful, quiet hunting had bought him.

He followed the creature from a distance instead, cataloguing its patrol route across two full nights, its blind spots, the exact rhythm of when it checked in with whatever was watching it from below. The first night taught him its habits — a loop through three buildings, a pause at the warded service door long enough to suggest a report delivered rather than simple routine, a second loop back out through the same gate it had entered by. It moved with the same careless confidence he'd seen in every low-ranked infiltrator he'd tracked since the Grey City, competent enough to pass a mortal glance, careless enough to reveal itself completely to anyone who actually knew what to look for.

The second night he tested what he'd learned, positioning himself a full building ahead of where he expected the creature to pass and finding it there, on schedule, within two minutes of his own prediction. By the time the second loop finished, he understood its route well enough to predict it three moves ahead, and understood, too, exactly how little effort it would cost him to end this the moment ending it stopped being the wrong tactical choice.

---

He sat with the whole of it that night, in a stairwell far enough from the guard's route to think without interruption, and found himself circling a question he hadn't expected to still be asking after everything the Grey City had already cost him. This hunt felt different from every one that had come before it, and it wasn't only the patience the crowded campus demanded of him. It was something quieter, harder to name, a persistent uncertainty that had nothing to do with tactics at all.

He thought of the woman from the fourth cell, of the voice that had spoken through him in that corridor without his permission, of a month spent learning that the line between justice and simple violence had never once been as clean as his old ledger had liked to pretend. He had come to this campus hunting a demon and a portal and a network of horror he understood perfectly well how to feel angry about. Somewhere beneath all of that, unexamined until tonight, sat a second, quieter question he hadn't let himself ask directly until this exact stairwell, this exact hour.

For the first time since this had all begun, he wasn't certain the thing waiting for him at the end of this trail was actually his enemy.

He turned the thought over a long while, uncomfortable with how little it resembled any doubt he'd ever let himself entertain before a job. He had built an entire career on knowing exactly who deserved what he brought to them. He did not know that anymore, walking these quiet, ordinary halls, and the not-knowing sat heavier in him than any danger this campus had shown him yet.
