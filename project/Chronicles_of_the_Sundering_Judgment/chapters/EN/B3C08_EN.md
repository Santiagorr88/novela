# B3C08 – The Whispering Scars

Barachiel had held this watchtower for longer than most soldiers held anything, and the star-dusted void beyond its edge had never once, in all that time, given him reason to doubt the value of watching it. The outpost sat at the far edge of contested space, a lonely bastion of pale stone and older wards, its lower levels quartering a garrison of some forty soldiers and its upper levels given over entirely to the long, quiet work of observation. It hummed with the familiar, steady note of celestial energy that had accompanied every hour of his long vigil, a note he'd grown so accustomed to hearing that he suspected he'd notice its absence faster than he'd notice most other things going wrong. He stood at the watchtower's outer rail this evening as he stood most evenings, hands folded behind his back, feeling the particular, quiet pride of a guardian who had never once let his post go unwatched.

He had commanded here for centuries, through quiet stretches and through the occasional flare of contested skirmish, and had come to know the sky beyond his tower the way another man might know the streets of his own childhood. He knew which stars belonged where. He knew the slow drift of the nearby nebula, its familiar violet bloom, the shape it held against the deeper dark behind it. He had watched two of his soldiers marry beneath that same drift of stars, years ago now, a small ceremony held on the tower's lower terrace that he still thought of, on quiet nights, with more warmth than the occasion had probably warranted from a commanding officer. So when a small, subtle flicker disturbed that shape tonight, his first instinct was the same instinct nine centuries of service had trained into him — dismiss it, most likely a stellar flare, the kind of minor cosmic event that occurred somewhere in his field of view perhaps once a decade.

He watched it a moment longer anyway, old habit outweighing easy dismissal.

---

The flicker was not a flare. Barachiel watched, his easy dismissal giving way to confusion, as a patch of the nebula's familiar violet began to lose its color — not dimming the way light dims, not fading the way distance fades a thing, but simply ceasing to hold any color at all, as though that specific patch of sky had quietly stopped being permitted to stay visible. He blinked, certain his own eyes were somehow at fault, and rubbed one hand across them for good measure before looking again. The patch remained exactly as wrong as it had been a moment before, perhaps a fraction larger than it had been thirty seconds ago, though he couldn't yet say for certain whether that was actual growth or simply his own attention sharpening around it.

He called for a second opinion, unwilling to trust a report this strange to his own eyes alone, and one of his junior officers, a watchful young sentinel named Halaliel, joined him at the rail within moments, still fastening the last strap of her uniform as she crossed the platform toward him. She studied the patch a long, careful moment before speaking.

"I don't recognize it, sir," she said, her voice carefully even, the particular evenness of someone working hard to keep her own alarm out of her report. "It's not registering on any of the standard instruments. It's just... not there." She lowered the instrument in her hand and looked at him directly, something uncertain crossing her otherwise composed face. "Should I wake the rest of the garrison, sir? Just as a precaution?"

"Do it," he said, and found his own voice carrying more weight than he'd intended, an old commander's instinct surfacing before he'd fully decided how seriously to treat the request himself. Halaliel didn't question the order twice. She was already moving toward the alarm bell before he'd finished speaking.

He reached out with the deeper senses his long service had sharpened in him, the same instinct that let any trained sentinel read danger and safety in the ordinary hum of creation the way a sailor reads weather in the color of a distant sky. He found nothing. Not danger, not safety, not the familiar celestial resonance that had filled this post for the entirety of his long vigil. A spreading, total silence where the music of creation should have been, expanding steadily outward from that single wrong patch of sky.

Halaliel's own instruments, small and well-worn from years of steady use, gave her nothing more useful on the second attempt than they had on the first. She looked up at him, waiting for an order, and he found himself, for one uncomfortable moment, without a clear order to give her. Nine centuries of service had prepared him for a great many threats. None of them had prepared him for a threat his own instruments simply refused to register at all.

He felt, beneath his boots, the outpost's own protective wards begin to falter — not struck, not besieged, nothing that resembled an attack in any shape he'd ever trained to recognize. They simply weakened, the way a man's grip weakens not from injury but from slowly forgetting what holding on was actually for.

---

He gave the order to sound the alert anyway, his voice steadier than he currently felt, and watched his soldiers scramble to stations they'd drilled at a hundred times before, none of that drilling quite prepared for an enemy with no visible shape and no visible weapon. Halaliel moved to relay the order down through the lower levels, her steps quick but controlled, the discipline of long training holding steady even now. The zone continued its slow, patient expansion, consuming more of the nebula's violet with every passing minute, and Barachiel found himself unable to look away from it even though every instinct nine centuries of training had given him insisted he focus on his own tower's defenses instead.

He thought, watching it grow, of every threat this outpost had weathered across his long tenure — raiding parties driven off before they reached the outer wall, the occasional probing skirmish from the contested territories beyond, once, memorably, a rogue comet that had required three full days of careful redirection to keep from clipping the tower's outer wall. Every one of those threats had announced itself plainly, had given him something concrete to plan against, some shape he could set his soldiers and his own considerable experience squarely against. This gave him nothing but its own steady, wrongful growth, and the growing certainty, settling into his chest like a stone he couldn't set back down, that nothing in his considerable experience had prepared him to answer it.

His second-in-command, a steady young captain named Ophaniel who had served under him for the better part of a century, appeared at his side, already reporting readiness across every station. Barachiel had eaten a hundred quiet meals across from this man in the tower's mess hall, had trusted him with command of the entire garrison on the rare occasions duty had called Barachiel away. Barachiel opened his mouth to acknowledge the report, and found, for one long and terrible second, that he could not recall the man's name.

It came back to him an instant later, but the gap itself — the sheer, impossible fact that it had happened at all — left him more shaken than any wound he'd taken in nine centuries of active service. He studied Ophaniel's face a moment longer than the report required, searching it for some sign that the other man had noticed the lapse. Ophaniel gave no sign of it. Whatever had briefly taken his commander's name had apparently taken nothing yet from him.

---

The zone reached the outpost's outer boundary before he'd fully recovered from that first small failure of memory, and the cold dread he felt at its approach was unlike any fear he'd ever catalogued in himself before. He had felt the fear of death countless times, in countless engagements, and had long ago made a settled peace with it, the ordinary occupational fear of a soldier who understood that his service might someday cost him everything. This was not that fear. This felt instead like standing at the edge of something that would not simply end him, but would quietly, patiently unmake the fact that he had ever existed to be ended in the first place.

He looked at Ophaniel again, then out at the advancing edge of the zone, then back at Ophaniel once more, unable to settle his attention on either for longer than a few seconds at a time, and felt his own sense of self begin to fray at its edges, the way old parchment frays when handled too many times by too many careless hands. His name. His centuries of service. The particular, hard-won pride he'd taken in this vigil, in every quiet night he'd stood exactly where he was standing now, watching over a stretch of sky that had trusted him to watch it properly. All of it felt, suddenly, less solid than it had felt an hour ago, as though the very fact of having been Barachiel at all might turn out, under close enough examination, to have been considerably more fragile than nine centuries had ever led him to believe.

---

He understood, with a clarity that arrived far too late to actually help him, that this was not a fear of dying. He had never truly feared dying, not in any form he recognized. This was a fear of never having been, a distinction so much larger and so much worse than ordinary mortality that his mind, even now, struggled to hold the full shape of it steady. He thought, absurdly, of the wedding he'd watched on the lower terrace all those years ago, and found himself unable to say with any certainty whether that memory still belonged to him or had already started slipping toward whatever the zone had been doing to everything else it touched.

The zone crossed the outpost's outer wall. Barachiel felt the first of his own soldiers go silent along the tower's lower level, not screaming, not falling, simply absent from the steady hum of readiness reports that had filled the tower's communication channel a moment before. He searched for Halaliel's voice among the remaining reports and didn't find it. Ophaniel, beside him, had gone rigid, his own eyes fixed on the advancing edge of the zone with the particular stillness of a soldier who has finally run out of orders worth giving. Barachiel had perhaps seconds left, by his own rough estimate, and he understood exactly what he needed to spend them on.

He reached out with everything remaining of his own failing will, not toward his immediate chain of command, not toward the standard reporting channels nine centuries of protocol had trained into him, but directly toward the heart of the Celestial City itself, toward whichever Archangel might be close enough and attentive enough to catch a desperate, dying signal aimed at no one in particular and everyone who might still be listening. He had never once, in nine centuries of service, broken protocol this completely. He found he no longer cared whether anyone above him would ever have cause to question the decision.

*It's not killing us, it's un-writing us!* he sent, the thought already fraying at its own edges even as he formed it, the words harder to hold together with every passing instant, each one costing him more effort than the last to keep whole. *It's the forgetting...*

The message ended there, mid-thought, the watchtower and everything inside it silently and completely gone from a stretch of sky that, moments before, had held nine centuries of quiet, faithful vigil, and now held nothing at all. No wreckage marked where the tower had stood. No debris drifted outward to mark that a garrison of forty soldiers, a young sentinel named Halaliel, a steady captain named Ophaniel, and the commander who had watched over all of them had ever occupied that particular stretch of contested sky. The nebula's violet, further out, continued its slow and ancient drift, indifferent to the silence that had just settled over everything that had once stood watch beneath it.
