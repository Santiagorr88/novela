# B1C20 – The Master of Lament

## The Agony

Belial drove the spear forward against his own lieutenant's guard and felt his knees buckle beneath him a half-second before the blow landed, Lament's grief flooding up through his grip with a force that had nothing to do with the strength of the parry meeting it. He had trained against lesser weapons for centuries without once losing his footing mid-strike. Lament made a mockery of that history every time he raised her, each thrust costing him more than the training itself should have demanded.

He forced himself upright, jaw locked against the sound he refused to let escape, and reset his stance for another pass. He had tried, across the last several days, to wield her the way he had wielded every weapon before her — force applied cleanly, met with equal force, victory decided by whoever struck truer. Lament answered none of that logic. Every strike he landed returned some fraction of its grief directly back through his own arm, as though the spear itself resented being used for anything so simple as combat.

His training partner, a lieutenant named Sarael who had served him loyally since his earliest campaigns, pressed the advantage without hesitation, and Belial met the attack late, his guard a fraction slower than it should have been. He absorbed the blow along one shoulder rather than his ribs only through instinct built over centuries, and even that smaller mercy cost him a grunt he could not entirely suppress.

The fortress around them offered no comfort of its own, a stark training hall carved from the same black stone as the rest of his command, its high walls doing nothing to soften the sound of his own labored breathing as it echoed back at him. He had chosen this apartness deliberately, days ago, precisely so that whatever struggle Lament cost him would play out somewhere his enemies could not witness it. He had not accounted, choosing the location, for how many of his own demons would witness it instead.

He reset his footing a third time, refusing the brief respite Sarael's own fatigue offered him, and drove the spear forward again. Lament's grief answered as it always answered — not with the clean resistance of a weapon poorly balanced, but with something that felt, each time, like an old wound reopening in a place he could not point to on his own body. He had faced physical pain without flinching more times than he cared to count across his long existence. This was not that. This was something that bypassed the body entirely and went straight for whatever remained, underneath all his considerable armor, still capable of grieving.

## What His Lieutenants Begin to See

The training ground had gathered a modest crowd by the third pass, demons of considerable standing in their own right pausing their own drills to watch their commander's spear falter against a lieutenant who would ordinarily have posed him no real challenge at all. Belial felt their attention without needing to look up and confirm it, a familiar weight from centuries of command that had, until recently, always carried something closer to reverence than the quality he sensed gathering around him now.

He caught Sarael's expression shift mid-exchange, the lieutenant's usual deference thinning into what Belial judged to be something considerably more calculating, as blow after blow found its mark against a spear its wielder plainly could not yet control. Belial had built his own rise through exactly this kind of opening, watching weaker commanders falter and moving to claim what their weakness left exposed. He recognized what he suspected was the same arithmetic now forming behind Sarael's eyes, and behind the eyes of every demon watching from the training ground's edge, and understood precisely how little time he actually had before that recognition hardened into something he would need to answer with more than words.

He had commanded ambitious subordinates for centuries and had never once mistaken their loyalty for anything more permanent than the strength that currently justified it. He did not resent Sarael for the calculation he suspected was currently forming behind his eyes. He would have run the same calculation himself, in Sarael's position, watching a commander falter this visibly against a weapon he had claimed as a triumph only weeks earlier. Resentment would have cost him effort he had no surplus of to spend. He filed the observation away instead, alongside every other piece of information a competent commander needed to survive his own ranks, and returned his attention to the spear still fighting him with every strike.

## A Revelation in Pain

He went to one knee under the next exchange, Lament's grief surging through him with a force that briefly filled his vision with an image he had not summoned and did not welcome — a weeping archangel, vast and sorrowful, bent under some grief too large for any single frame to hold. The vision passed as quickly as it had arrived, leaving him gasping on the training ground floor with his lieutenants watching in a silence that felt, to him, considerably louder than any jeer would have.

He understood, kneeling there, exactly what he had been doing wrong across every one of these training sessions. He had spent every ounce of his considerable will trying to silence the spear's grief entirely, treating it as an enemy to be crushed the same way he crushed any other enemy foolish enough to oppose him. That approach had cost him everything and returned him nothing — every hour spent forcing the grief back inward had only left him weaker for the next exchange, the spear's sorrow accumulating with nowhere to go but back through his own body.

He thought back over every session since the Sepulcher, cataloguing the pattern he had somehow failed to notice until this precise moment. Each day he trained harder against the spear's grief, and each day the grief answered with correspondingly greater force, as though his own resistance were somehow feeding whatever fueled it rather than diminishing it. He had assumed, wielding every previous weapon of his long career, that force applied consistently eventually broke whatever opposed it. Lament had offered him nothing but proof to the contrary, week after exhausting week, and he had been too stubborn, until this moment on his knees, to read the proof correctly.

He did not need to stop resisting Lament. The thought arrived with a clarity that steadied him more than the pain itself had unsteadied him a moment earlier. He needed to stop spending that resistance entirely on himself, and start aiming a portion of it outward instead. The grief was not a curse he needed to make peace with. It was a weapon he simply had not yet learned to throw.

The vision of the weeping figure lingered at the edge of his thoughts even as the clarity settled into something more useful, and he set it aside deliberately, unwilling to spend what remained of his concentration puzzling over an image he had not asked for and did not currently need explained. Whatever the vision had actually been, it had at least arrived alongside the single insight he had failed to reach on his own across days of fruitless repetition, and that insight, he judged, was the only part of it worth keeping hold of tonight.

## Weaponizing Sorrow

He rose from the training ground floor without conceding a single measure of ground to the spear itself, his will locked as hard against her as it had been since the moment he first claimed her from the Weeping Sepulcher. He would not surrender to Lament. He had never once considered surrender an option worth entertaining, and tonight offered him no reason to start. What he opened, deliberately and for the first time, was an outlet — a channel through which the grief he had spent days trying to contain entirely within himself could instead be aimed, precisely, at whoever stood opposite him.

He drove the next thrust forward with that intention fully behind it, and watched Sarael's guard collapse not from the blow's physical force but from something considerably worse arriving alongside it. The lieutenant went to his knees mid-parry, weeping openly, overwhelmed by what looked, to anyone watching, like the accumulated weight of every betrayal and failure his long service had ever cost him.

The cost remained entirely Belial's own to bear. Directing the grief outward rather than absorbing it whole cost him nearly as much as containing it had, a fresh wave of sorrow bleeding through his own chest with every ounce he redirected toward his lieutenant. He straightened anyway, jaw set hard against the strain, no closer to peace with the spear than he had been at the start of the session, and considerably closer to mastering exactly what she could be made to do to someone else.

He tested the working a second time against a fresh opponent, wanting proof that the first success had not simply been Sarael's own particular vulnerability finding him at a convenient moment. The result held. A second lieutenant crumpled under the same directed grief, and a third after him, each collapse costing Belial roughly the same toll as the one before it, the pattern repeating with a consistency that told him more clearly than any single success could have that he had found something real rather than something merely lucky. He filed the confirmation away with the same cold satisfaction he gave any successfully tested strategy, setting aside, for now, the considerable price each demonstration continued to extract from him.

## What Foras Watched Shrink

Among the gathered legionnaires stood Foras, his multiple unblinking eyes fixed on the spectacle with an attention that gave away nothing of what moved behind them. Belial noticed him there without turning his focus fully away from Sarael, some old instinct for reading a room never fully switched off even mid-working.

Foras's own position had grown considerably more precarious since Belial's return from the Weeping Sepulcher, and narrower still, Belial understood, from whatever ground Foras had already been losing to Vepar in the maneuvering both men knew better than to discuss openly — a commander weakened on two fronts at once has little appetite for watching a rival grow only more essential to the war effort by the week. Watching Lament finally begin to answer to its wielder, Belial judged, offered Foras no new tool against him. It offered him considerably less room to maneuver than he had possessed even an hour earlier, a Belial who could not be questioned being a Belial who could not easily be replaced either.

Belial held Foras's gaze a moment longer than strictly necessary before returning his full attention to the training ground, a small, deliberate cruelty he permitted himself without regret. Whatever calculations were currently running behind those unblinking eyes, he suspected Foras had already arrived at the same conclusion Belial himself would have reached in his position, and found no particular need to make the moment any more comfortable for him.

He had never counted Foras among his allies, precisely, in all the years both had served the same cause — a useful subordinate, competent enough to leave largely to his own devices, but never a commander whose ambitions Belial had mistaken for loyalty. He felt no particular satisfaction watching Foras's position narrow further tonight, only the same flat calculation he applied to any development that left his own authority more secure than it had been an hour earlier. A war fought on two fronts, one against Heaven and one against his own increasingly restless command, was a war he intended to keep firmly weighted in his own favor on both counts.

## The New Campaign

He gathered his legions before the week was finished, Lament raised overhead in a grip that no longer betrayed him with every movement, the spear's grief now flowing outward through him rather than pooling uselessly within. The assembled ranks fell silent as he raised his voice, a cold despair radiating from the weapon in his hand that he could feel settling over the gathered demons even before he spoke a single word.

He had spent the intervening days testing the working against opponent after opponent, refining the aim of it the way he refined any weapon he intended to carry into a real campaign rather than a training hall. The cost had not lessened with practice, not meaningfully, but he had grown considerably more efficient at absorbing it, spending only what each demonstration actually required rather than bleeding out further reserves than the moment called for. He stood before his assembled legions tonight with that efficiency fully tested, and with no illusions remaining about what tomorrow's campaign would continue to cost him personally, whatever victories it delivered against Heaven's own ranks.

"We will not break their shields," he said, his voice carrying easily across the assembled ranks, Lament's edge catching what little light the fortress offered and turning it the color of old grief. "We will break their hearts."

The legions answered with a roar that shook the fortress walls, and Belial felt, standing at their head with the spear finally answering to his will, something considerably closer to satisfaction than peace — a war he had not yet finished fighting against the weapon in his own hand, folded now into a war he intended to win against everyone still foolish enough to stand against him.
