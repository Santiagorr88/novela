# B2C06 – An Echo in the Archives

The proposal Mikel had submitted to the department chair three weeks ago read, on paper, like a natural continuation of his existing work — *Mythological Echoes in Pre-Modern Military History*, a survey of the folk narratives that grew up around real campaigns, the way legend accreted onto fact until the two became inseparable. It was the kind of title that raised no eyebrows and required no explanation, the kind of research a man could disappear into for months without anyone thinking to ask what he was actually looking for. He had chosen the wording carefully, in the specific, guilty way a man chooses a lie he intends to keep telling himself as well as everyone else.

He was not, if he was honest with the version of himself that only surfaced at two in the morning, researching echoes of war anymore. He was researching something he didn't yet have a proper word for — a persistent, contradictory shape that kept surfacing whenever he let his mind wander too far from the material he was supposed to be teaching, a sense that the judgment he dreamed of night after night was not the whole of the story, only the part that had happened to land on him. He found himself, more mornings than not, sitting in his office with a stack of folklore anthologies that had nothing to do with his actual field, chasing a single half-formed idea through every culture that had ever bothered to write anything down about the men and gods who dispensed verdicts: that somewhere, in some telling he hadn't found yet, judgment and mercy might not have been opposites at all. He didn't know yet what he was looking for well enough to give it a proper name. He only knew that when he found the right thread, something in his chest — that same old, familiar absence — would recognize it before his mind caught up.

---

He found the footnote on a Tuesday, deep in the university's special collections, in a section of the stacks so rarely visited that the librarian had to consult a paper ledger to locate the shelf at all. The book itself was unremarkable — a privately printed ethnography of an island culture, the kind of academic vanity project that got printed in editions of two hundred and then forgotten by everyone except the estate that had funded it. Mikel had pulled it almost at random, following a citation trail three sources removed from anything relevant, and had nearly set it back down twice before his eye caught on a single line buried in a footnote near the end of the third chapter.

*The islanders speak of a sky-smith who forged a sword not from divine rage, but from a star's laughter.*

He read it twice, and then a third time, the hair on his arms rising in a way that had nothing to do with the archive's poor climate control. The author had dismissed the line as "fanciful nonsense, likely a corruption of a more conventional creation myth," and moved on without a second thought. Mikel could not move on. He sat with the book open on his knees for what felt like minutes and turned out, when he finally checked his watch, to have been the better part of an hour, the light through the high archive windows shifting from morning to early afternoon without his noticing it pass.

---

He had been sitting there long enough that the archivist noticed. She was someone he knew mostly by sight and occasional nod in the corridor — competent, a little sharp-tongued, the kind of person who ran the deep stacks with the quiet proprietary care of someone who considered the entire collection at least partly her own responsibility. She stopped at the end of his table with a cart of returns balanced on one hip and looked at him for a long moment before she spoke.

"You've been sitting with that same book since before my lunch break," she said. "Either it's the most gripping thing this collection owns, or you've fallen asleep with your eyes open. I've seen professors do both."

"Comparative folklore," Mikel said, which was true in the way a half-truth is true, technically accurate and entirely misleading. "I'm chasing down a citation trail for a new project. Turns out it goes deeper than I expected."

"Comparative folklore." She raised an eyebrow, not quite believing him and not quite willing to say so outright. "You've published exactly nothing on folklore in nine years, Ardon. You do sieges. You do doomed garrisons. Are you secretly writing a novel and hiding it behind a grant proposal?"

"Would you think less of me if I were?"

"I'd think you were finally doing something with your evenings besides marking." She glanced at the book properly for the first time, at the cracked spine and the faded, water-stained cover, and something in her expression shifted toward genuine curiosity. "That whole section, honestly — nobody comes back here anymore. I had to check the paper ledger to find you a call number, because it's not even in the digital catalogue properly. The last time anyone requested anything from that shelf was over twenty years ago, according to the log, and I don't even recognize the name who signed it out. Some visiting researcher, I think. Long before my time here."

"Do you remember what they were researching?"

"No idea. The log just has a name and a date. Nobody writes down *why* anymore, not back then." She shrugged, already turning back toward her cart. "Let me know if you need help finding anything else down here. It's nice having a reason to dust off a section nobody remembers exists."

She left him with the ledger detail sitting oddly in his mind, a small stray fact with no obvious use yet. He didn't know why it mattered that someone else had once stood exactly where he was standing, chasing something in this same forgotten corner of the archive. He kept it anyway, the same way he'd learned to keep the ache in his chest and the dreams he couldn't place — not understood, simply held, on the quiet theory that understanding would eventually catch up if he refused to let go of any of it first.

---

The footnote, once he chased it properly, opened onto a longer passage he'd nearly missed on his first pass — a description, secondhand and clearly embellished by generations of retelling, of a weapon that "judged with irreverent joy," forged by a smith the islanders called, in a phrase that didn't translate cleanly, something closer to *the one who laughs at what the world takes seriously*. The sword's judgments, the text insisted, were absolute — no criminal it turned against ever escaped its verdict — and yet never once, in any version of the story the author had collected, had it left behind grief. Only, strangely, relief. A weight lifted rather than a wound delivered.

It was the precise, exact opposite of everything Mikel remembered from the only judgment he'd ever felt land against his own body in a dream — a spear of pure, wrong-colored dark, driving into him with no laughter in it at all, only an old and patient cruelty. He read the passage a fourth time, trying to reconcile the two, and could not. He had no evidence the two things were even related. He only knew, with a certainty he couldn't yet justify to anyone, least of all himself, that he wanted them to be.

---

The text named a place before it exhausted itself into further speculation the author clearly hadn't trusted enough to commit to with confidence: an island, shrouded in enough contradictory legend and navigational myth that the author had spent an entire paragraph simply cataloguing the ways different informants disagreed about where, precisely, it could be found. One elder swore it lay three days' sail past a reef that appeared on no chart. Another insisted it could not be found by sailing toward it at all, only by sailing away from something else and letting the island find you instead. The author had thrown up his hands, in prose that carried the particular exasperation of a scholar who'd expected simple geography and been handed a riddle. Turein. Mikel stared at the name until it stopped looking like a collection of letters and started looking, instead, like the first solid ground he'd stood on in weeks. Not a myth. Not a feeling he couldn't name. A place, on a map, however unreliable that map might prove to be — and somewhere he might finally be able to walk toward instead of simply sitting still and waiting for the next dream to tell him more than the last one had.

He closed the book with hands that weren't entirely steady, and did not return it to the shelf.

---

He called the dean's office from his own before he could talk himself out of it, rehearsing the request twice in his head before he let himself dial, and delivered the field-research excuse he'd prepared with a fluency that surprised him — not because it was hard to say, but because it came out sounding, even to his own ear, almost entirely true.

"Turein." The dean said the name back to him with the particular flat skepticism of a man who had never once heard it before. "I'll be honest, Ardon, I had to look at a map twice just now to confirm it exists. You're sure this is worth the department's travel budget?"

"I think it might be the most important research I've done in years," Mikel said, and heard, distantly, how strange the sentence sounded coming out of his own mouth — sincere in a way his usual academic hedging never allowed itself to be.

There was a pause on the line, long enough that Mikel wondered if the call had dropped. "You know," the dean said finally, something almost gentler creeping into his voice, "in ten years, you have never once asked this department for so much as a single day off. Not for illness, not for family, nothing. Ten years of perfectly punctual attendance, and I never had to think about you missing a lecture. So I'll authorize the leave — of course I will — but I have to ask, professor to professor and not dean to faculty for a second: are you actually all right?"

"I'm fine," Mikel said, and heard, even as he said it, exactly how little conviction the words carried, the way a coin sounds different when it's counterfeit, a fraction of a tone off from the truth in a way that couldn't quite be named but could absolutely be heard. The dean didn't push. Mikel suspected, hanging up a few minutes later with the leave approved and a flight already half-booked in his mind, that the lack of pushing was its own kind of answer — one more person who had noticed something changing in him and decided, out of whatever mixture of respect and unease, not to ask the question a second time.

He sat with the phone in his hand a while longer after the call ended, looking at the old book still open on his desk to the page with the footnote, and let himself feel, for the length of exactly one held breath, something that was not quite hope and not quite fear, but sat close enough to both that he couldn't tell, anymore, which one was winning.
