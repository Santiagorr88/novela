# B1L02 — What the Council Won't Decide

*(Lament Arc, Chapter 2 of 6 — POV Iofiel)*

The Codex Mortalis did not give up its pages easily, and Iofiel had long ago stopped expecting it to. Knowledge that mattered was never handed over. It was excavated, one reluctant layer at a time, from a text that seemed to believe forgetting was a mercy it owed the living.

She had been three days at the same fragment — a scorched half-page, its script warped by whatever fire had tried and failed to erase it — when the shape beneath the damage finally resolved into something legible. Not a story. A location.

*Sealed where sorrow itself became solid, beyond judgment's reach.*

She had known, for longer than she liked to admit, that a second weapon existed. Every serious student of the Codex knew it, the way one knows a shape by the hole it leaves in a room. A sword of irreverent judgment implied a counterpart; nothing in creation this deliberate came alone. But implication was not evidence, and Iofiel did not deal in implication. She dealt in what could be footnoted.

This could be footnoted. A partial coordinate, cross-referenced against three older fragments she'd already catalogued, converged — not perfectly, but closely enough that the overlap itself was a kind of proof — on a single plane. An intermediate realm. Not Heaven's territory, not Hell's. A place built from grief rather than governed by it.

She read the fragment four more times before she let herself believe it, and then she did what she had done with every dangerous truth she'd ever excavated from this archive: she went to Gabriel first, because Gabriel, unlike most of the Council, could hear bad news without needing to immediately win an argument about it.

He listened the way he always listened, still and complete, and when she finished he was quiet long enough that she wondered if he'd understood the weight of what she'd just handed him.

"You're certain," he said. It wasn't really a question.

"I'm certain there's a location. I'm not certain what's still there to find." She hesitated, because the next part mattered and she wanted him to hear it as fact, not alarm. "If it exists, Gabriel, it's been sitting unguarded since before either of us had a name for guarding things."

"Then it can sit a little longer."

"Or it can't," she said. "That's why I'm bringing it to the Council instead of the archive."

---

The chamber was fuller than she'd hoped and quieter than she'd expected, the particular quiet of a room already exhausted by the war it kept having to discuss. Uriel was the first to break it, which surprised no one.

"A second weapon." He said it the way he said most things, like a man already three steps into an argument no one else had agreed to start. "And you're telling us this *now*, with Zaphor'el barely held and half our reserve fighting a plane that wasn't even ours to lose."

"I'm telling you now because I found it now," Iofiel said. "The Codex doesn't keep a schedule for my convenience."

"Then convenience isn't the question." Uriel was on his feet, which he did when he wanted the room to feel his urgency rather than merely hear it. "The question is whether we send someone before someone else does. You said it yourself — unguarded. Unguarded doesn't stay unguarded forever, not with the whole of Hell watching every tremor Solmire sends through the world."

He didn't say Belial's name. He didn't need to; the shape of the fear was the same regardless of whose hands eventually closed around it. What Uriel wanted, plainly, was to be first. Not out of greed — Iofiel had known him long enough to be sure of that much — but out of the particular dread of a commander who has already lost ground once this season and cannot stomach losing it twice.

"We can't spare an expedition," Gabriel said, and something in his voice made the room's attention swing toward him instead. He hadn't raised it. He rarely needed to. "Every capable unit we have is either holding a line or resting from holding one. You want to send soldiers chasing a rumor into a plane none of us has mapped, on the strength of a partial coordinate, while Miguel—" He stopped himself. Not because the thought wasn't finished. Because it was, and he didn't want the room to watch him finish it.

Iofiel watched him not finish it anyway. Everyone did. It was the kind of silence that said more than the sentence would have.

"While Miguel is exactly the reason we can't afford to be careless," Uriel said, quieter now, though not gentler. "If this thing exists and it finds its way into the wrong hands, careful is what gets us buried."

---

It was Zadkiel who ended the argument before it could become one worth having.

He hadn't spoken yet. He rarely did until a debate had exhausted its own momentum, preferring — Iofiel suspected — to let others waste their conviction on each other before spending his own. When he finally did, the room's noise didn't so much stop as concede.

"A partial coordinate," he said, "cross-referenced against fragments you yourself describe as damaged, converging on a plane no living soul has entered in an age none of us can date." He looked at Iofiel, not unkindly, but without any warmth spent on softening it. "That is not evidence, Wisdom Keeper. That is a compelling guess."

"It's the best guess the Codex has ever given us on this," Iofiel said.

"I don't doubt your diligence. I doubt the wisdom of moving resources — any resources, however small — on the strength of an inference, however elegant." Zadkiel's silver gaze didn't waver. "We are a Host at war. Every soldier we commit to a rumor is a soldier not defending a line that is real and already bleeding. If this weapon has waited this long unguarded, it can wait for proof."

"And if it doesn't?" Uriel said.

"Then we will have lost a spear we were never certain existed, to an enemy we cannot yet name, in a war for a location none of us has verified." Zadkiel let that sit. "I would rather answer for caution than for a legion spent chasing a legend."

It was, Iofiel thought, the kind of argument that was very hard to be angry at and very easy to resent. He wasn't wrong. That was the trouble with Zadkiel; he was rarely wrong, and being right in a room this frightened had a way of looking exactly like courage, whether or not it was.

---

The compromise, when it came, satisfied no one enough to be called a victory. No expedition — Zadkiel would not authorize the resources, and Gabriel, for his own reasons, did not fight him on it. But not nothing, either: a single scout, low-profile, sent to confirm or deny the coordinate before anyone committed further. Uriel argued for a squad. He was overruled. He argued for a captain, at minimum, someone with the standing to make a real judgment on-site. He was overruled again, more gently, by Gabriel this time, who simply pointed out that a captain's absence would be noticed and a scout's would not.

Iofiel said nothing further. She had brought them a fire; watching them agree to send one candle to investigate it felt less like caution and more like a room deciding, collectively, that it would rather not know how bad the news was until it had no choice.

She understood the arithmetic. Every soldier mattered. Every front was bleeding. She understood, too, watching the Council disperse with the particular relief of people who have postponed a decision and are calling it resolution, that understanding the arithmetic did not make it comfortable to sit inside.

The scout would leave within the day. It would take him time to cross a distance none of them could properly measure, to confirm a location built from fragments and inference, to report back through channels that were themselves not fast. It was, by any reasonable accounting, the responsible choice — proportionate, careful, exactly the kind of decision a Host husbanding its strength was supposed to make.

Iofiel gathered the fragment back into its case and did not say aloud the thought that followed her out of the chamber: that responsible and fast were not, in her experience, words that had ever once described the same decision at the same time. Somewhere beyond any map they owned, a door was standing open to whoever reached it first, and the Host that had found it had just chosen to walk toward it at the speed of its own caution.

She hoped, without much confidence, that caution would be fast enough.
