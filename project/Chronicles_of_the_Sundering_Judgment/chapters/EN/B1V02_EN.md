# B1V02 – A Gift With No Name

## An Ordinary Tuesday

Corin Vasse had spent the better part of six years fixing other people's cars in the same cramped garage on the edge of town, and most days he counted himself lucky for it — steady work, an honest trade, a boss who mostly left him alone as long as the bay stayed clean and the customers stayed happy. He had a particular fondness for the older engines, the kind that still rewarded patience and a careful ear over the diagnostic computers the newer models leaned on, and had spent that very afternoon coaxing a decade-old sedan back to life for a woman who had nearly given up on it entirely, her relief at the news worth more to him, most days, than the invoice itself.

Tonight was not a work night, which was exactly why he had let his friend Dez talk him into the corner booth at Halloran's instead of heading straight home like he usually did. Dez worked the counter at the hardware store two doors down from Corin's garage, and the two of them had fallen into an easy, undemanding friendship over the years mostly built out of proximity, mutual convenience, and a shared tolerance for the same handful of bars in this part of town. He nursed a beer he had been nursing for the better part of an hour, half-listening to the bar's usual Tuesday murmur, content in the particular way a man gets content when nothing in his life currently requires anything of him. He liked this bar precisely because nothing ever happened in it worth remembering the next morning — the same regulars at the same stools, the same television playing the same highlights on a low, forgettable loop above the counter, the same bartender who knew his order without needing to ask for it anymore, the same faded neon sign in the window that had needed replacing for years and probably never would.

Dez was midway through some long story about a coworker's disastrous attempt at fixing his own plumbing, the kind of story Corin had heard some version of a dozen times before from a dozen different friends, and Corin let it wash over him without paying it much real attention. It was, by every measure that mattered to him, an entirely ordinary night — the kind he had lived a thousand versions of already and expected, without particularly thinking about it, to live a thousand more of before anything in his life changed in any real way.

## What Starts Small

The argument two tables over started small enough that Corin barely registered it at first, two men trading the kind of sharp words that usually burned themselves out before anyone needed to get involved. He caught only fragments of it over Dez's ongoing story — something about money owed, or a favor not returned, the specifics less important than the rising edge creeping into both men's voices with every exchange. Neither man looked like the type Corin would have pegged for trouble on an ordinary night, which somehow made the whole thing feel more unsettling to watch rather than less. A few of the regulars nearby had already gone quiet, watching now instead of talking among themselves, the particular hush of a room sensing something about to tip past the point where it can easily be pulled back.

It did not burn out. Corin watched, still mostly a bystander, as the volume climbed and a third voice joined in on one side, then a fourth on the other, the argument pulling in bystanders the way arguments in crowded bars sometimes did when nobody wanted to be the one who backed down first. Dez had stopped his own story somewhere along the way, both of them now watching the same slow unraveling everyone else in the bar had started watching too, conversations at nearby tables trailing off one by one as more attention turned toward the corner where it was happening. Chairs scraped back. Someone's hand closed hard enough around a bottle that the gesture alone changed the temperature of the whole room. The bartender had already started moving toward the corner, calling out something about taking it outside that neither man appeared to be listening to at all. Corin felt his own pulse pick up without any clear decision on his part to get involved, the ordinary human instinct to brace for something about to go badly wrong in a space too small and too crowded to contain it safely.

## What He Doesn't Understand Doing

He was not sure, afterward, what exactly he did. He remembered standing, remembered some wordless certainty rising up in him that this needed to stop, a pull he had no more chosen than he had chosen his own heartbeat, and remembered nothing at all about the actual mechanism of how it stopped. He had felt, in the instant just before it happened, a strange heat move through his chest and out along both arms, gone again almost before he could register it as strange at all. Dez said something to him as he rose, some half-formed warning about staying out of it, but Corin was already moving before the sentence finished.

The room simply changed. Whatever had been building toward a fight a moment earlier softened all at once, the raised voices dropping without any of the men involved seeming to notice they had lowered them, hands unclenching from bottles and chair backs with no memory, Corin guessed from their faces, of having clenched them in the first place. One of the two men who had started the whole exchange reached out and gripped the other's shoulder, the gesture landing less like a challenge and more like something closer to an apology neither of them seemed to have words ready for yet. It was not that the anger vanished. It was that some thread of ordinary fondness Corin could now see must have already been sitting quietly underneath it all along — regulars who drank at the same bar most weeks, who probably knew each other's names and families and old stories — rose up and crowded the anger toward the edges of the room the way sudden company crowds out an argument nobody actually wanted to be having. He watched the two men who had nearly come to blows a moment ago start talking instead, low and almost friendly now, the kind of conversation that looked, from where Corin stood, like two men who had simply remembered, all at once, how long they had actually known each other. Corin stood there in the middle of it, breathing hard for reasons he could not account for, with no idea at all what he had just done or how he had done it.

## What the Room Does With It

The whole bar seemed to exhale at once, conversations picking back up around the room in a looser, easier register than they had carried even before the argument started. A woman near the bar caught his eye first, an odd, searching look on her face, and crossed the room toward him with a directness that made Corin want to take a step back before she even opened her mouth.

"Thank you," she said, and the intensity in it landed strangely on him, considerably heavier than anything he felt he had actually earned. Her eyes were still faintly damp, as though whatever she had just felt in that room had genuinely moved her, and she seemed to be searching his face for some further explanation he had no way of providing her. He had not thrown a punch, had not said anything he could actually remember beyond some half-formed word aimed vaguely at the room, had not, as far as his own memory could account for, done anything at all beyond standing up and wanting the fight to stop. He tried, for a moment, to remember anything more specific than that — a gesture, a word, some concrete action he could point to as the actual cause — and found nothing there but the wanting itself, stubbornly refusing to resolve into anything more explainable no matter how many times he turned it over. "I didn't do anything," he told her, and meant it, though the words felt thinner leaving his mouth than he wanted them to. She looked at him a moment longer, as though weighing whether to press the point, and then simply nodded and returned to her seat, leaving Corin standing there with a room full of strangers who all seemed, in one way or another, to be looking at him differently than they had ten minutes earlier. Even the bartender, who had been halfway to the corner ready to physically separate the two men a minute ago, gave him a small, grateful nod on the way back behind the counter, the kind of acknowledgment usually reserved for regulars rather than a man who mostly kept to himself and his own corner booth.

## What He Doesn't Say Out Loud

He sat back down across from Dez, hands not quite steady around his own glass, and said nothing for a while, running the last several minutes back through his head and finding no version of the story that made ordinary sense. Dez, for his part, had gone unusually quiet too, watching Corin with an expression that was still working its way toward some conclusion he had not reached yet.

He had felt something leave him in that moment, the same way a man feels a held breath finally go, though he could not have named what it was or where it had come from or why his own body had apparently known how to do it without asking his permission first. A quiet unease settled into him that had nothing to do with the argument itself and everything to do with the plain fact that some part of him, some capability he had never once suspected he carried, had just acted entirely on its own. He had spent his whole life, as far as he knew it, being an entirely unremarkable man — decent at his trade, forgettable in most rooms he walked into, never once the person a crowd turned to look at, never once the one anybody depended on for anything more dramatic than a working transmission. Nothing in thirty-odd years of ordinary living had prepared him for the sudden, uncomfortable sensation of being that person now, if only for the length of one strange minute in a bar he had chosen specifically because nothing here ever asked anything of him, in a life he had built, quietly and on purpose, around never having to be more than that. He turned his own hands over once, studying them like they might offer some explanation he clearly wasn't getting anywhere else, and found them exactly as unremarkable as they had always been — the same calluses from six years of engine work, the same small scar along one knuckle from a wrench that had slipped years ago, nothing in the ordinary shape of them that could account for whatever had just moved through that room.

## What Dez Notices

"What did you say to them?" Dez asked, leaning in with the particular curiosity of a man who had watched something happen and couldn't quite make the pieces fit either. "Looked like you lifted something off all of them at once." He glanced back once toward the corner where the two men were still talking, easier now than either of them had probably been all night, and then back to Corin with the same unresolved expression from a minute ago.

Corin shrugged, reaching for something that would sound like a reasonable answer even though he did not have one ready, buying himself a second longer by taking another pull from his glass first. "I didn't say anything strange. I just... talked." He did not fully believe it himself, even as the words left his mouth, and judging by the way Dez's eyebrows stayed raised a moment longer than the answer deserved, Dez did not entirely believe it either. Neither of them pushed the conversation any further than that, the two of them settling into the kind of silence old friends could sit inside comfortably even when neither one had anything useful left to say. Dez eventually picked his abandoned plumbing story back up, though with noticeably less enthusiasm than before, and Corin let himself pretend to listen while the actual weight of the evening sat elsewhere entirely, somewhere he had no ready name for and no particular interest in naming just yet. Corin finished his beer in something closer to silence than conversation, the unease from a few minutes ago still sitting quiet and unresolved somewhere behind his ribs, and found, walking out into the cold a while later, that he still had no better answer for himself than the one he had just given Dez.

He walked the three blocks home slower than usual, hands buried in his jacket pockets against a cold he barely registered, replaying the strange minute in the bar over and over without ever managing to make it land any differently on the second or third pass than it had the first time through. Somewhere underneath the confusion sat a smaller, quieter fear he did not yet have words for either — not fear of what he had done, exactly, but fear of not knowing what else, given the right room and the right moment, that same unnamed part of him might decide to do next, entirely without asking his permission first.
