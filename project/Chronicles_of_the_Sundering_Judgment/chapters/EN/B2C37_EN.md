# B2C37 – The Unaccounted Variable

The seer who brought the report could not stop his hands from shaking, and Belial noted the tremor with the same flat, professional attention he gave any messenger's tells. The scrying pool behind him still hadn't fully settled, its surface holding a faint gray shimmer that hadn't been there before tonight's viewing. In this court, fear belonged to whoever received bad news, never to whoever delivered it. This one had clearly forgotten that rule entirely, or judged tonight's news worth the lapse.

"Speak plainly," Belial said, not looking up from the reports already scattered across the table in front of him. "I have no patience left tonight for whatever softened version you rehearsed on the walk here."

The seer bowed low enough that Belial caught the faint tremor running through his shoulders as well as his hands, an unusual degree of fear from a man who had delivered dozens of difficult reports across his years of service without ever once losing his composure this completely.

"The Enforcer is gone, my lord. His entire squad with him." The seer's voice cracked on the second sentence, and he pressed on before Belial could demand he steady it. "Not slain. I want to be precise about that, because the distinction matters more than I can properly explain. They were denied. There is nothing left of them to recover. No bodies. No essence to reclaim or punish. Nothing." He swallowed hard, visibly gathering himself before he continued. "I've scried a thousand deaths in my service to this court, my lord. I've never once scried an absence like this."

Belial didn't react immediately. He had a category for slain soldiers, and a category for captured ones. He had built entire strategies around the ordinary math of both, knowing roughly what each would cost him and roughly what he could expect to recover from it. He had never once, in all his centuries of this war, needed a category for denied.

"Denied by what," Belial asked, finally setting the reports aside entirely.

"We don't know, my lord. The scrying that reached us shows only the aftermath. Dust, scattered across a corridor, and a silence where eight trained soldiers and a decorated officer should have been standing." He hesitated a moment before adding the rest. "I tried to trace it further back, my lord. Whatever caused it, the pool refused to show me the moment itself. I tried three times, from three different angles, and each attempt returned the same blank instant. I have never once had a scrying refuse me before tonight."

---

The council chamber, when Belial finally called it, carried a stillness he hadn't felt in it for longer than he cared to measure. These commanders had spent centuries training themselves never to show anything plainly at all, and none of them were managing it tonight. Foras stood near the chamber's rear wall, his usual cold composure visibly strained, one hand resting against the wall itself as though he needed the support of it. Even Vepar, who Belial had never once seen surprised by anything in the whole of his long service, kept his hands folded a shade too tightly for the gesture to read as ordinary calm.

Belial had watched this court absorb violence in every shape it came in across the whole span of this long war, without flinching, without once needing to be reassured. He had never once watched it absorb an event nobody in the room could name. He read the silence around him as the particular quiet of men measuring a threat against whatever weapons they personally commanded, and finding, one face after another, nothing in their own arsenal that seemed to answer it.

No one spoke first. That alone told him more than the seer's report had. Belial let the silence stretch a full minute before he broke it himself, unwilling to let this particular quiet outlast his own patience for it.

---

"Does this change anything," Vepar finally asked, his voice carrying a careful, testing quality that didn't quite manage to disguise the calculation underneath it, "about the arrangement between commanders that's been under discussion these last several months?"

Several faces turned toward Foras before their owners caught themselves and looked away again. Belial had always known the dispute between the two commanders was common knowledge across his court, whatever both men pretended in his presence. He'd let the pretense stand. A court arguing with itself rarely had much energy left over for questioning the one commander sitting above both sides of the argument.

Belial turned to face Vepar with a calm he did not entirely feel, aware of every eye in the chamber measuring how he'd answer a question this transparently self-interested at a moment this precarious. He let the silence stretch a beat longer than strictly necessary, giving the room time to feel the weight of it before he finally answered. "Save your ambitions," he said, his voice carrying none of the heat the moment might have earned, "for whenever we've confirmed we're still going to exist long enough to enjoy them."

Vepar held his gaze a long moment, something unreadable behind his own composed expression, and inclined his head once, conceding the point without conceding anything beyond it. The dispute between him and Foras hadn't ended. It had simply gone quiet, for now, next to a threat too large to argue past.

---

Belial sat alone once the chamber had finally emptied, going back over everything he actually knew about the two signals converging on that quiet corner of Earth. Not much. His own scout, before he died, had confirmed only that there were two of them, one sleeping and one loud, and that Belial ought to fear both equally. Nobody had ever told him what they were, and he'd never found the source that could tell him with any confidence. He had let that gap sit unfilled for weeks, confident enough in his own numbers to close it by force instead of understanding, the same calculation that had served him well across centuries of considerably harder engagements than this one had promised to be.

He no longer trusted that confidence. Eight soldiers and a decorated officer did not simply cease to exist for no reason at all, and he had no report, no fragment, nothing beyond a scrying pool that had refused, for the first time in his memory, to show him what had actually happened. He didn't know what had intervened. He didn't know why. He didn't know whether it had been waiting for him specifically, or for the two signals, or for something else entirely that had simply happened to be standing in the same corridor at the same hour. Every question he asked of the report only produced another question underneath it, and he had spent enough of his long existence hunting other men's secrets to recognize, uncomfortably, exactly how it felt to be the one left without any answers this time.

He weighed the possibilities cautiously, unwilling to trust any single conclusion built on evidence this thin. Perhaps the two men had been bait, set deliberately to draw him in. Perhaps they'd simply been standing too close to something else's business when it finally came due. He ran through every scrap of intelligence his court had gathered across the last month, searching for any detail that might settle the question one way or the other, and found nothing conclusive in any of it. He couldn't rule either possibility out, and found, without a scrap of real evidence to settle the question either way, that the not-knowing itself felt like the most dangerous fact in the entire report.

---

He summoned his remaining commanders back within the hour, standing rather than sitting when they filed in, and gave the order without softening any part of it for their comfort. "Abandon the harvesting operations. All of them, effective immediately. I want every soul-farm we currently run stripped down and its forces redirected to Hell's own borders." He watched the disbelief move across more than one face in front of him, several commanders exchanging brief, unreadable glances with each other before catching themselves, and let it pass without any acknowledgment at all. "We have spent this entire season treating Earth as a resource to be harvested. I am no longer confident that assumption was ever safe to make. Until I understand what actually intervened in that corridor, I would rather hold what we already have than lose more of it chasing what we don't."

"The Navarion operation alone represents years of investment," one of his commanders said, careful to keep any real objection out of his tone, though his eyes drifted briefly toward the ledgers he'd spent years compiling on his own private wall of the chamber. "Abandoning it entirely seems—"

"Premature, were the losses tonight smaller than they were." Belial didn't raise his voice at all to meet the interruption. "They weren't smaller. I have watched this court survive worse than a stripped harvesting network. I have never once watched it survive a commander too proud to admit he'd misjudged his enemy. I don't intend to be the first tonight, and I don't intend anyone else at this table proving it twice."

No one argued the order aloud. Belial understood, watching their faces, that the report of dust had done more to secure their compliance tonight than any threat he could have offered them directly, and found himself, distantly, grateful for a fear he would otherwise have spent considerable effort cultivating himself.

---

He sat alone afterward in his private chamber, Lament resting across his knees the way it had rested there every night since the theft that had cost Aamon his life to secure it. He hadn't thought of Aamon directly in some time, and the shape of the marshal's face came back to him now with a clarity he hadn't expected, a man who had followed every order without ever once asking what it would cost him personally. He thought of him now — the efficiency of that sacrifice, how certain he'd been, that night, that the door it opened was worth the price. He wondered, for the first time, whether Aamon had understood what he was being spent for, or whether that much, at least, had been kept from him too.

The spear's grief pulsed against him in its usual slow rhythm, a familiar ache settled somewhere behind his sternum that no number of years had ever quite dulled. He had long ago learned to live with it. He had never once learned to read it, and sitting here now, alone with tonight's failure, he found himself asking it a question for the first time since the day he'd claimed it.

*Did you know something like this was possible. Did you know, and simply never find a way to warn me, or never want to. You've never once given me anything freely, not in all the years I've carried you. Why would tonight have been different.*

The spear offered him nothing beyond its ordinary ache, the same grief it always offered regardless of what he asked it. Belial sat with the silence a long while, one hand resting against the weapon's cold length, tracing the same worn groove along its haft his fingers had traced a thousand ordinary nights before this one. No answer came tonight either. For the first time in longer than he could measure, the not-knowing troubled him more than the losses that had prompted it.

He had built his reign on the belief that he understood every piece on the board. That belief was gone now, along with his Enforcer, and Belial understood, sitting alone with a weapon that had never once trusted him with its own secrets, that the first would take considerably longer to replace than the second. He set the spear aside eventually, laying it across the arm of his chair with the same careful precision he brought to everything he touched. Its grief followed him regardless, a faint residue that stayed with him even now, at a distance from the weapon itself. Every commander below him needed his certainty to stay steady themselves, and he would give it to them again tomorrow, whatever it actually cost him tonight to find it.

---

Belial's formal report reached Dis three days later, carried through the same private channel of correspondence he had always used to keep his sovereign informed of anything too large to wait for the ordinary rhythm of scheduled reports. Lucifer read it alone, as he read every report that mattered enough to arrive sealed rather than spoken, in the unhurried, exhaustive way he read anything he suspected he would need to remember precisely.

The decree itself was plain enough on its surface — Belial abandoning the soul-harvest, folding a season's investment back into Hell's own borders, a defensive posture from a commander who had not held one in longer than Lucifer cared to measure. He had expected, reading that much, the particular flavor of alarm any sovereign felt when a capable subordinate's confidence finally cracked. What held him past that first plain reading was the detail buried three paragraphs in, the one the report's own author had plainly struggled to phrase in any of a soldier's ordinary vocabulary: not slain, not captured, not broken — an entire squad and its officer denied, a scrying pool refusing three times to show the moment it happened, until there was, in the report's own careful wording, nothing left to recover and nothing left to mourn.

"Shall I have a formal acknowledgment drafted, my lord?" the aide asked, waiting at the edge of the private chamber with the patience of someone who had learned exactly how long Lucifer's silences tended to run before they wanted company again. "Lord Belial's people will expect some reply, given the scale of what he's reporting."

"Not yet." Lucifer did not look up from the page. "Let him fortify first. He'll want my confidence a great deal more once he's finished than he'd appreciate my questions right now."

The aide bowed and withdrew, and Lucifer read the paragraph a second time, and then a third, in the particular silence he reserved for the very small number of things in his domain that did not resolve, on repeated reading, into something he already understood. Belial's own council, by every account folded into the report, had read the incident as fear — a threat with no shape and no name, the kind that unsettled soldiers precisely because it refused to announce itself as either weapon or enemy. Lucifer read it and felt something else move through him entirely, faint and unplaceable, the way a man might catch, in a crowded and unfamiliar street, a single word spoken in a language he had not heard in longer than he could measure, and could not, for the length of several breaths, remember why it should have meant anything to him at all.

He asked no one what it meant. There was no one in the chamber to ask, and nothing in the report itself that offered an answer to a question he had not managed to properly form. He set it down once, meaning to move on to the next matter waiting in the evening's stack of correspondence. He picked it back up within the minute, without quite deciding to, and read the same three paragraphs again.

He kept the report a good while longer than protocol required of him that night, long after every other matter on the table had been read, weighed, and set properly aside.
