# B2C36.8 – A Truth Told First

Days before the pages he would eventually seal with his own hand ever reached Lucifer's desk, Agares received him in the smaller of his two chambers, the one reserved for business he had not yet decided how to categorize, and Vual read that choice the way he read most things Agares did without explaining them — as information offered sideways, never handed over directly. "You've caught something," Vual said, taking the seat he was not quite invited to take. "You never use this room for anything you've already made up your mind about."

"I've caught the shape of something, which is considerably more interesting than catching the thing itself." Agares turned a scrap of parchment over in his fingers without reading it, the bifid edge of his voice splitting the sentence into two overlapping tones that never quite agreed with each other. "Rumor moves through this court the way water moves through cracked stone, Vual — always along the path already there, never the path anyone chose for it. For the better part of a week, every crack has been carrying the same water. A text. Old. Contested. Something about three shapes returning that were never supposed to be counted as three at all." He set the parchment down. "Heaven has it too. Heaven has had it longer than we have, in fact, and Heaven is doing precisely what Heaven always does with a truth this size — convening, deliberating, forming a committee to decide how carefully the thing ought to be handled before anyone is permitted to hear it."

"And you'd rather someone heard it first."

"I'd rather someone heard *a* version of it first." Agares's smile widened, unhurried — Vual read in it the particular pleasure of a man watching his own reasoning arrive exactly where he'd steered it. "There is a considerable difference, Vual, between a lie and a truth arranged to travel faster than the truth it was arranged from. I have never once found the first worth my trouble. The second, told well enough and early enough, does more lasting work than a hundred outright fabrications — because no one ever thinks to argue with a thing they believe they discovered for themselves." He tapped the parchment once. "I want this in the mortal world before Gabriel's people have finished agreeing on a font to print the real one in. Not shouted. Planted. Somewhere it will grow roots before anyone thinks to ask who watered it."

"You could send it through any of a dozen channels that already answer to you."

"I could. I'm choosing not to." Agares studied him with what Vual read as frank, appraising patience — a man selecting a tool for a job he'd already solved in his own head. "Fear-channels are fast and they are traceable, and I have no interest in a distortion the enemy can pull up by the root the moment they notice the soil disturbed. I want something that spreads the way *belief* spreads. You've built an entire discipline out of understanding exactly how that happens, one patient soul at a time, for longer than I've bothered to count. Use the same patience on a rumor that you use on a man's certainty about his own life, and it will outlive anything I could simply order shouted into the dark." He slid the parchment across the table, still unread by Vual, its seal still whole. "Get it moving. I don't care whose mouth it starts in, so long as it isn't recognizably mine."

---

Vual carried the parchment back to his own quarters and broke the seal alone, the way he preferred to unwrap any prize before deciding how best to make use of it. What was written inside was rougher, Vual judged, than anything Agares would ever let leave Dis with his own name attached to it — three shapes, old grievances, a phrase or two not yet worth arguing over — and Vual read it once, closely, then set it aside without troubling to memorize the exact wording. The polish, if the thing ever needed polish, was Agares's business to add later. His own part was simpler: the shape of it, planted in the right ear, long before anyone thought to ask after the precise words.

Nymos arrived at the second summons already amused, and when Vual looked up he read something in the too-composed set of the man's features — an expression that struck him as arrived at rather than simply worn. "You only send for me when you want a face that isn't mine," Nymos said, dropping into the chair across from him with a loose, unbothered confidence Vual had never once seen him need to work for. "Whose truth am I wearing tonight?"

"Not a face yet. A second opinion." Vual set the parchment between them, its seal already broken. "Agares wants a piece of the Codex loose in the mortal world, dressed as something other than what it is, and moving faster than anything Heaven's willing to put its own name to. I intend to plant it myself, the way I plant anything I actually want to take root — slowly, in one particular ear, until it starts to feel like the ear's own idea." He watched Nymos's constantly shifting features settle, briefly, into something like genuine interest. "The trouble with a single planting is that a clever mind eventually asks where the seed came from. I want a second source. Independent, or near enough to pass for it. Something that confirms what I've already said without appearing to have spoken to me at all."

"You want me to be someone she already trusts."

"I want you to be whoever she already wants to trust, which is a much larger category, and in my experience, the easier one to fill." Vual allowed himself a small, private satisfaction at Nymos's answering smile, the particular pleasure of watching a plan land on ground already prepared to receive it. "You've never told anyone the whole truth in your existence, Nymos. I'm not asking you to start. I'm only asking you to let someone see, in your borrowed face, exactly the confirmation she was already hoping to find."

"That's not a difficult assignment. That's simply Tuesday." Nymos rose, unhurried, already half a step toward whatever shape he intended to wear by morning. "Where am I confirming it?"

"The Undertow. Give me two nights first. I want the ground broken before you plant anything on top of it."

---

The Undertow ran beneath a river city old enough that no one currently trading in its cellars could have named which war had first driven commerce underground, three levels of reclaimed foundation stitched together by ladders and borrowed doorways, lit by enough candlelight to read a page and not quite enough to read a face clearly across the room. Vual had used markets like it in a dozen mortal centuries, and he had long since stopped finding the pattern remarkable — wherever mortals gathered to trade in secrets, they built the same low ceilings and the same watchful quiet, as though privacy were a physical material that only accumulated in the dark.

He found Selke at her usual corner table, a woman whose reputation in three cities rested entirely on a single, carefully cultivated fact: whatever she sold, she had always heard first. It was not wealth she traded in, not directly. It was the narrower, hungrier currency of being early — the particular status of the broker whose name attached itself to a rumor before anyone else's did, so that the rumor, however it eventually resolved, would always be remembered as hers. Vual understood that hunger the way he understood most hungers, as a door left slightly open, and he had learned, across centuries of patient work, that a door like that never needed to be forced. It only needed the right thing offered on the other side of it.

"You've the look of a man carrying something he hasn't decided whether to sell," Selke said, not looking up from the ledger in front of her, though something in the set of her shoulders told Vual she had clocked him the moment he'd come down the last ladder. "I don't buy things men haven't decided about."

"I'm not selling anything." Vual took the seat across from her without asking, the same unhurried claim he made on any space he intended to occupy for a while. "I'm giving you first look at something I heard from a source I can't name, which I understand is the only kind of source anyone in this room actually trusts." He let the silence that followed do a portion of the work for him, watching her ledger stay open but unattended. "Three shapes, old as the world's own memory, coming back together after longer apart than any prophecy bothered to count. Nobody official is saying it yet. That's rather the point of telling you before they do."

Her pen stopped moving entirely. "Coming back together as what. A blessing or a warning."

"That," Vual said, with the particular warmth he reserved for the exact center of a lie built from nothing but true pieces, "is the part nobody agrees on yet, which I'd think makes it worth a good deal more than a story everyone's already settled the ending of. I'm not telling you what to believe, Selke. I'm telling you what the shape of the thing is, and letting you decide for yourself which way it leans." It cost him nothing to say, and every word of it was, strictly, true — the arranging was the only lie in it, and the arranging left no seam for her to catch a blade under.

---

Nymos returned to Vual's borrowed rooms above the Undertow two nights later, wearing, for the moment, a face Vual didn't recognize and suspected wasn't meant to be recognized by anyone but its intended audience. "She confirmed it herself before I'd finished my second sentence," he said, dropping into the chair with what struck Vual as the satisfied economy of a man reporting a job that had required less effort than promised. "I didn't need to convince her of anything. I only needed to look, for about four minutes, exactly like whoever she'd already decided a second source ought to look like. Volarien does the rest. It always does." He said it lightly, the way he said most things, and Vual noted — filed rather than remarked upon, the way he filed most small things worth remembering — a flicker beneath the lightness that hadn't quite finished settling into a shape. Nymos glanced once at his own reflection in the dark window glass, and looked away from it faster than the gesture strictly required. Whatever passed behind his shifting face in that half-second, he offered nothing further to explain it, and Vual, whose own long trade depended on knowing precisely which doors were worth knocking on and which were better left shut, chose not to ask.

"She believes she's heard it independently confirmed."

"She believes considerably more than that. She believes she's clever for having heard it twice from two men who never met each other." Nymos's smile came back easily, whatever had briefly interrupted it filed away, Vual guessed, wherever he kept things he preferred not examined. "You'd be amazed how rarely anyone stops to wonder why the confirmation arrived exactly when they were hoping for one."

"I wouldn't," Vual said. "I've built a very long career on precisely that failure of imagination."

---

By the fourth night, the shape of it had left Selke's hands entirely, exactly as Vual had known it would the moment two independent mouths had spoken the same careful arrangement of true things into a market built to carry whispers faster than any authorized courier could follow them. It moved the way water moved through cracked stone, as Agares had predicted it would, along paths already worn smooth by centuries of the Undertow's own hunger for being first — a broker in one quarter selling it as prophecy to a congregation eager for reassurance, another selling the identical facts, rearranged half a shade darker, as a warning to a circle that traded in fear instead. Neither buyer, Vual was fairly sure, knew they were purchasing the same seed, grown to fit two different appetites. Vual doubted the two would ever compare notes long enough to notice.

He thought, watching from the Undertow's upper gallery as a courier he had never spoken to carried the rumor toward the docks and whatever ship would next spread it along some coast Vual had not troubled to name, of the councils in Heaven that had not yet finished agreeing on a single sentence of the truth this whole market was already trading on credit. He imagined it easily enough: a committee, somewhere above the reach of anything he could see from this cellar, weighing caution against candor, drafting language careful enough to survive its own scrutiny. Every hour they spent deliberating was an hour Agares's arrangement had already spent growing roots no later honesty would dig out cleanly. It was not victory exactly, watching that gap widen. It was simply arithmetic, the kind Vual had trusted longer than he had trusted almost anything else — the first telling always outlasted the true one, not because it was stronger, but because by the time the true one arrived, there was no longer an empty space left for it to fill.

---

He reported to Agares before the week was finished, and found his commander receiving the account the way he received most successes — with a stillness Vual had learned to read as carrying more satisfaction in it than any open pleasure could have managed.

"Two sources, unconnected in appearance, converging on the same arrangement of true things," Agares said, turning the report over once behind eyes that missed nothing worth keeping. "That's considerably more elegant than I expected from a first attempt. I'll admit I wondered whether Nymos's particular talent might prove too blunt an instrument for work this delicate."

"He wasn't blunt. He was exactly as sharp as the moment required, and no sharper." Vual said it without particular warmth, a plain accounting rather than a defense. "The market's carrying it forward on its own now. It no longer needs either of us standing behind it."

"Then it's done precisely what I asked of it." Agares's smile settled into something almost restful — Vual read in it the particular ease of a man who had built a mechanism and watched it run exactly to specification. "The word spoken first outlives the word spoken true, Vual. Every century I've spent proving that sentence to myself has never once made it any less satisfying to watch prove itself again." He set the report aside, unfinished business already folding itself into the evening's larger ledger. "Good work. I won't forget whose patience actually built it."

Vual inclined his head and let himself out without further comment, the compliment received the way he received most things offered to him by someone he served rather than someone he'd chosen — filed, set aside for whatever it might be worth later. Walking back through Dis, he found the ease of the whole affair sitting oddly against his ribs, a discomfort he could not immediately place and did not particularly enjoy finding in himself. He had spent centuries studying that same mortal hunger in a single soul at a time, patient, deliberate, savoring each conquest as its own private craft, worth exactly as much as the care he had put into it. He had never once needed to watch an entire market do the wanting for him, faster and more willingly than any soul he had ever courted alone — and some part of him, the part that had always taken quiet pride in the slowness of his work, had not yet decided whether what he'd just done tonight should be called a success, or simply the first time he'd let something else do the seducing in his name.
