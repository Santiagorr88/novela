# B1L01 — The First Threshold

*(Lament Arc, Chapter 1 of 6 — POV Belial)*

The portal did not open so much as it wept.

Belial had expected a door. A seam in the world, a wound he could widen with will and Ruach and the particular patience he reserved for things that resisted him. What he found instead was a slow bleeding-through, reality thinning at one point until the black crystal beyond simply became visible, the way a bruise becomes visible before it becomes pain. There was no threshold to cross. There was only a moment when he was in Hell, and then a moment, immeasurably soon after, when he was not.

The ground here was grief made solid. Jagged shards of black crystal, each one keening faintly when his boot came down, a chorus of small chimes with no melody in them, only the flat, patient tone of something that had been crying long enough to stop expecting comfort. The air tasted of salt and old regret, thick enough that he felt it settle in his chest like sediment. With every breath he took in phantom sorrow that was not his own — the grief of a star that had died alone, the loneliness of something that had outlived every creature that once knew its name. It came in waves, indifferent to him, the way weather is indifferent to the man walking through it.

He had been warned. Lucifer's warning, delivered in that infuriating half-attentive tone he used for things he considered beneath his notice — *some notes are best left unsung* — had followed Belial through the Tower of the Eternals, through the sacrifice of a commander who had trusted him, through a portal that had cost a legion's worth of blood to open. He had carried that warning the whole way, the way a man carries an insult, turning it over until its edges softened into something almost like fuel.

He did not intend to sing carefully. He intended to take what he had come for and leave this weeping place behind him, and if the Sepulcher wanted to mourn about it, it could mourn the way everything mourned him eventually — from a distance, powerlessly, after the fact.

Ruach hung heavy and familiar against his hip, its weight a small, constant reassurance. It had never failed him. It would not fail him here. He had killed gods with less.

He walked.

The crystal plain stretched in every direction under a sky the color of a healing wound, grey shot through with something that might once have been violet. There was no horizon, only a place where the grief-light dimmed enough that his eyes gave up trying to resolve it. He walked toward nothing in particular, trusting — as he always trusted — that anything worth finding would eventually make itself known to a man who refused to stop looking.

It found him sooner than he expected.

It arrived not as a sound but as a *pull*, low and insistent, a sorrow so dense it had a gravity of its own. Belial felt it catch behind his sternum and drag, the exact sensation of a hook finding purchase in something that did not want to be caught. He had felt fear before — rarely, and never for long, and never in a way he would have admitted to anyone with a voice to repeat it — but this was not fear. This was recognition. Something in him, some old and buried part that predated his pride, knew this pull the way a starving man knows the smell of bread three streets away.

*There.* Not a direction so much as a certainty. He turned toward it and it was already stronger, already singing through the crystal underfoot, and he thought, with the particular satisfaction of a man watching his own cleverness confirmed: *this is it. This is the tomb.*

He had expected resistance measured in centuries — locks, wards, the particular cruelty of a place built to keep out exactly his kind of ambition. He had not expected it to be *easy*. But the pull grew with every stride, and the keening of the crystal rose with it, and somewhere beneath the salt-thick air he thought he could smell something new — iron, and beneath the iron, the ozone-sharp memory of a battlefield centuries cold. It felt like arrival. It felt, absurdly, like being expected.

He should have known that a place built entirely from grief would understand, better than anyone, the particular vanity of a man convinced he was owed an easy victory.

The pull resolved into a shape at the crest of a low rise of black glass — not a tomb, not a door, not anything Belial had a name ready for. A figure, vaguely woman-shaped in the way a pillar of smoke is vaguely a person, built from the same weeping crystal as the ground beneath her, her surface catching the grief-light and scattering it into a thousand small, sorrowful glints. She did not have a face so much as a suggestion of one, a smoothness where a face should be that was somehow more unsettling than any expression could have managed. She was not large. She did not need to be.

She was not, he understood a half-second too late, hostile. Not by nature. She simply *was* — a shape sorrow took when sorrow needed a shape, standing where she had always stood, asking nothing of anyone who did not try to take what did not belong to them.

Belial had never in his existence been the kind of man who heard a distinction like that and slowed down.

"I am not here for you," he said, which was true, and which he said anyway with the particular contempt he reserved for things he did not expect to need to negotiate with. He stepped past her, toward the pull, toward the smell of iron and cold ozone that he was now certain — *certain*, the specific certainty of a proud man who has already begun celebrating — marked the tomb itself.

The voice, when it came, did not come from the smooth suggestion of a face. It came from the whole plain at once, a thousand small crystal chimes finding, for one instant, a single shared cadence.

*"Nothing is taken here. Only given."*

Belial did not break stride. "Then give it to me."

The crystal beneath his second step did not chime. It screamed.

He would think about that sound for a long time afterward, though he would never admit as much aloud: it had not been pain in the sound, not exactly. It had been *insult*. The particular high, keening note of something ancient and patient being handled without its consent, the same note Belial himself might have made if someone had reached into his chest and closed a fist around something he had spent an age agreeing not to feel.

The ground did not open. The figure did not strike him, not the way Ruach would have struck, not with force answering force. She simply *grieved at him*, the whole weight of her nature turned in his direction at once, and it hit him with none of the theater of a blow and all of its consequence — every phantom sorrow the plain had been feeding him in careful, bearable doses arrived together, undiluted, a star's death and a species' extinction and ten thousand years of a single unbearable loneliness, all of it his, all at once, for exactly as long as it took his knees to buckle.

He did not remember deciding to draw Ruach. He remembered its weight already in his hand, remembered swinging it through a shape that had no body to strike, remembered the mace passing through crystalline grief the way a blade passes through smoke — meeting nothing, achieving nothing, the doubt-laced impact he was so famous for landing on absolutely no one at all.

The retaliation was not violence. It was worse. It was *understanding*, forced into him whether he wanted it or not — a single instant where he knew, with total and humiliating clarity, exactly how small his ambition looked from the perspective of something that had been mourning longer than his entire species had existed. Then the grief-light guttered, and the pull he had followed here — the smell of iron, the ozone-sharp promise of the tomb — receded, folding back into the general ache of the plain, gone the way a held breath goes when a man finally, involuntarily, exhales.

*"Nothing is taken here,"* the plain said again, quieter now, without triumph and without mercy — the same sentence, offered a second time to a man who had not needed to hear it twice to understand it, only refused to. It was the first time in the entire encounter that Belial had no answer ready.

He came back to himself on his knees. His knees. The last time he had knelt for anything had been a lie he told a commander he was about to sacrifice.

Golden eyes narrowed, he pushed himself upright, one hand pressed flat against black glass that still keened faintly under his palm, resentful and low. His armor was unmarked. Nothing in this place had touched his body at all. That, somehow, was worse than a wound would have been — a wound he could have answered. This place had not fought him. It had simply *shown* him something, the way you might show a child a mirror, and let his own reflection do the rest of the work.

Fury arrived late, the way it always did with him, after the shock had cleared enough ground for it to stand on. He wanted to strike something. There was nothing here built to be struck. The figure was gone, or had never fully been *there* in any sense his rage could get purchase on, and the plain stretched out around him exactly as indifferent as it had been before he arrived, its grief entirely unbothered by his humiliation, one more sorrow among the uncountable many it had already absorbed.

He stood. It cost him more than he would ever say.

*This is not a door to be broken,* he thought, and the thought arrived in a voice that was, infuriatingly, not entirely his own — a low echo of the warning he had carried the whole way here, softened now into something closer to fact than insult. Lucifer had not told him the Sepulcher would refuse him. Lucifer had told him some notes were best left unsung, and Belial understood now, with the particular clarity that follows being humbled in a place too honest to lie about it, that this had never been a warning about danger at all.

It had been a warning about *method*.

He looked out across the weeping plain — no visible path, no marked way forward, only crystal and grief and the distant, patient ache of something that had all the time that had ever existed and no interest whatsoever in his. He had come here certain that pride and force were the only two tools an existence like his required. Both had just been handed back to him, unused, by something that had not even bothered to consider them a threat.

Belial did not retreat. Retreat was not a shape his pride knew how to take. But he stood a long moment at the edge of the rise where he had fallen, tasting salt and iron and the bitter, unfamiliar flavor of a lesson he had not asked for, and understood — the way a man understands a debt he did not agree to — that the Weeping Sepulcher was not going to hand him anything.

He would have to learn its grief before it would ever let him touch its truth.

He did not know yet what that meant. He knew only that he would have to come back, and that the coming back would cost him something he had never before been asked to spend.
