# B3C43 – The Parting of Ways

Michael found the fulcrum exactly where he had left it, and found himself, standing at its edge again after so long away, considerably less certain of his own footing than he had expected to feel.

The Nexus had not changed. It still hung around him in every direction at once, an endless drift of half-formed possibility that never quite resolved into anything his eyes could hold steady for more than a moment, and at its center still sat that same small, unremarkable stillness Azael had once called the point of perfect potential. Michael had spent these last months on Earth mostly, walking quiet roads under an ordinary sun, learning slowly what it actually meant to live inside a world he'd once only ever been sent to protect. He had felt the pull back here the moment it arrived, sudden and unmistakable, and had known without needing to ask what it meant. The three of them had unfinished business at this exact stillness, whether any of them had planned on returning to settle it or not.

He had not planned on coming back alone. He had assumed, walking toward the pull that had brought him here, that he would find the fulcrum empty a while longer yet, and had spent the last stretch of the journey rehearsing what he might actually do with the silence if it turned out to be waiting for him instead. He needn't have bothered with the rehearsal.

He saw Ereloth first, a familiar shape resolving out of the drifting light near the fulcrum's edge, and felt something in his own chest ease slightly at the sight of him, an old, uncomplicated relief he hadn't realized he'd been missing until it actually arrived. Ereloth's easy grin found its way onto his face readily enough, the same grin Michael remembered from every quieter moment they'd shared before the forging, but it settled differently now, resting atop something steadier than it once had. Michael studied him a moment, searching for whatever had actually changed, and found it eventually in the man's stillness rather than his expression — Ereloth stood with both feet planted, unhurried, a man who had apparently stopped needing motion to prove he was still capable of joy. Michael thought, watching him, that the shift looked less like the loss of something than the addition of a weight carried carefully enough not to show.

Thaeriel arrived a moment later, stepping out of the drift with none of Ereloth's easy unhurriedness, a man who still crossed distance like it owed him something even now, months removed from any battlefield that might have actually demanded the pace of him. Here the change struck Michael immediately, before he'd even had time to properly brace for it. The old fire was still there, banked low behind Thaeriel's eyes the way it always had been, but it no longer burned the way Michael remembered from the war, righteous and absolute and entirely certain of its own verdict. It burned quieter now, tempered by something Michael recognized, watching the careful, deliberate way Thaeriel crossed the last stretch of drifting light toward them, as a kind of mercy he hadn't previously associated with the man at all. Thaeriel inclined his head once in greeting, a small, grave courtesy that carried more warmth in it than any of his old battlefield nods ever had, and Michael understood, reading the gesture, that whatever grief Thaeriel had learned to carry these last months had not hardened him further. It had simply taught him where to set the fire down.

"You both felt it too, then," Michael said, once the three of them had settled into roughly the same positions they'd once occupied around this exact stillness, the old habit of the forging circle apparently too deep now to abandon even without a working left to perform.

"Hard to miss," Ereloth said, glancing once toward the fulcrum's quiet center before letting his gaze settle back on the two of them. "Some pulls you don't argue with."

Michael studied them both a long moment in the comfortable quiet that followed, searching each face for the particular shadow he'd learned, across these last strange months, to recognize in his own reflection whenever he let himself look too long. He found it in both of them readily enough once he knew what he was looking for — a certain stillness behind Ereloth's easy humor that hadn't been there before the fulcrum, a certain gravity behind Thaeriel's tempered patience that no amount of quiet counsel work had managed to fully soften. He understood, recognizing it, that the fragment each of them had accepted here hadn't simply vanished once its work was done. It had settled instead, the way any true weight eventually settles, into a permanent part of what each of them now was.

"I can feel it," Michael said, one hand rising without quite meaning to and settling briefly against his own chest. "The doubt. It never goes away."

"Nor the sorrow," Thaeriel answered, and his voice, when it came, carried a gentleness Michael had genuinely never heard from him before, not once across every year they'd fought side by side. "I've made something of a practice of it, these last months. Sitting with people carrying their own versions of the same weight. It doesn't lighten. You just learn to carry it further before it starts to show."

"Nor the weight," Ereloth added, and for once, saying it, his familiar grin didn't quite make its usual appearance. He looked, in the moment it stayed absent, considerably older than Michael had ever actually seen him look, older in the particular way of someone who had finally stopped performing lightness long enough to admit how much of it had always been effort. "I used to think the song did all the carrying for me. Turns out it just made the load easier to disguise."

Michael let the admission settle a moment, unhurried, the fulcrum's own patient stillness seeming to make room for it without demanding either of them fill the silence any faster than they needed to. He thought of every version of himself that had once believed a sword, wielded well enough, could settle any question a soul might actually need answering. That version of him felt very far away now, standing here between two men who had each, in their own particular fashion, finally stopped believing the same simple thing about their own gifts.

"He's still here, you know," Michael said eventually, nodding once toward the fulcrum's quiet center. "I can feel that too. Just not the way we used to."

As if the words themselves had been enough to call it forward, a voice moved through the stillness around them, faint and unhurried, carrying no particular direction Michael's ears could actually locate. It did not sound like speech exactly, not the way Azael's voice had once carried across a council table or a battlefield. It arrived instead the way warmth arrives in a room already lit, present everywhere at once rather than coming from any single point, and Michael understood immediately, without needing to reason his way to it, exactly whose voice this actually was.

I am not gone, Azael said, the words settling into all three of them at once, too diffuse to belong to any single direction and too familiar to belong to anyone else. I have simply become large enough that gone no longer means what it used to. You have done well, all three of you. Walk your own roads now. I will be watching, in whatever way this shape still lets me watch.

Michael closed his eyes a moment, letting the sensation move through him fully rather than trying to parse it into anything more precise than what it already was. He glanced sideways once he'd opened them again and found Ereloth doing the same, one hand pressed briefly against his own sternum in a gesture that mirrored Michael's own from a moment earlier, and found Thaeriel simply standing very still, head bowed slightly, in the exact posture of a man receiving something he had no intention of asking to keep any longer than it chose to stay.

"He's saying goodbye," Thaeriel said quietly, once the sensation had settled back into the fulcrum's ordinary stillness. "Properly, this time. Not the way we said it at the end of the forging, when none of us had words left worth using."

"He's saying more than that," Ereloth said, and something in his tone made Michael look at him more closely. "He's telling us it's finished. Whatever this was — the four of us, working together, keeping watch over the same exact stretch of the balance. That part's done now. Has been since the fulcrum, if I'm honest. We just hadn't actually said it aloud yet."

Michael weighed the thought slowly against everything he'd felt these last months on Earth, the particular ache of walking unfamiliar roads without any of the three men who had once stood beside him through the worst of the actual fighting. He understood, weighing it, that Ereloth was right about the shape of it even if the admission cost something to hear plainly. Whatever they had built together at this fulcrum had never been meant to continue as a standing arrangement, four men keeping permanent watch over a balance that needed, by its own delicate nature, considerably less watching than any of them might have once assumed. The balance had been forged to hold itself. Their continued presence here, circling it together the way they once had, risked disturbing the very thing they'd worked so hard to set right.

"Separate paths, then," Michael said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Separate paths," Thaeriel agreed. "Whatever each of us finds worth doing with what's left. I imagine we'll cross each other's roads again eventually. Just not like this. Not as a standing council."

Nobody spoke again after that. Thaeriel reached out first, wordless, and gripped Michael's forearm the plain, soldier's way he'd have once used to seal a battlefield oath, and Michael answered the grip with his own, holding it a moment longer than the gesture strictly required. Ereloth's hand closed over both of theirs a heartbeat later, no ceremony in it at all, just three hands briefly joined at the exact center where a working had once passed through all four of them together. None of them said what the gesture meant. None of them needed to.

Ereloth was the first to let go and step back, in the end, once the quiet had stretched long enough that further words would have only diminished whatever the silence had already managed to say better than any of them could. He offered a small, rueful salute, two fingers rising briefly to his brow in a gesture that carried none of his old theatrical flourish, only a plain, honest sadness Michael suspected he'd have hidden behind a joke a year ago. He held Michael's gaze a moment longer, something warm and grateful passing through his expression before he finally turned, and walked without hurry toward wherever the drifting light had already decided to carry him.

Thaeriel lingered only a moment longer. He inclined his head once, the same grave courtesy he'd offered on arriving, and Michael understood the gesture well enough now to read everything it wasn't saying aloud — gratitude, and grief, and something that might, in a man less careful with his own fire, have looked almost like peace. He said nothing further. He simply closed his eyes, and let the pale light already gathering around him carry him away in a single unhurried flicker, gone before Michael had quite finished drawing the breath he'd meant to answer him with.

And then Michael stood alone at the fulcrum, the last of the four still standing exactly where the forging had once asked all of them to stand together.

He looked out at the Nexus a long moment, at the countless half-formed paths still drifting in every direction he could see, more possibilities than any single existence could ever hope to walk in full. He had come from one of those paths already, had learned, walking it these last months, more about what an ordinary life actually cost and offered than centuries of service had ever managed to teach him. He found, studying the drift now with something closer to plain recognition than uncertainty, that he already knew which path was actually his to walk next.

He closed his eyes briefly, let the fulcrum's old stillness settle once more through his chest, and turned. A single door stood waiting a short distance off, plain and unremarkable against the drifting light around it, marked with nothing more than a single word he'd have recognized blind. Earth.

He walked toward it without hurry, carrying no grand speech and needing none, only the quiet, settled certainty of a man who had finally finished the work three others had once needed him for, and had found, in its place, a considerably smaller and considerably more honest calling waiting on the other side of an ordinary door.
