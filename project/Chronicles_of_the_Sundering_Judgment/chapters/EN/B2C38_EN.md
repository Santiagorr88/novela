# B2C38 – The Vow of the Just

Arin stood at the edge of a cliff he'd walked three days to reach, wind coming hard off the water below. He was finally ready to consider the figure's words properly. *It makes you what you always were, before forgetting decided you were something else.* He had heard that sentence in the corridor beneath Navarion. He had spent three days deciding whether to believe it.

He'd chosen this stretch of coast without fully deciding to, some old instinct pulling him toward open water rather than toward the faster, more crowded roads south. The path here had taken him through two towns and a stretch of empty farmland, past fields left fallow for the season, past a gas station where he'd bought water and nothing else. The first town had a diner with its lights still on at two in the morning, and he'd sat at the counter for twenty minutes without ordering anything, just to be somewhere warm for a while. The second town he'd walked straight through without stopping, its streets empty at that hour except for a dog that watched him pass from behind a chain-link fence. Three days alone had given him more time with his own thoughts than he'd had since before the Grey City. He hadn't eaten properly across most of that walk. He hadn't especially noticed.

He thought of the cold place in his chest that had never once warmed, not across a decade of contracts, not across a month of learning what mercy actually cost him. He had noticed it first as a teenager, long before any of this started, a strange calm that settled over him whenever other people around him panicked. He'd assumed, for years, that everyone had something similar and simply hid it better than he did. He remembered the first job that had made him notice it properly, years ago now: a client crying real tears over an unpaid debt, and Arin feeling nothing behind his own ribs but the flat certainty that the debt was owed regardless of the tears. For that entire decade he had treated the coldness as a flaw, something broken in him before memory started, something to work around.

He understood now that the coldness was not a defect. It was simply what he was. Judgment did not feel warmth toward the person it judged. It looked at the facts and stated what they meant, regardless of what the one stating them happened to feel while he did it. He sat with that understanding a long while, watching the water below the cliff move without any particular hurry. Naming it plainly, in exactly those terms, was easier than he had expected after three days of avoiding the same conclusion.

---

He made the call before he let himself walk any further south, sitting on a low rock with his back to the wind. He'd kept the same number for this particular contact across the entire decade, a small, deliberate habit that had served him well more than once. The broker picked up on the third ring, his voice carrying the same flat wariness Arin remembered from every job they'd ever done together.

"Didn't expect to hear from you again," the broker said. "Last I heard, you'd gone quiet enough that I figured you'd finally gotten yourself killed properly."

"Not yet." Arin waited a moment before he said the rest. "I'm calling to tell you I'm out. For good this time, not the usual disappearing act. Don't hold anything for me. Don't pass my name along if it comes up."

A pause stretched on the other end, long enough that Arin almost thought the connection had dropped. "You're serious."

"I am." He'd expected the broker to argue, or at least to push for a better explanation than the one Arin was willing to give him. Neither came. In a decade of contracts negotiated through this exact voice, Arin couldn't remember a single instance of the man accepting anything at face value without at least one follow-up question.

"You alright?" The question came out rougher than the broker's usual careful neutrality, a note in his voice Arin had never once heard from him in all their years of working together. Arin could hear, faintly, some radio or television playing in the background on the broker's end, the kind of ordinary noise that told him the man was standing in his own kitchen or living room, somewhere he'd never once pictured him in a decade of purely transactional calls.

"I'm going to reclaim something that's mine," Arin said. "After that, I don't know what I'll be." Saying it aloud cost him less than he'd expected. "Take care of yourself."

He ended the call before the broker could ask anything further. He thought of every job the broker had arranged for him over the years — every payment collected, every target confirmed and eliminated. He had once believed that doing the work cleanly was the same thing as doing it honestly. It wasn't. He tried, briefly, to count the jobs, the way he had years ago before he'd stopped bothering. He gave up somewhere past sixty, the same place he always gave up, the true number lost somewhere further back than that. None of it called to him now. He put the phone away for good.

---

He stood again once the wind had eased, and said aloud, for the first time, the word that had been surfacing unbidden in his mind for days now.

"Thaeriel."

No name had ever felt correct in his mouth before, not even his own. This one did. He said it again, quieter the second time, listening to the exact shape the syllables made against the wind. A third time after that, louder, out toward the water, until a passing gull startled off a nearby rock at the sound of it. It felt less like trying on a name and more like finally using the right one, after years of answering to the wrong one.

Arin Cross had been a useful name, a name a man could hand a client without flinching, a name that had opened doors and closed contracts across a decade of careful work. It had never once felt like this. He thought of every version of himself that name had covered — the eager young man just starting out, quick to take any job that paid; the tired veteran who'd stopped counting contracts once the number stopped meaning anything and started measuring years instead. He remembered the exact office where he'd first signed that name on a contract, a cramped room above a pawn shop with a landlord who never asked questions, and how proud he'd been of the signature at the time, how deliberately he'd practiced it beforehand so it would look like something he'd always had. None of those versions had ever been the whole of him. He'd known that much for a while now, even before he'd had a better name to explain it with.

---

He raised his empty hand without quite deciding to. Something answered — a faint, cold light gathering along his palm, resolving slowly into the shape of a weapon he recognized without ever having held it. A spear, long and elegant, its edge catching light that didn't exist in the gray afternoon around him. The shaft was longer than he'd have guessed, nearly his own height, tapering to a point that looked capable of far more precision than brute force would have required. The haft looked worn smooth in places his own hand had never touched.

He couldn't close his fingers around it. It wasn't actually here. It was an image only, cast across whatever distance separated him from the real weapon, still held, he suspected, in Hell, by hands that had no right to it. He tested the image a moment longer, moving his hand slowly through the space where the light hung, and found no resistance at all, nothing solid enough to answer the movement. He tried a second time anyway, more slowly, watching his own fingers pass through the light without disturbing it in the slightest, and only then let the effort go.

He had expected fury from a weapon meant to punish. This was not fury. It was judgment in its purest, coldest form — precise, not cruel, painful only because the truth it delivered was sometimes painful to hear. He studied the image a long moment, comparing it against every weapon he had actually held across a decade of contracts, and found nothing in his memory that matched it. The rage that had driven him through those years had never come from this weapon. That rage had been his own addition, a mortal man's rough substitute for something that never needed anger to do its work.

---

He thought of Belial, holding that light, and did not feel the hatred he'd expected. What he felt instead was closer to pity — the specific pity a craftsman feels watching an amateur mishandle a tool he never understood. Arin had never met Belial in person, had never even seen him from any real distance. He knew him only through the harm he'd left across a month of hunting — the enforcer sent to Navarion, the frightened scouts who spoke his name like a threat, the whole apparatus of a war fought over prizes that had never actually belonged to either side. The pity came anyway.

Arin didn't know the shape of it exactly, couldn't have laid out the history the way a scholar might have, but something about the image left him certain enough of the outline: whoever now held this weapon had taken it believing it a prize, and had carried it since without understanding what carrying it actually cost him. The image gave him no dates, no count of years, nothing he could have written down as fact. It only gave him the feeling underneath the guess — a man mistaking a punishment for the ordinary price of power, never once asking himself why victory should have hurt this much. Arin found that almost sad, if his own half-formed sense of it was even close to right.

The pity didn't last long enough to become doubt. Arin let the image fade from his hand before it could. Belial had taken something that wasn't his. Whatever grief the theft had cost him since didn't cancel the theft. Arin had learned, across a decade of contracts, never to let a man's suffering excuse what he'd actually done — he'd watched too many clients try that exact argument on him, tears and all, and none of them had ever once changed the debt they owed. This proved only one thing plainly: some things could not belong to anyone who hadn't rightfully earned them.

---

His path was clear now, clearer than it had been since the corridor beneath Navarion. He would go to Hell itself, if that was what reclaiming Lament required of him. He did not know yet how a mortal man crossed into Hell and came back out again, only that the answer existed somewhere and that he intended to find it. He was not afraid of that journey the way the old, uncertain version of himself would have been. He thought, briefly, of Mikel — somewhere else on his own road, still dreaming of a sword he'd never held — and found himself hoping the man found what he was looking for.

He was not going to punish Belial out of vengeance. He was going to correct something that should never have stayed broken this long. That distinction mattered to him now in a way it never would have mattered a month ago, before a granary, a corridor, and a stranger's mercy taught him the difference. A month ago he would have called the distinction meaningless, a word men used to feel better about the same violence. He no longer believed that. The reasons a man carried into a fight were not decoration. They were the whole of what separated judgment from murder.

He walked south, unhurried, each step steady with the certainty of a man who had finally stopped arguing with what he actually was. The wind had dropped since morning, and the water below the cliff had gone quiet in a way it hadn't been since he'd first arrived. He counted his steps for a while out of old habit, the way he'd counted contracts, then stopped counting and simply walked. Whatever waited for him at the end of that road, he would meet it as Thaeriel, not as the mercenary who had spent a decade mistaking judgment for something smaller than it had always been.
