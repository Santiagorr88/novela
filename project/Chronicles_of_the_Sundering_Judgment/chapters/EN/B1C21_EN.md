# B1C21 – The Summit

## The Unmoving Figure

At the highest point of a peak higher than any the world had ever charted, where the air thinned past anything a mortal lung could draw and hold, a figure sat cross-legged upon bare stone. Centuries of snow had gathered across the folds of its robes without once disturbing the stillness beneath them, ice building layer upon layer along shoulders that had not shifted, had not settled, had not so much as trembled against the wind in longer than any record of this world could measure.

Its posture held the same shape it must have held at the very beginning of this long stillness — spine straight, hands resting open upon its knees, head tilted forward at an angle that suggested, to no eye present to observe it, something between rest and vigilance. Whatever had first settled it into this position, whatever purpose or exhaustion had brought it to this exact seat upon this exact stone, had left no trace beyond the posture itself, preserved now past any reasonable measure of time by nothing more than the simple, continued absence of movement.

It had not moved. It had not breathed. It had not thought, in any sense a living mind would recognize as thought, in millennia uncounted. The frost had claimed every visible surface of it in a slow, patient accumulation, until the figure and the granite it sat upon had become, to any eye that might have found its way up this impossible height, nearly indistinguishable from one another — a shape carved from the mountain's own heart rather than seated upon it.

Ice had thickened across the folds of its robes season upon season, each fresh storm adding some fraction more to a weight the figure showed no sign of registering, until the fabric itself had long since disappeared beneath a shell of pale, unbroken white. Beneath that shell, whatever remained still held the same silence it had always held. Nothing about the shape suggested decay. Nothing about it suggested growth, or change, or any of the small daily erosions that marked the passage of time everywhere else in the world below. It simply persisted, exactly as it had persisted, for longer than persistence itself usually meant anything at all.

No bird crossed this height, no insect, no living creature of any kind willing or able to test air this thin against its own survival. The silence here was not merely the absence of sound. It was the absence of anything that might have made sound in the first place, a stillness so complete that the wind itself, when it finally rose, seemed almost an intrusion against a quiet that had otherwise gone entirely uncontested for as long as the figure had occupied this particular stone.

The stars above the summit, on the rare nights the clouds thinned enough to reveal them, burned with a clarity no lower elevation could match, uncountable points of light scattered across a darkness this height alone seemed close enough to actually touch. The figure gave no sign of ever having looked up to see them. Whether it saw anything at all, in whatever sense seeing still applied to something that had not opened its eyes, by any outward measure, in longer than the mountain itself had likely stood at its current height, remained entirely unknowable to anything capable of asking the question.

## A Height Without a Name

The summit itself had never been charted by any cartographer of Heaven, Hell, or the mortal world beneath both, not because any will had hidden it, but because no expedition mounted by any of the three had ever survived the ascent long enough to record it. The air here held too little of whatever the body required to sustain itself, thin past the point where even the most disciplined lungs could draw more than a handful of desperate breaths before the world at the edges of vision began to dim.

No power had sealed this place away. No design had kept it secret. It remained untouched simply because reaching it demanded more than any mortal frame, however determined, had ever managed to survive giving — a summit preserved not by mystery but by altitude alone, indifferent to whoever might have wished to stand upon it.

Below the final approach, where the mountain's lower slopes still permitted breath and footing, generations of climbers across a dozen scattered kingdoms had left their markers — cairns of stacked stone, a rusted length of rope still anchored to an outcrop, the bleached remains of an expedition that had come further than most and still turned back with half its number lost to the thinning air. None had reached the summit itself. None had come close enough even to glimpse what waited there, cross-legged and silent above the last line any living climber had ever crossed and survived.

The mountain's name, where it had one at all, varied by kingdom and by century, each culture that glimpsed its distant silhouette from the lowlands settling on its own word for a shape too vast and too remote to properly claim. None of those names had ever reached the summit itself, any more than any climber had. Whatever this height was called in the valleys far below, it remained, at its peak, entirely without a name of its own — simply the place the air ran out, and the world, by whatever measure mattered at this altitude, effectively ended.

The wind at this height carried no warmth in any season, scouring the exposed rock down to a smoothness no lower slope could match, stripping away anything not anchored deeply enough to withstand centuries of its passage. Snow fell here in flurries too fine to properly be called snow, more a suspended crystalline haze than any accumulation a mortal traveler would have recognized from lower ground. It was, by any measure available to the world beneath it, simply the most extreme geography that world contained — nothing more, and nothing less.

## A Tremor in Reality

Something moved through the world far below, faint enough that no single point of origin could have accounted for it alone. It arrived not as a single note but as an accumulation, dozens of small dissonances rising at once from places too distant and too varied to trace back to any one source — the sound, if it could be called sound at all, of a tension that had been building for longer than any single event could explain, finally beginning to find its way toward release.

It was not an earthquake, not in any sense the mortal world below would have recognized the word. No ground split open beneath any city, no ocean rose to swallow any shore. The disturbance moved through something considerably less visible than stone or water, a discord felt rather than witnessed, present everywhere at once and yet nowhere specific enough to be pointed to and named. It did not announce its cause. It offered only its arrival, distant and unmistakably real, spreading outward from wherever it had begun toward every corner of a world that had, for a very long time now, been drawing tighter around some strain it had not yet been asked to bear.

The world's ordinary business continued below, unaware and unaffected in any way that could be measured by instrument or sense. No sky darkened. No sea stilled. Merchants in a dozen scattered ports went on loading their ships, soldiers in a dozen scattered armies went on sharpening their blades, and not one of them registered so much as a flicker of whatever had just passed through the deeper architecture underlying all of it. Whatever the tremor actually was, it moved through some layer of the world that ordinary attention never reached and ordinary instruments had no means of registering, leaving the surface of things exactly as undisturbed as it had been the moment before.

It moved, all the same, upward — through layers the mortal world had no name for, past the last thin edges of the mountain's lower slopes, climbing higher still until it finally reached the summit at the very top.

## The First Crack

The tremor found the summit eventually, thinned by distance to something barely perceptible by the time it finally reached the highest point of the world. It should have arrived as nothing at all, a disturbance too faint by the time it climbed this high to register against anything solid enough to feel it.

It reached the stone beneath the seated figure regardless, and the stone cracked.

A single hairline fissure traced its way across the granite's frost-covered surface, beginning at some point beneath the figure's stillness and spreading outward in a thin, irregular line before finally stopping. It was, by any ordinary measure, an insignificant thing — a crack no wider than a single strand of the ice that covered it, in a place no living creature would ever travel far enough to witness. But this was ground that had not changed in longer than memory could reliably hold, granite that had outlasted empires without once shifting beneath the weight of the millennia pressing down upon it. Here, in this particular stillness, so small a fracture landed with the force of something considerably larger.

The ice covering the stone shifted fractionally around the new fissure's edges, the first true movement this particular patch of ground had registered since long before the figure seated upon it had first settled into its current stillness. A thin dusting of frost broke loose along the crack's length and drifted, briefly, on air too thin to properly carry it, before settling again into the same unbroken white that covered everything else at this height. No sound accompanied the movement. At this altitude, sound itself struggled to travel any real distance, thinned by the same starved air that made the ascent impossible for anything still capable of breathing it.

The figure did not react. Whatever passed for awareness in something this still, this old, gave no visible sign of registering the change occurring in the very stone supporting it. The crack remained regardless, a fact now written into ground that had held no new facts in longer than any measure of time could properly account for.

## A Universe Holding Its Breath

The wind rose around the seated figure in the crack's aftermath, howling across a summit that had known no other sound for longer than any record could measure, scouring frost from stone that had held its shape, unbroken, since long before this height had a name at all. The figure did not stir. Snow continued to gather along shoulders that had not shifted in millennia, indifferent to whatever had just passed beneath them.

The fissure remained, thin and pale against the granite, curved along part of its length into something that resembled, to no eye present to read it that way, a question mark carved into the heart of the world. Somewhere in the vast, tangled architecture of a world balanced on forces older than any single kingdom's memory, something had been touched that had not been touched in a very long time.

Nothing about the summit had changed beyond that single thin line in the stone. The wind continued its ceaseless circuit around the seated shape, the snow continued its slow, patient accumulation, and fresh frost gathered along the fissure's edges without erasing it. The crack held its shape beneath every new layer the wind carried over it, small and entirely new against ground that had known nothing new in longer than memory itself extended.

Elsewhere in the world, far below this particular stillness, the faint disturbance that had produced the crack continued to fade, dissipating back into whatever depth it had first risen from, leaving no visible trace anywhere it had passed except for this one thin line in ancient stone. No record would ever mark the moment. Nothing beyond the mountain itself, and whatever remained of the figure seated upon it, would ever register that the tremor had reached this high at all.

The figure did not move. The world, in whatever way a world can be said to hold anything at all, seemed to hold its breath regardless, waiting on the one small, unlikely thing it had not seen happen in longer than anyone living could measure — the figure, finally, beginning to stir.
