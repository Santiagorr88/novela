# B1T02 – The One Already Watching

## The Method

Selm had been holding position above the same ridge for the better part of two days when the target finally showed himself, a mid-ranking angelic courier whose route Hell's intelligence apparatus had been mapping for weeks in anticipation of exactly this movement. He did not draw Specter the moment the courier came into range. He waited instead, motionless behind the active camouflage that had made his name across Hell's ranks, watching the courier's habits settle into a predictable rhythm before committing to anything as final as a shot. Patience cost nothing a hunter couldn't afford to spend. Selm had built his entire reputation on that principle, and had never once found reason to abandon it.

The kill itself, when it finally came, took less time than the two days of waiting that preceded it — a single arrow from a position well outside the courier's line of sight, the angel falling without ever turning toward the shot. Selm did not linger over the body. He had learned long ago that the waiting was the actual work, and the shot itself only its brief, necessary conclusion.

He gathered what intelligence the courier's satchel actually held, cataloguing it with the same unhurried thoroughness he brought to any part of the assignment, and moved on without further ceremony. Two days of waiting for a kill that took less than a second rarely struck outsiders as an efficient use of time. Selm had long since stopped explaining himself to anyone who thought otherwise. The results spoke plainly enough on their own, assignment after assignment, and he had never needed more of an argument than that.

Other hunters under Hell's banner favored speed, closing the distance fast and trusting raw aggression to carry them through whatever resistance a target managed to offer. Selm had watched enough of them die to know exactly how that particular gamble tended to resolve itself over a long enough career. He preferred a slower calculus — identify the target's habits, wait for the single moment those habits created an opening too clean to waste, and take it without hesitation once it arrived. It had kept him alive across centuries where flashier hunters had not lasted a fraction as long.

## The New Assignment

He was still cleaning Specter's string when the summons reached him, relayed through the usual quiet channels Hell's intelligence network kept for exactly this purpose. The liaison who delivered it in person wasted no time on pleasantries. "Locate what it is," the liaison said. "If Heaven's already looking, we look closer." Selm did not lower his eyes from the horizon while he listened, a habit too deeply ingrained to break even for briefings that actually mattered, one more small discipline among the many that had kept him alive this long. "And if it's nothing?" he asked. "Then you'll have lost a day," the liaison said. "Go."

Selm gathered what little detail the liaison actually had to offer — Vepar's own apparatus had picked up a faint resonance out past the eastern patrol lines, unremarkable at first pass except for the fact that an angelic scout had gone missing from his usual route for the better part of a day and reappeared with no explanation Hell's informants had yet managed to extract. Whatever the resonance actually was, no one on Vepar's side claimed to understand it yet. That alone was reason enough to send someone competent after it before Heaven's own investigation got any further ahead. Selm's orders were simple enough not to require further elaboration: deny Heaven any advantage they had already found, or at minimum, understand it well enough that Hell was not the last to know.

"You said Foras's people flagged this," Selm said, more confirmation than question, already running through what little he knew of that particular chain of informants. "Vepar's, this time," the liaison corrected. "Same instinct either way. Something's moving out there that neither side fully understands yet, and both of them would rather understand it first than find out the hard way what happens if they don't." Selm turned the information over briefly, weighing it against the sparse detail he had actually been given to work with. It was not much of a briefing. It was, in his experience, rarely much more than this before a hunt actually began — the interesting part of the work had never been the briefing itself, only what a hunter managed to piece together once he was actually in motion.

He asked no further questions before setting out, the same discipline that had served him across every assignment before this one. A hunter who needed every detail explained before he could begin working was, in Selm's long experience, rarely worth sending in the first place. He preferred to build his own understanding from the ground the trail itself provided, trusting his own reconstruction over anyone else's secondhand account of what they thought had happened.

## Following the Trail

The trail itself took him the better part of a day to reconstruct, built from fragments rather than any single clean lead — a discarded travel permit here, a half-erased footprint there, the accumulated small carelessness that even a careful scout inevitably left behind across enough ground. Selm had tracked harder quarries than this one across his long service, and found something almost satisfying in the meticulous, unhurried process of piecing together a route from evidence this thin. It was, in its own way, no different from the patience that had carried him through two days above a ridge waiting for a single courier. The hunt rewarded exactly the kind of attention most soldiers found tedious, and Selm had never once found it tedious himself.

The travel permit had been the easiest piece to work from, a stamped waypoint that placed the scout's last confirmed position somewhere along a stretch of coastline Selm knew reasonably well from prior assignments. The footprints proved more useful still, a set of tracks leading down to the waterline itself, where a single overturned skiff and a length of mooring rope cut clean rather than untied told him plainly enough that the scout had left in some hurry, without waiting to secure the boat properly behind him. Selm found the skiff's twin a short distance down the shore, half-hidden in reeds, and used its own weathered heading marks to reconstruct the rough bearing the scout had most likely taken out across open water. He crouched beside the cut rope a long moment, reading the evidence the way another hunter might read a written report, building a picture of a man moving with purpose rather than wandering.

He crossed onto unlisted water sometime past midday, following the heading he had reconstructed from the skiff and the shoreline evidence together, and found, well before any coastline came into view, that his own sense of direction had begun behaving strangely — subtle at first, a faint reluctance in his instincts to commit fully to the course he had already charted. He noted the sensation without alarm, filing it alongside everything else the trail had already taught him about wherever it was actually leading.

The unease deepened as he closed the remaining distance, a persistent, low-grade wrongness he could not immediately trace to any single cause. Selm had crossed hostile ground more times than he could easily count, had walked through wards and traps and every manner of deliberate deterrent Heaven's engineers had ever devised. This felt like none of those things. It felt, if he allowed himself the comparison, more like the ground itself simply did not agree with straightforward navigation, indifferent rather than hostile, which somehow unsettled him more than outright hostility would have. He pressed on regardless, trusting the course established by the evidence despite the growing discomfort.

## Watching Unseen

He found the scout on the island's far shore, working his way back toward the coastline with the particular unhurried care of a man retracing his own steps with more caution than his first approach had used. Selm settled into cover well before the scout came within any reasonable range of noticing him, camouflage folding seamlessly into the island's strange, spiraling foliage, and simply watched. The scout moved carefully, methodically, pausing often to check his own bearings against landmarks Selm could not immediately identify — the particular caution, Selm judged from everything he could observe, of a man who had already been badly disoriented once today and did not intend to repeat the experience.

Selm held the position a long while, patient in the way his entire service had trained him to be, cataloguing every detail of the scout's movement and equipment with the same careful attention he brought to any target. A staff, unremarkable in appearance but plainly significant to its bearer, judging by how frequently the scout consulted it. Angelic, certainly — the bearing alone confirmed that much before Selm ever needed a closer look. Beyond that, he had no immediate read on exactly what this particular angel represented, or why Heaven had sent someone this deep into unlisted water chasing whatever had drawn them both here.

He noted, too, the particular quality of the scout's attention to his surroundings — not the hurried scanning of a soldier expecting an ambush, but something closer to genuine care, the movement of a man trying to understand a place rather than simply survive passing through it. Selm had hunted enough angels over his long service to recognize when one was afraid and when one was merely being careful, and this scout, whatever else he might be, did not read to Selm as afraid. That distinction mattered more than it might have to a less patient hunter. Fear made targets predictable in one set of ways. Genuine caution made them predictable in an entirely different set, and Selm preferred to know which kind of prey he was actually tracking before he committed to any further move.

The scout paused near a cluster of spiraling undergrowth, kneeling to examine something Selm could not make out from his own position, and remained there long enough that Selm allowed himself to shift his weight slightly, easing the strain of holding still for so long without breaking his own cover in the process. He had learned patience the hard way, early in his service, watching a less careful hunter break position a moment too soon and pay for the mistake permanently. He did not intend to repeat that particular lesson tonight.

## The Easy Kill

The shot would have been trivial. Selm understood that with the same flat, professional certainty he brought to any assessment of a target's vulnerability — the scout giving no indication that he had detected him, no cover between them worth the name, the angle clean enough that Specter would not even need to work for it. He held the position anyway, arrow nocked but not drawn, weighing the kill against everything he did not yet know about why it mattered.

A dead scout told Hell nothing beyond the fact that he had once existed. A scout followed, watched, understood, might eventually lead somewhere worth actually knowing about — whatever had drawn Heaven's attention to this particular stretch of unlisted water in the first place. Selm had never once mistaken restraint for mercy, and did not mistake it for that now. This was tactics, plain and simple, the same calculation that had kept him alive and effective across centuries of exactly this kind of work. A hunter who killed the first quarry he found rarely learned anything worth the kill.

He thought briefly of the liaison's own instructions, deny Heaven any advantage they had already found. A body left on this shore accomplished exactly none of that. It would only tell whoever came looking eventually that something out here was worth killing over, without ever explaining what. Selm preferred information to bodies whenever the choice was actually his to make, and today, watching a careful scout work his way back across unfamiliar ground showing no sign that he suspected he was being observed, the choice was entirely his.

## The Decision to Keep Watching

He eased Specter back down without releasing the arrow, settling deeper into the camouflage rather than closing the distance. The scout, still working his careful way back toward the shore, continued to give no indication that he knew he had been found, moving through the island's strange terrain with the same unhurried caution he had shown since Selm first laid eyes on him. Selm found something almost familiar in that caution, watching it from cover — a hunter's instinct for unfamiliar ground, however differently it expressed itself on each side of this particular divide.

He would follow rather than strike, at least for tonight, patient the way patience had always served him better than haste. Whatever this island actually held, whatever had drawn an angelic scout this far from his usual patrol and left Selm's own instincts faintly unsettled the closer he came to its shore, it was worth understanding before it was worth ending.

He settled in to wait, unseen, adjusting his position only enough to keep the scout in clear view as the light overhead shifted in ways that struck him, watching it now with nothing else to occupy his attention, as considerably stranger than he had first allowed himself to notice. The scout showed no sign of alarm at it, which told Selm either that the phenomenon was familiar enough to expect, or that the scout's own attention was occupied elsewhere entirely. Either answer was worth having. He intended to have both before the night was finished, and let the scout lead him wherever he intended to go next.
