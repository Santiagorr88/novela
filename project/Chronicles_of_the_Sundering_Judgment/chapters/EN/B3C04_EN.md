# B3C04 – The First Lesson: Laughter

Ereloth had a plan for the morning, and true to form, he refused to explain any part of it until they were already three streets deep into executing it. He'd shown up at the door of Mikel's rented room before sunrise, guitar case slung across one shoulder, and announced that today was going to be educational whether Mikel liked the sound of that or not. Mikel had learned, over the handful of days they'd spent together since the tavern, that arguing with the plan before it started never once changed its shape. He found himself standing at the edge of a small plaza an hour later, watching a street musician struggle through a set that nobody in the passing crowd seemed inclined to stop for, while Ereloth crouched beside a fountain doing something with his fingers that Mikel couldn't quite follow.

"Watch the light," Ereloth said, not looking up from whatever he was doing with the water.

Mikel watched. A moment later, the water in the fountain caught the morning sun at an angle it hadn't been catching a moment before, throwing a scatter of small rainbows across the plaza that hadn't existed until Ereloth apparently decided they should. People slowed. A few stopped outright, phones raised, and within a minute a loose crowd had gathered close enough to the musician that his hat started collecting coins for the first time all morning.

"That's it?" Mikel asked. "A magic trick to help a street performer make rent?"

"That's most of it, yes." Ereloth stood, brushing off his knees, entirely satisfied with himself. "You were expecting something grander?"

"I was expecting you to actually address what I came here to talk about."

---

They didn't. Instead, an hour later, Mikel found himself standing in a hotel ballroom in a jacket Ereloth had produced from somewhere without explanation, the sleeves a half-inch too short and the fabric smelling faintly of someone else's cologne. Neither of them had been invited. Ereloth had walked past the two attendants at the door with the easy confidence of a man who belonged exactly where he was standing, and Mikel, trailing a step behind, had apparently read as convincing enough company that nobody stopped either of them. He watched his companion tip an entire tray of canapés into the air with a flick of two fingers just as a particularly self-important gala guest turned to accept it from a waiter. What followed was less a fight than a slow-motion catastrophe of shrimp and pastry, three more trays going down in the chaos before anyone identified a cause, and Mikel felt a laugh building somewhere behind his ribs, caught and swallowed before it could fully escape him, appalled at how close it had come to getting out.

"You keep bringing up the thing that's eating the border realms," Ereloth said later, once they'd left the gala at a brisk and unhurried walk that somehow outpaced the security called after them, "like I'm going to forget you asked if I ignore it long enough. I'm not going to forget. I'm choosing not to answer it yet, which is a different thing entirely." He led them three more blocks without explaining where, until the noise of the city thinned into the quieter hush of a small municipal park, empty at this hour except for the two of them. He crouched at the edge of a park pond, selecting a flat stone from the shore with the seriousness of a man choosing a weapon. "Do you know how to skip one of these?"

"That's not remotely what I asked."

"No," Ereloth agreed, settling onto the pond's edge with the easy comfort of a man who intended to stay there a while. "But it's what I'm teaching. Sit."

---

Mikel sat, mostly because standing and arguing with a man who wasn't listening felt like wasted effort, the damp grass cool beneath him even through his coat, and watched Ereloth demonstrate the throw four times before handing him a stone of his own. His first three attempts sank without a single skip. His fourth managed one weak hop before disappearing beneath the surface. Ereloth whooped like he'd witnessed something remarkable.

"There," he said. "Progress."

"It skipped once."

"It skipped once *more than the last one did*. That's the entire skill, Miki. Progress doesn't announce itself with trumpets. It just quietly does one more thing than it did yesterday." He selected another stone, weighed it in his palm, and sent it skittering across the water in six clean bounces. "I'm not teaching you to skip stones because I think you need a hobby. I'm teaching you because you've spent a month treating every failure like a verdict instead of information."

Mikel picked up another stone, turning it in his fingers to find its flattest edge the way Ereloth had shown him, and threw it with more care than the last attempt. Two skips this time, then a third, weaker one before it sank. He felt an unreasonable satisfaction at the sight of it, disproportionate to the actual accomplishment involved.

"See," Ereloth said, not looking at him, already selecting his next stone. "You didn't need my permission to notice that either."

---

Night found them on the flat roof of a building Mikel hadn't asked permission to access and suspected Ereloth hadn't either, the city spread out below them in scattered light. Ereloth's easy performer's manner had gone somewhere quieter, the way it had once before, in the tavern, right before he'd said something Mikel had lain awake with every night since.

"Solmire wasn't forged to punish," Ereloth said, without any preamble to soften the shift in tone. "It was forged to prune. There's a difference, and you spent your whole first life failing to see it."

"Prune."

"Cut away what's already dead so something alive can keep growing." Ereloth looked out over the city rather than at him. "You used it like a club instead, brother. You beat the world with it until all the light went out, and you called that justice because the alternative was admitting you'd built your entire authority on being right rather than being kind." He said it without cruelty, the way a man states a fact he's long since made peace with. "That's not an accusation. It's just what happened. I watched it happen."

Mikel sat with that a long moment, the city noise rising faint and distant below them. He had no argument ready. He suspected, sitting there, that arguing would have missed the point of Ereloth saying it at all. He thought instead of a hundred small verdicts he'd handed down as Michael, certainties he'd never once questioned at the time because questioning had never once occurred to him as a possibility worth entertaining. He thought, too, of nine years grading essays with the same unquestioned confidence, marking down every argument that didn't fit the shape he'd already decided was correct. Two very different lives, and the same flaw running through both of them like a single thread he'd simply never noticed before tonight.

---

"So where's a sword made of laughter and light gone to sulk?" Ereloth asked eventually, the lightness returning to his voice as abruptly as it had left. "That's the actual question, isn't it? Where is she?"

"I assumed Belial had her. Or destroyed her. I've assumed a dozen things and confirmed none of them."

"Belial never had her." Ereloth said it plainly, no drama in the delivery at all. "She left on her own, the day you fell. Rejected every hand that tried to claim her since, because none of them had learned what they needed to learn first. She's been waiting a long while now, on an island most maps have given up trying to hold onto properly." He picked up a stone from beside the ledge, turned it once, and set it back down without throwing it. "Waiting long enough that she built herself a storm out of the waiting — grief she manufactured entirely on her own, with nobody else's help at all." He said this last part almost fondly, the way one might describe a stubborn old friend's worst habit. "A sword made of pure joy, sulking in a storm of her own making. There's something almost funny about that, if you squint."

Mikel found he couldn't quite laugh at it, not yet, though he understood why Ereloth wanted him to. A sword forged from nothing but delight, punishing itself for a fall it hadn't caused, in a storm it had built entirely alone. It sounded less like a joke the longer he considered it, and more like something he recognized far too well to find funny.

"You're saying she's been punishing herself."

"I'm saying waiting does that to a person. Or a sword. Doesn't much matter which." Ereloth stood, stretching, the seriousness already draining back out of him. "You'll see her soon enough. She won't make it easy. She's earned the right not to."

---

He demonstrated, unprompted, an elaborate courtly bow clearly meant to punctuate the moment with some flourish of theater, and misjudged the edge of the rooftop by exactly enough to send himself sprawling, catching himself at the last second on one outflung arm with a total absence of grace. He lay there a moment, apparently pleased with the outcome regardless, and looked up at Mikel with an expression that dared him not to laugh.

Mikel laughed. It came out before he'd decided to allow it, sharp and startled and entirely genuine, the kind of laugh he couldn't remember producing even once during nine careful, well-ordered years as Mikel Ardon. It surprised him more than it seemed to surprise Ereloth, who was already scrambling back to his feet, pointing one triumphant finger.

"Ah! There you are." He said it like a man greeting someone he'd been waiting on for a very long time. "Now you're almost ready."

"Ready for what?"

"For the part where the lesson actually starts mattering." Ereloth didn't explain further. He rarely did, on the nights that mattered most.

---

Mikel sat with that laugh a long while after Ereloth had wandered off to find them both something to eat, working through the evening in his own quiet way, not with words exactly, more with the plain sense of having crossed some small distance he couldn't yet measure. He tried, out of old habit, to catalogue the day the way he'd have catalogued any lesson worth remembering — the fountain and its unearned rainbows, the ruined gala and its scattered canapés, the stones and their small, quiet progress, the rooftop confession, the fall that had made him laugh without permission.

He noticed, cataloguing it, an absence rather than a presence, the kind of gap that only becomes visible once you go looking for the thing that should have filled it: he had not once, across the entire day, reached for the word efficiency. It had structured nearly every hour of his life as a professor, a private measuring stick he applied to lectures and arguments and even, on his worse days, to his own patience with other people. He searched for it now, out of habit, and found it simply hadn't come up. Not because he'd consciously set it aside. Because a fountain full of rainbows and a ruined tray of canapés and one badly executed bow had, somewhere in the course of an ordinary afternoon, made the word feel entirely beside the point.

He didn't know yet what that absence meant for the man he was becoming. He suspected, sitting alone on a rooftop in a city he hadn't planned to visit, that he was about to find out.

Ereloth returned some while later with two paper bags of food from somewhere he never bothered explaining, and settled back onto the ledge without ceremony, tearing into his own meal with the same unhurried enthusiasm he brought to everything else. Mikel accepted the bag handed to him and found, to his mild surprise, that he was genuinely hungry, in a plain and uncomplicated way that had nothing to do with the day's stranger revelations. They ate in silence for a while, the city humming faintly below them, and Mikel found the silence didn't need filling the way it usually did.
