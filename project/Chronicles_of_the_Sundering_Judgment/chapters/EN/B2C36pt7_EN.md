# B2C36.7 – No One Trained to Hear It

Raziel kept his wards the way other commanders kept armories: quietly, personally, and without delegating a single stone of them to anyone who might one day report on what they found. Twelve of them ringed his private study, each tuned to a cadence he had set with his own hand decades before any of his three current captains had earned their rank, and for most of those decades they had done nothing but hum at a pitch too low for even his own ear to notice, which was precisely the point of a ward built to wait. Tonight, the seventh one had gone bright.

He set Ignotus down closed, which he rarely did mid-evening, and crossed to the stone before the light had finished its first pulse. The cadence rising off it matched nothing he had built into the ward to expect — not an intrusion, not a weapon test, not the ordinary friction of two realms grinding against each other at their usual seams. It matched the four lines Selaphiel had carried into the council chamber three nights past, close enough in shape that he needed no translation to recognize the family the sound belonged to, and different enough in weight that he understood immediately it was not the same fragment. Older. Weaker. Still unclaimed by anyone's liturgy. Still, by every measure his ward could take, real.

He did not send word to Gabriel. He did not send word to Iofiel, whose translation work would have made short, generous use of a second confirmed fragment, and whose enthusiasm for exactly that use was the reason he chose not to. A council already fracturing over the speed of one revelation did not need a second handed to it before anyone had determined what the first one had cost the world simply by surfacing. He sent instead for the three captains who answered to no authority above him but Gabriel's own, and who had never once, in all the centuries of his command, needed the reason behind an order explained to them before they'd act on it.

---

Eshriel arrived first, already moving before he'd finished crossing the threshold, the restless heat of him filling the doorway a full breath before the rest of him did. Jahiel came next, unhurried, his robe's white catching the ward-light without holding onto any of it. Zaphiel came last and stood straightest, as he always did, in a stillness Raziel had long since decided was a discipline rather than simply the absence of motion.

"A resonance," Raziel said, without preamble, and told them what the ward had found, and where — a burned monastic ruin on Earth called, by the handful of scholars who still remembered it existed, the Ashgrave, built four centuries past on a leyline no one currently living had mapped. "Old. Weak. Unclaimed. I want it verified, contained, and reported to me alone before it is reported to anyone else."

"Contained." Eshriel said the word like it cost him something to hold in his mouth that long. "If it's Codex, burn it clean and we're done by morning. Wildfire doesn't ask the tinder's permission to be efficient, Commander."

"If it's Codex," Jahiel said, mild and immovable at once, "then it may also be scripture no one has yet been given the chance to keep. My blade cleaves what's corrupted. It has never once needed to cleave what's merely old." He did not look at Eshriel when he said it, which somehow carried more weight than if he had.

Zaphiel said nothing for a moment, his pale gaze resting on Raziel rather than on either of the other two. "You didn't take this to the council," he said finally. "You have every fragment you need to justify taking it there. You didn't."

"No," Raziel agreed, and offered nothing further.

"Then there's something in the shape of it you already recognize, and you're sending the three of us to confirm a thing you've already decided is true." Zaphiel's voice held no accusation in it, only the flat, unhurried certainty of a man reading a verdict off a wall. "That's not verification. That's cover, and you know it."

"It's both," Raziel said, "and you'll have everything I can safely give you before you leave this room, which is the coordinates, the danger, and my order. The rest isn't safely mine to give yet — not because I doubt you'd carry it, but because I've watched knowledge like this cost captains who carried it before they understood the weight, and I am not yet willing to spend either of you finding out whether this is that kind of knowledge or not." He held Zaphiel's stare until the younger officer's jaw eased, not into agreement, but into what Raziel read as the patience of a soldier filing a grievance rather than abandoning it. "Go. Report to me. No one else."

---

The Ashgrave had not burned in the way its name suggested fire burned — no scorch, no collapse, only a slow grey stillness settled into stone that should have long since been reclaimed by moss and weather and simply hadn't been, as though the ruin itself had been asked to wait for something and had never been told the waiting was over. Eshriel felt the wrongness of it before he understood any part of what it meant, a heat under his own skin answering a heat that wasn't there, and had to stop himself twice from simply loosing Helios into the nearest standing wall on the theory that whatever was wrong here would stop being wrong once it stopped existing.

"You'll bring the whole roof down before I've had a chance to look at what's under it," came the voice at his shoulder, not sharp, only certain, and Eshriel turned to find Jahiel already crouched at the threshold, one palm flat against a floor stone gone smooth with an age that predated the fire that supposedly claimed the place.

"It's not talking back to you yet. Give it a reason."

"It's already talking," Jahiel said. "Just not to you." He rose, and for the first time since they'd arrived, something in his gentle, iron-willed face went carefully still. "Stand at the door, Eshriel. Not because I doubt your nerve. Because I don't yet know what walking further in costs."

---

What it cost, Jahiel found within a dozen more steps, was not a wound in any shape Caelum had ever been built to answer. The corruption his sword hunted for on instinct simply wasn't here — no rot, no infernal residue, no enemy hand at work in the stone at all. The deepest chamber was smaller than the ruin above it suggested, a low round room with a single unbroken script spiraling inward from the walls to a point at the room's exact center, each letter cut deep and clean by a hand that had known precisely what it was carving. He crouched at the outer ring and read what he could of it — an old dialect, close enough to liturgical Heaven-tongue that meaning came through in patches, a promise made by someone to someone else, the specifics worn illegible by three separate scorch-lines that had eaten through the stone without ever quite reaching the words themselves. Scripture, plainly. Abandoned by whatever order once kept it, when the fire that never finished burning had scattered them. It should have simply been old. Instead the deepest letters, nearest the room's center, pulsed faint and getting less faint under his palm, in the exact cadence of a thing recently remembered rather than a thing merely rediscovered.

Words that no one had spoken aloud in centuries did not, in Jahiel's long experience, simply sit quietly and wait to be read. Left too long without an ear built to receive them properly, meaning curdled — not into falsehood, but into a hunger for whatever ear came nearest, willing or not. He had seen precisely this described in old training texts he'd assumed, until tonight, were metaphor. Watching the carved letters at the center shiver faintly at the sound of his own breath, close enough now that he could feel the stone give back a warmth no ordinary rock held, he revised that assumption, and did not enjoy revising it.

---

Zaphiel found the caretaker on his second circuit of the ruin's outer wall — an old man, mortal, half-blind with cataracts and entirely unaware that the ruin he tended for a handful of coins a season had ever been more than a burned church too stubborn to finish falling down. He was sweeping ash from a doorway that led nowhere, singing tunelessly to himself in the dialect of a village two valleys over, and he did not look up, did not flinch, gave no sign whatsoever that anything stood before him at all. The man's eyes passed over Zaphiel the way they passed over the air.

It should have settled the question cleanly. It didn't. The resonance was strengthening — Zaphiel could feel it now the way he felt the charge before a storm, a pressure behind the eyes that Claris answered without being asked — and every doctrine he'd been raised on said a threat this size, this close to waking fully, was cleansed root and soil both, without exception carved out for what couldn't be helped standing too near the root. True light didn't comfort. He'd said as much to squads a hundred times, meant it every time he'd said it.

"He can't see us," Jahiel said, arriving at his shoulder before Zaphiel had finished deciding what his own silence meant. "That doesn't make him safe from what's underneath his feet. But it means whatever we do here, we do it around him, not through him."

"Mercy was never on the list of things I was built to hand out," Zaphiel said. "Only judgment. And judgment doesn't usually stop to ask who's standing in its light."

"No one's asking you to stop it. Only to aim it."

"If the site goes fully unstable—"

"Then it goes unstable around him too, and we've lost nothing by trying to keep him breathing in the meantime." Jahiel's voice didn't rise. It rarely did, in Zaphiel's experience. "You judge what deserves the light, Zaphiel. I've never once heard you claim an old man sweeping ash he doesn't understand deserves to be judged for it."

Zaphiel said nothing. Somewhere beneath them, the fragment's pulse quickened, and the floor of the Ashgrave began, very softly, to sing.

---

It came apart from the chamber's central carving all at once — not a creature, not quite, but a shape assembled out of meaning with nowhere left to go, half-formed syllables given weight by centuries with no one there to catch them, spreading outward from the room's center toward the three points where Zaphiel and the other two stood spaced around the spiral's outer ring. Eshriel took it first, nearest the door, Helios's incandescent burst driving into the shape's leading edge and scattering it into a dozen smaller pieces instead of one — no clean kill in it, not from where Zaphiel stood watching across the width of the chamber, only enough force to keep it from closing the distance whole.

Jahiel went in after, closer to the center than either of the others now, Caelum held tight to his body, each strike landing only where the blade found something worth cleaving and pulling short everywhere else. Zaphiel watched him take one glancing return across the shoulder rather than let the sword swing wide into a piece it hadn't yet judged — a choice made in less time than it took to see it made.

The largest piece came for Zaphiel himself, driven back toward the doorway and the caretaker still sweeping ash three strides beyond it, and he met it there rather than let it pass him, Claris through its center in a single thrust that blinded and pierced in the same motion the way it always had. In the half-second before it unmade, entirely without the malice he'd spent a career assuming its kind always carried, the shape resolved — not into a monster, not into anything that deserved the word enemy — into four words, worn soft with repetition, that some long-dead voice had once meant only as a promise: *someone will remember this.* Nothing more. No threat behind it. No design.

Zaphiel stood over the ash it left with the lance still raised, close enough now to hear the old man's tuneless singing carry on undisturbed behind him, and found that true light, judging exactly what it had been built to judge, had nothing left this time to say back to him.

---

They sealed the chamber rather than scoured it — Jahiel's decision, made without a vote, and neither of the others contested it, though for different reasons Jahiel suspected neither would ever say aloud to him. Before he sealed it, he knelt at the spiral's outer ring and copied the whole inscription onto a single page, word by unfamiliar word — Raziel had asked for a report, and to Jahiel's mind a report meant bringing back what was actually written, not his own summary of it. What remained of the carving, once the shape built on top of it had burned away, was true scripture and nothing else: a promise someone had once made and meant, orphaned by whatever fire had taken the rest of the order that kept it, guilty of nothing but being left too long without a listener. Caelum found no corruption left to cleave once the shape it had worn was gone, and Jahiel had learned enough tonight not to trust his own certainty past the evidence in front of him. He set a ward of his own devising over the entrance instead — patient, listening, built to wake if the cadence changed again — sealed the page inside his coat, and left the old man sweeping his doorway to nowhere, memory untouched, ash undisturbed.

Eshriel wanted to burn it anyway, said so twice on the walk back to where they could open a passage home, and stopped saying so the third time only because Jahiel didn't bother arguing back, just let the silence sit until Eshriel filled it himself with something closer to agreement than either of them would call it by that name. Zaphiel said nothing at all, the lance still faintly warm at his side, turning four unthreatening words over in a mind built for verdicts that had never before needed to hold one this small.

---

Raziel received the report alone, as ordered, and did not open the sealed page Jahiel brought back from the chamber floor in front of any of them. He set it beside Ignotus instead, unread, and told his captains only that they had done exactly as he'd asked and would be told the rest when there was a rest worth telling.

Zaphiel held his gaze a moment past the point courtesy required, the same unspent grievance from the study still sitting behind his eyes, and left without pressing it a second time. Eshriel left already restless for whatever came next. Jahiel lingered longest at the door, said nothing, and Raziel let him go without offering him anything either.

Alone, he broke the seal himself and read past the promise his captains had already told him about — *someone will remember this*, unremarkable, exactly as reported — to a second clause further down the spiral, one Jahiel's copy had rendered faithfully without remarking on it at all. Four words, in a cadence he recognized at once, close enough to the shape of a verse he had already decided, weeks ago and for reasons he had given no one, not even Gabriel, he was not yet ready to speak aloud. He set the page face-down beside the tome that had never once, in all his long keeping of it, needed to be told what it was for. His own captains had carried the words home for him themselves, copied in Jahiel's own careful hand, and never once recognized what they were carrying — and he found, turning that fact over in the study's low light, that he did not yet know which one of them he had actually protected tonight, and which one he had simply used.
