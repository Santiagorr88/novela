# Chapter 7 — Hunter of Echoes

## An Unclaimed Assignment

Camael found him in the training yard, spear already in hand, and did not waste time on preamble. "There's a demon masking itself in a human host, in a city called Navarion. I want you on it." He said it the way he said most orders in the training yard, already half-turned to leave, as though the matter had been settled before he arrived.

Sariel did not look up from Penumbra, running a cloth along its edge out of habit more than need, the motion unbroken by the interruption. "Why me?"

"Because souls don't lie to you, and this hunt needs someone who won't be talked out of anything." Camael's tone left no room for negotiation, though it very rarely did with him.

Sariel nodded once, set the cloth carefully aside, and asked nothing further. He had learned long ago that Camael's assignments came with exactly as much explanation as Camael judged necessary, and that pressing for more rarely produced anything beyond a repeated version of the same answer. Other captains under Camael's command, he knew, found this brusqueness difficult to work under. Sariel himself had never much minded it. Words spent on unnecessary explanation were words that could have been spent on the work itself. He gathered what he needed and left the Bastion within the hour.

## The Scent of Damnation

The mortal city surrounded him with garish colors and layers of sound he could not fully parse, the way it always did on the rare occasions his duty brought him to Earth. Sariel did not track his target by sight. He tracked it by scent — not smell in any sense a human would recognize, but a faint, specific wrongness beneath the ordinary noise of human souls, sulfur and spiritual rot masked under a thin veneer of borrowed humanity.

He moved through the crowded streets unseen, invisible to every mortal eye by simple virtue of not choosing to be seen, a lone purpose walking quietly through a world that had no idea it was being walked through at all. Vendors called out prices he did not need and never would. A pair of lovers argued near a bus stop, oblivious to him passing within arm's reach of either of them. His want was uncomplicated: find the trail, follow it to its end, close the matter cleanly. He had done this a hundred times before. He expected no different tonight.

## A City That Does Not Know

The trail led him past a market square filled with mortal commerce, past children chasing each other around a fountain, past an old man arguing cheerfully with a vendor over the price of fruit. A lone street musician played something slow and mournful near the square's edge, coins scattered thin in the open case at his feet, and a small crowd had gathered to listen despite the evening chill. None of them so much as glanced in Sariel's direction. None of them had any reason to suspect that the war that might determine their afterlives continued beyond their perception. It might also decide whether reality itself endured.

Sariel had walked among mortals often enough, over centuries of similar assignments, that their ignorance no longer struck him as remarkable. It struck him, tonight, as something closer to mercy. Whatever else this war cost, it had at least spared these particular strangers the burden of knowing what stood behind the sky. He passed the musician's case without dropping a coin into it, a small gesture that would have meant nothing to the man and cost Sariel nothing at all to make, and found himself, briefly, wondering why he had not made it anyway.

## The University of Navarion

The scent thickened as he crossed onto the university grounds, ivy-covered brick buildings rising around him beneath ancient oaks that had clearly stood there longer than the school itself. Lamplight spilled warm from windows across the quad, and somewhere nearby an old bell tower marked the hour with a sound that felt, for a moment, almost like the tolling bells of the Bastion. Students moved between buildings in loose, unhurried clusters, absorbed in conversations Sariel could not begin to follow — arguments over coursework, plans for the evening, the small, urgent dramas of lives that would end, by mortal reckoning, impossibly soon.

He paused despite himself, struck by the sheer, disorganized vitality of it all. Their concerns lacked the absolute consequences of Sariel's duty, yet their intensity was impossible to dismiss. A small group of students passed close enough that he caught fragments of their argument — something about a professor's unreasonable deadline, delivered with more outrage than the subject seemed to warrant, and one of them laughing despite the complaint. Something old and tired stirred in Sariel, briefly, a longing for some version of existence unshaped by duty. He did not examine it. He had learned that particular feeling did not improve under examination, and there was still a demon to find tonight.

## Readings No One Understood Yet

The instrument in the basement archive had been humming at an unusual frequency for three days running, and Professor Calder had stopped trying to explain it to anyone who asked. The archive itself was little more than a small, converted storage room, tucked away beneath the main library where a previous generation of the physics department had installed equipment nobody currently on staff fully understood, and Calder had inherited responsibility for it the way one inherits an elderly relative's furniture — without being consulted, and without any real choice in the matter.

He was not, by his own admission, the sort of scholar equipped to interpret it properly — his specialty was pre-modern architecture, not whatever branch of physics this particular device had originally been built to measure, decades before his tenure, by a predecessor whose notes had long since gone missing. He only knew how to read the needle, and the needle had been climbing steadily since Tuesday, in patterns that did not match anything in the maintenance log left behind by the device's original custodian, a log itself half-illegible with age and written, apparently, in a private shorthand no one currently on staff could fully decipher.

He made a note of it, as he had every day this week, and filed the note away in a drawer that already held several unanswered questions about this building — a door that occasionally would not stay locked, a section of the archive that ran colder than the rest of the basement for no reason the maintenance staff could identify. Someone with the right expertise would eventually take an interest, he told himself. It was not, he was fairly certain, anything that concerned him directly.

He straightened the stack of unfiled paperwork on the desk beside the instrument, a habit he kept more for his own peace of mind than for any real necessity, and glanced once more at the needle before switching off the lamp for the night, half-expecting, absurdly, that it might have settled back to normal in the time it took him to gather his coat. It had not. He closed the drawer, locked the door out of habit rather than any real sense of urgency, and climbed the basement stairs into a campus already quiet for the evening, going home telling himself, as he had every night this week, that it would surely resolve itself by morning.

## A Fleeting Encounter

Sariel found his target's trail thinning near the university library, and it was there, crossing the quad in the day's fading light, that he passed the young man sitting alone on a low stone wall.

He nearly did not register him at all — an ordinary-looking man in a worn jacket, seeming to brood over something Sariel could not identify, one more forgettable face among the campus's constant traffic, sitting apart from the students who passed him without a second glance. A pair of students crossed near the wall, and one called out a careless greeting — "Hey, Arin" — that went unanswered. Then the air around him changed, and Sariel stopped walking entirely.

It was not heat and it was not cold, though it resembled both. It felt like standing before a verdict already fully rendered, ancient and absolute, pressing against him from a source he could not locate. He had stood before judges of considerable rank in his long service, had felt the particular weight of a superior's disapproval more times than he cared to count during his early centuries, and none of it had prepared him for whatever this was.

A memory surfaced, unbidden and unwelcome — a minor infraction he had failed to report, millennia ago, involving a fellow angel and an oversight so small he had not thought of it in centuries. He could see it now with an unnatural, itemized clarity: the exact hour, the exact words he had chosen not to say, the exact silence that had followed his decision to let the matter pass unremarked. It rose in him now with an urgency that made him want to kneel where he stood and confess it to whoever might be listening, however irrational the impulse.

He did not kneel. He made himself keep walking, every step deliberate, his hand tightening around Penumbra's shaft though there was nothing here to fight. He did not look back at the young man until he had put considerable distance between them, and even then he allowed himself only a single glance, quick and controlled. The young man had not moved. He sat exactly as he had before, unaware, as far as Sariel could tell, that anything unusual had passed near him at all.

Nothing about what Sariel had just felt matched anything in his long experience of demons, angels, or the countless shades of corrupted souls he had hunted across a hundred cities. He had no name for it. He was not sure he wanted one.

## The Hunt Resumes

He found his actual target in the library's lower stacks not long after, the demon's borrowed host slumped over a desk between two towers of unread textbooks, showing no sign that he knew a war was being waged for the seat of his own soul. Sariel approached without hesitation, grateful, in a distant and unexamined way, for a task simple enough to steady him after what had just passed on the quad. His touch was quick and silent, Penumbra's point finding the corrupted essence beneath the human shell and unmaking it cleanly, without spectacle. The host slumped further into an ordinary, dreamless sleep. If this cleansing followed the pattern Sariel had seen a hundred times before, the student would wake sometime in the morning with no memory of having carried anything but himself.

It should have felt like the end of the matter. It had, every other time, in every other city — a clean kill, a cleared assignment, nothing left to occupy him but the walk home. Sariel turned to leave the library and found his gaze drawn back, against his own intention, toward the quad and the empty stretch of wall where the young man had been sitting. He was already gone now. The unease he had left behind was not.

Sariel told himself, as he left the campus, that it had been some anomaly of the place itself — old buildings sometimes held old resonances, and universities built over centuries occasionally sat atop stranger foundations than their architects had ever documented. It was, he reasoned, the sort of place where a leyline might run close enough to the surface to unsettle even a seasoned hunter's instincts. He told himself this several times, in fact, on the long walk back to the place he would open a way home, reviewing the explanation as critically as he would a suspect report. He did not entirely believe it by the time he arrived.

## The Report, and What It Omitted

He filed his official report before the night was through, as protocol required: target located, confirmed, neutralized without incident, host unharmed. Clean, efficient, exactly the kind of report Camael expected from him and exactly the kind he had filed a hundred times before, the words coming easily even as his mind kept circling back to the quad.

He did not mention the young man on the wall. He told himself this omission was reasonable — an unverified sensation was not evidence, and evidence was the only thing worth reporting to a Commander who had no patience for feelings without shape. Camael had built his reputation, across the long war, on demanding facts over hunches, and Sariel had, until tonight, never once had cause to withhold anything from him. That reasoning held together well enough as he wrote it down.

It held together less well afterward, alone, turning the memory over one more time before he let himself rest. He filed the report as written, unchanged, and told no one — not Camael, not anyone — what he had actually felt standing near that wall. It was, he told himself, simply not relevant yet. He did not examine too closely why he had already decided there might come a time when it was, or why some part of him had already begun cataloguing what little he knew of the young man — a single overheard name, Arin, nothing more — and the plain fact of his face, which Sariel found, lying in the dark afterward, he could recall without difficulty at all.
