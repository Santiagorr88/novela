# B3C44.6 – The Door No One Sealed

Valefar kept his own accounts of the fractured court the way he kept everything — indexed, cross-referenced, locked behind seals only he held the keys to — and the entry he kept returning to, three nights running, was the one he trusted least: *Vepar declines to march.*

He had the report from four separate couriers by now, each one confirming the same plain fact in slightly different language. Foras had marched on Belial's empty fortress a fourth time that week and failed a fourth time, his own soldiers watching him do it rather than following him in. Vepar had not marched at all. Vepar had stayed with his fleet, at the staging ground he'd held for a season now alongside a Host commander no demon in this court's long memory had ever trusted, and had let the throne sit empty rather than reach for it. Every other captain in Hell's current disarray read that as caution, or as a marshal too occupied with his tides to notice an opportunity. Valefar read it the way he read every lock left conspicuously unattended: not as absence, but as an opening someone had simply not yet bothered to walk through.

*Oblivion* hung at his side the way it always did, cool and patient, and he turned the key over once in his fingers, an old habit that helped him think the way other men paced. A marshal who had spent a season building trust with the Host was a marshal who had, whether he understood it that way or not, left a door standing open at the exact seam where Hell's oldest enemy could someday walk through it. Nothing opened without a price. He had built his entire long service on that single fact, had spent centuries making certain no gate under his own authority ever forgot it. He found it far harder to accept that the gate in question here belonged to his own commander, and that no one above Valefar's own rank appeared to have thought yet to ask what the door was actually costing them to leave unwatched.

---

Vine smelled the unrest on his own pack before he heard any report of it, in the loose camp further along the shared line where Vepar's beasts had been kept fed and idle for longer than any of them had patience for.

The largest of them, a scarred thing with too many legs and a temper Vine had spent years shaping rather than breaking, paced the length of its tether in a tight, restless circuit, snapping at nothing, working itself toward a fight that wasn't coming. Vine understood the mood better than he understood most things he was asked to explain in words. He lifted *Ferox* to his lips and played three low notes, not a command exactly, closer to an answer to a question the creature hadn't finished asking — and watched the pacing slow, the muscle along its flank settle by degrees, though the restlessness itself didn't leave. It simply went where restlessness always went when it had nowhere honest to spend itself. Underneath the skin, waiting.

He knew the feeling from the inside well enough to recognize it without needing a report to name it for him. A season of holding position along a peace line was not a season a creature built for the hunt was meant to survive gracefully, and Vine did not consider himself, on his better days, meaningfully different from the animals he commanded. He had built packs, evolved them, thrown them at enemies who deserved far worse than what his creatures gave them, and had done none of it, not once, standing still. He set *Ferox* back against his belt and looked out toward the dark line where the celestial camp's watch-fires burned, close enough now, after a season of this arrangement, that he'd half stopped noticing they were there at all. He found that fact more unsettling than any report Valefar could have brought him. A predator that stopped noticing its prey wasn't practicing patience. It was going soft in a way that would eventually cost it something real.

---

Valefar found him there, tether creaking, the scarred beast finally settling into something like sleep. "You feel it too," Valefar said, without preamble, in what Vine had come to read as the economy of a man who considered small talk a wasted key on a lock that didn't need one. "The whole camp's grown too quiet to trust."

"I feel a pack that hasn't fought in a season," Vine said. "Not the same complaint as yours."

"Isn't it." Valefar let *Oblivion* turn once more in his grip, a habit Vine had learned, across their shared season on this line, meant the captain was already several moves further into a thought than his voice currently let on. "Vepar's built something with that Host commander nobody above him has bothered to audit properly. I don't care whether it's holding. I care that no one's checked what it would cost us the day it stopped."

"You think he's been played."

"I think a door left open long enough stops looking like a door and starts looking like a wall, to anyone who's stopped checking whether it's locked." Valefar's voice carried none of the paranoia the wider court liked to joke about behind his back — only the flat, exact tone of a man reciting an inventory. "I don't trust Lucifer with my own seals, Vine. I've never once trusted him with anything I couldn't personally verify twice. I'm not about to extend a courtesy to an angel that I've withheld from my own throne my entire career, simply because our marshal's grown fond of the man's tactics."

"Then say it to Vepar instead of to me." Vine rose, the beast's tether slack now in his hand, and found, watching Valefar's carefully controlled stillness, something almost like sympathy for a kind of restlessness he didn't personally share but recognized well enough by shape. "I'll come. Not for your locks — for my own ears."

---

Vepar received them both at the staging ground's edge, Marea resting easy across one forearm, his attention only half turning from the anchored fleet behind him. "Captain," he said to Valefar, and then, with the faintest shift of acknowledgment, "and the animal you brought with you as backup. I assume this isn't a courtesy visit."

"It's an audit," Valefar said. "Overdue, in my judgment. You've spent a season building something with a Host commander that no one under your own command has been permitted to examine for its actual weaknesses. I don't say that as an accusation, Marshal. I say it as a captain whose entire domain is the difference between a door that's locked and a door that only looks locked from a comfortable distance."

Vepar studied him a long moment, the same unhurried study he gave a stretch of open water before deciding whether it was worth crossing. "You think Camael's men mean to walk through me the day I stop watching."

"I think you've never once needed to convince me that Camael himself means it. I think it only takes one soldier on either side who wants a war more than his own commander does, and an arrangement like yours has considerably more of those soldiers standing near it than you've apparently accounted for." Valefar held the marshal's gaze without flinching from it, which Vine judged, watching him, to be rarer in this court than the captain likely realized. "I'm not asking you to end it. I'm telling you no one's built the seal that keeps it from being used against you, and I'd rather be the one who builds it than the one who explains, afterward, why nobody thought to."

Vepar was quiet a moment, and Vine watched something settle behind his translucent stillness that read less like offense than like a man recalculating how much of his own certainty he still wanted to defend against an argument that had, against his expectation, actually landed somewhere useful. "Then audit it," Vepar said finally. "Heaven's own side of it least of all — Camael tells anyone who asks him that there's no border left standing worth a formal watch, and no one above him has troubled to check whether the men actually holding it agree. Every point of contact along the shared line, every soldier on either side with access to more than his own orders. Report the gaps to me directly, not to the wider court, and not to Foras's remaining faithful, who'd use it for considerably worse purposes than sealing it. If you find nothing, you'll have wasted a season's patience proving a season's suspicion wrong. I judge that an acceptable cost, against the alternative of you carrying it around unspoken and unresolved."

"And me," Vine said, before the silence could close back over the arrangement. "I didn't come to watch him work a lock."

"No," Vepar agreed, "you came because standing still is starting to cost you something, and you're honest enough not to pretend otherwise." He considered Vine a moment, the same unhurried weighing. "There's a stretch of broken ground two days south, feral packs still running loose out of whatever Thamorak's collapse scattered across that region before the balance settled. Neither side's claimed responsibility for clearing it, because neither side currently has a soldier to spare who isn't standing this line. You do. Take what you need. Bring back what's worth keeping, and put down what isn't." His attention had already begun drifting back toward the fleet, the matter, as far as he was concerned, settled. "Acceptable. For now."

---

Valefar's audit took him the better part of three days, moving the length of the shared perimeter with the same patient thoroughness he gave any lock he hadn't yet fully trusted, and he found, by the second afternoon, precisely the kind of gap he'd expected to find and precisely not the kind he'd feared. A supply route on the camp's eastern edge ran undefended for a stretch of nearly a mile, warded on both sides but not across the seam where the two wardings met, the sort of oversight that came not from betrayal but from two allied quartermasters each assuming the other had already handled it. He sealed it himself, methodically, *Oblivion* closing the gap the way it closed anything, and found the work satisfying in the narrow, exact way most of his work satisfied him — not because the danger had been large, but because it had been real, and now it wasn't.

He did not find evidence of betrayal. He found, instead, the plainer and less comfortable truth that an alliance built on two commanders' stubborn competence, rather than on any court's careful design, was always going to carry gaps like this one — not treachery, only the ordinary carelessness of an arrangement nobody above either marshal had ever actually built to last. He filed the finding exactly as instructed, to Vepar alone, and kept for himself the smaller, harder-to-file fact that the audit had not made him trust the arrangement. It had only made him trust that someone was finally watching the parts of it that mattered — and made him wonder, walking the last stretch of the line back toward the staging ground, how many other seams like it he simply hadn't found yet.

---

Vine's stretch of broken ground gave him far less to think about and a great deal more to do. The largest of the feral packs ran him to ground on the second morning, seven of them circling out of the wreckage of what had once been a supply road, hunger and Thamorak's old chaos still working through them in equal measure. He didn't reach for Ferox first. He let them come, took the leader's charge low and hard enough to break its foreleg under him, and only lifted the flute once the rest had scattered back to a wary ring around the two of them, unsure which of the two predators in front of them was actually in charge. The notes he played then weren't a mercy. They were an argument, patient and absolute, and he felt the pack's resistance give way under it the same way he'd felt every pack give way eventually — not broken, exactly, just finally willing to listen to the larger animal in the clearing. He spent two days doing the one thing peacetime had denied him for a season — moving, deciding, killing where killing was owed and claiming where claiming was worth the trouble. He brought back eleven creatures worth keeping, reshaped already by *Ferox*'s patient work into something closer to disciplined than they'd been running wild, and left the rest of the stretch quieter than he'd found it.

It cost him nothing he minded losing. It answered nothing about the wider arrangement he'd questioned Vepar over. He found, riding back toward the staging ground with the new pack falling into loose formation behind him, that he minded that considerably less than Valefar likely would — right up until he thought about how long it would be before Vepar found him another stretch of ground worth the trip.

---

They met again at the perimeter's edge before either reported back to Vepar, Valefar's ledger closed under one arm, Vine's new pack settling restlessly at his heel. "You found something," Vine said. It wasn't a question.

"A gap. Ordinary carelessness, not treachery." Valefar's voice carried none of the relief Vine had half expected from him. "I sealed it. I'll keep looking, because one ordinary gap doesn't prove there isn't a second one built on purpose, underneath it, by someone considerably more patient than the quartermasters who left the first one open." He looked toward the celestial watch-fires, still burning steady in the dark. "I don't trust it, Vine. I don't think I'm built to. I trust, now, that the marshal's at least letting someone watch it properly, which is more than I trusted a week ago."

"I don't care if it's trusted." Vine let the pack settle fully before he spoke again, the words coming easier now that his hands had had something honest to do for two days running. "I care if I've got something to hunt. He gave me that. Rest of it, I'll worry about when it bites me — same as you."

Neither of them resolved the disagreement between them, and neither, Vine judged, watching Valefar turn back toward the camp's lamplit rows of tents, particularly expected to.

---

Vepar received both reports before the night was through, standing at the edge of his own still pool with Marea resting loose across one forearm, and found in neither of them anything that surprised him — a gap sealed, a stretch of wasted ground put back to some use, two captains who had gone looking for opposite reassurances and come back with neither, exactly the outcome he'd have predicted had either of them asked him to before they left. Word had reached him already, secondhand, of Foras's fourth failed march on the empty fortress that same week, the soldiers watching their own commander fail to claim what everyone above him assumed he'd wanted most. Vepar found he felt no particular satisfaction in the comparison. A captain testing his own commander's weakness was, he judged, watching the pool settle back into its patient stillness, exactly the arithmetic every court in Hell ran on sooner or later. The only real difference between his own captains and Foras's scattering ones was that his had come to him directly, and asked, rather than simply moving first and calculating the cost of being caught afterward.

He did not know whether the arrangement he'd built with Camael would survive whatever came next for either court. He knew only that it had survived one captain's paranoia and another's restlessness, both of them handled, both of them still standing under his command by morning, which was, in the particular arithmetic of a war he'd never once expected to end in anything resembling peace, as close to a victory as he currently required.
