# B1V01 – What Should Not Have Burned

## Ground Worth Nothing to Anyone

Krass had held this stretch of neutral plane for the better part of a decade, long enough that the crystalline groves and quiet twilight of it had stopped feeling like an assignment and started feeling like something closer to a home he had not asked for but had come to keep anyway. His arms bore the marks of every long season he had spent here, ink shifting slow and constant beneath his skin the way it always had since the day he first bonded with it.

It served Hell nothing, strategically. No resources worth extracting, no souls worth harvesting in any real number, nothing on any ledger that would have justified a single soldier's presence here beyond the standing order that had planted his squad on this ground years ago and never bothered to revoke it since. He had stopped questioning the order a long time back. He simply held the line he had been given, day after quiet day, tattoos shifting faintly along his forearms with whatever mood happened to be passing through him, his bow resting unstrung across his back more often than not, Empath's arrows quiet in their quiver beside it.

The groves themselves chimed softly at dusk, an ordinary music he had come to recognize the way a man recognizes his own street after enough years of walking it. He had watched entire seasons pass here without a single skirmish worth reporting up the chain, watched squads rotate in and out around him while he stayed, and in all that quiet time had not needed Empath's arrows for anything darker than talking a homesick soldier through a bad night.

## What the Boy Admits

The soldier assigned to his flank tonight had never stood a real line before, a fact obvious in the way his hands would not quite stop moving even when there was nothing yet to do with them. He was young even by the standards of a squad that ran younger than most, freshly assigned from a training company that had apparently not prepared him for the particular quiet of standing watch on a border no one had expected to see real action in years.

"I don't know if I'll be able to hold it," the boy admitted, voice low enough that Krass had to lean in slightly to catch all of it over the wind moving through the groves around them. He did not answer right away with the bow. He answered first the way any commander worth trusting answered a soldier admitting fear like that, before a fight neither of them had chosen. "No one knows until they're holding it," Krass said. "I didn't either, the first time." The boy looked at him like that admission cost something to hear, the way it had cost Krass something true to say it. He said nothing further for a moment, just stood there working his jaw the way soldiers did when they were trying to decide whether the words had actually helped or only sounded like they should have, eyes still fixed somewhere past Krass's shoulder on the dark tree line beyond. Only then did Krass reach for his bow, nocking one of Empath's arrows, one he had no intention of loosing at any enemy, and let it graze close enough across the boy's shoulder to work without wounding him, the tattoos along his own forearm flaring briefly brighter as the gift left the string.

What it carried into him was not any dulling of the fear itself — in all Krass's long years carrying Empath, the gift had never offered anyone that particular mercy. What it gave the boy instead was love, sudden and full-force: the boy's own quiet affection for the soldiers standing beside him, the ones he had trained with these past months, drawn up out of wherever he had been keeping it modestly tucked away and set burning bright enough that he could not mistake it for anything smaller. Fear, once it had to share the space with something that large, tended to matter less than the plainer fear of failing people he now felt, with sudden and total clarity, that he loved. The boy's shoulders steadied, not because the fear had left him, but because something else had finally arrived to stand next to it. He looked up at Krass with an expression that had shifted from raw dread into something closer to resolve, still afraid, plainly, but no longer alone with it in quite the same way.

"Thank you," the boy said quietly, and Krass only nodded, already turning his attention back toward the tree line where the wrongness had first begun to gather.

## What Camael Already Learned

The first sign of Miguel's force reached them not as noise but as a wrongness in the air itself, a silence too total for any approach this size should have managed. The crystalline groves that had chimed so gently every dusk for as long as Krass had held this ground fell quiet all at once, every note dying mid-tone as though something had reached out and simply stopped them. Krass had commanded soldiers long enough to recognize the shape of an actual battle before it began — the ragged edges, the uncertainty, the small chaos that always crept into even the most disciplined assault.

This carried none of that. The formation advancing toward his line moved with a precision that left no room for anything resembling a fight, each angel's approach so exactly coordinated with the next that Krass understood, watching it unfold, that he was not looking at a battle at all. No flanking maneuvers, no probing strikes to test the strength of the line before committing fully to it, no pause of any kind to reconsider the ground ahead — only a single, unbroken wall of light advancing at a pace that did not waver or hesitate, already certain of an outcome it apparently saw no need to question. He had heard, secondhand, some fragment of a story about a demon officer elsewhere on this front who had described a similar assault the same way — not a fight, an execution, delivered by soldiers who had stopped needing to consider whether what stood in front of them deserved to be considered a fight in the first place. He had not fully believed the story when it first reached him, had assumed some exaggeration in the retelling the way most battlefield stories picked up exaggeration on their way from one squad to the next, each teller adding a little more weight to the fear it was meant to carry. Krass recognized the description now from the inside, watching his own line about to receive exactly what that other officer had already survived once, and found he owed that story a great deal more credit than he had originally given it.

## The Last Use of the Gift

He knew, watching the formation close the remaining distance, that nothing his squad did in the next several minutes was going to change the shape of what was coming. He looked once down the line at the faces braced on either side of him, most of them faces he had known for years, a few he had known for only weeks, and understood that whatever else this night became, it would not become a night any of them walked away from telling stories about later. He had led plenty of nights before this one that ended in exactly that kind of story. He did not expect this to be one of them. He raised his bow anyway, Empath's last arrow already nocked, and turned it not toward the approaching light but toward his own soldiers standing braced along the line beside him.

He loosed it wide, a single shot angled to graze as many of them as the arc would allow, the arrow catching the last of the dying light as it crossed the space between him and the line. He felt the gift do its work across the whole line at once rather than one soldier at a time the way he usually delivered it, a strain on the gift itself he had not attempted before and would not survive long enough to attempt twice. Not fear. Not even courage, exactly. He gave each of them, in the last moments he had any real say over what they felt, something that mattered more to him than either — their own love for the soldiers standing beside them, drawn up and set burning as bright as he could manage, a feeling most of them had carried quietly for years without ever once saying it aloud. If this was the last thing any of them was going to feel, Krass intended it to be that, and nothing colder. He felt his own tattoos flare across his arms with the effort of it, brighter than he had ever felt them burn before, and let the sensation pass through him without trying to hold onto whatever it was actually costing him to give away this much love at once.

## What Falls First

The boy went down before the true fighting even reached him properly, caught by a blow meant for someone else entirely, dropping to the ground directly in Krass's line of sight before Krass had time to reach for a single thing that might have stopped it. He fell without a sound, or without any sound Krass could actually hear over everything else already breaking loose around them, and lay still in a way that left no real room for hoping otherwise.

It was not rage that moved through Krass, watching that particular soldier fall before any of the others. Rage was loud, and what he felt instead arrived cold and exact, a clarity he had not expected grief to carry with it. He had lost soldiers before, plenty of them, across centuries of holding lines exactly like this one, and every one of those losses had come wrapped in some version of the same hot, familiar anger. This one did not. This was not some abstract injustice he could file away as the ordinary cost of a war neither side had started kindly. This had a face he had spoken to an hour ago, a voice that had admitted its own fear to him in words plain enough that Krass still heard them clearly even now — I don't know if I'll be able to hold it, and Krass had told him no one ever did until they were already holding it, and the boy had believed him, and had held it anyway, right up until the exact moment holding it stopped mattering at all. He understood, kneeling beside a body that would not answer him, exactly what this was. It had a name. It had cost something specific and irreplaceable, and no accounting of strategic necessity anywhere in Hell's ledgers was ever going to balance that particular loss back out. He did not have time to kneel there long. The line was already breaking further down its length, soldiers falling in twos and threes at a pace that gave no one any room to stop and mourn properly, and whatever grief he owed this particular death would have to wait for a moment this battle was never going to grant him. He rose anyway, unsteady, and turned back toward whatever remained of the fight because there was nothing else left for him to do with the time that was left.

## What He Carries Out

The blow that took Krass down himself came only moments later, faster than he had any real chance to prepare for, and he felt it arrive with a strange, sudden calm, his mind already a pace behind his body, watching the rest of it happen from a small distance away. There was no pain worth the name, only a sudden loss of the ground beneath him, the crystalline chime of the grove reaching his ears one final time as he went down hard onto the cold stone beneath it.

He did not spend his last conscious moment hating the angels advancing over his fallen line, precise and untouched by whatever they had just finished doing here, their formation still as coordinated now as it had been at the very start of the assault, as though nothing that had just happened had cost them anything at all to accomplish. He spent it instead on the same cold, exact recognition that had taken hold of him the instant the boy fell — a specific injustice, with a face and a name, that no war either side claimed to be fighting had ever actually required. Ground worth nothing to anyone had cost him a soldier who had trusted his word an hour before he needed it most, and no strategic ledger anywhere in Hell or Heaven had a column wide enough to hold that particular debt. That clarity did not fade as the rest of the world around him did, the way the sound of the fighting and the fading chime of the groves eventually did. It was, in the end, the last thing Krass carried with him out of the only ground he had ever truly called his — not the years of quiet watches, not the centuries of small kindnesses handed out one arrow at a time, only that one specific injustice, held clear and unforgiven until it, too, finally went dark.
