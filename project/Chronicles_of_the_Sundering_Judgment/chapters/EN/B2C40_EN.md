# B2C40 – The Voices Gather

The entrance had no gate, no marker, nothing a mortal eye would have troubled itself to notice. Thaeriel found it anyway, three days after the cliff, at the bottom of a ravine no map he'd ever consulted had bothered naming — a fissure in old rock, narrow enough that a careless man might have walked past it a dozen times without once suspecting it led anywhere at all. He stood at its mouth a long moment, the cold air rising from it carrying none of the ordinary smell of stone and earth he'd have expected from any cave he'd ever entered in a decade of careful, dangerous work.

He was not afraid, and hadn't been for some while now. He noted the absence of fear the way he'd once noted the absence of a wound after a fight that should have left him with one, a fact worth registering rather than celebrating. He had spent three days walking south with nothing but his own certainty for company, and that certainty had not once wavered, not through the cold nights or the long silent stretches of road or the last hard climb down into this ravine. He had slept twice under open sky, both nights without a fire, watching stars he'd never bothered learning the names of and not minding the gap in his own education for once. He had eaten little, and hadn't especially cared. He knew what waited for him below, or near enough to know it. He knew what he intended to do once he found it.

The ravine itself had taken the better part of a day to reach on foot, a long, switchbacking descent down loose scree that had cost him one torn boot sole and considerable patience. He'd passed no one on the way down, not even the ordinary hikers a stretch of wilderness this size should have produced somewhere along its length. That absence had struck him as its own kind of confirmation, a place the world quietly steered its ordinary travelers away from without ever explaining why.

He set one hand against the fissure's rock edge, feeling its rough cold under his palm, and stepped through without ceremony, the way he'd once walked into a hundred ordinary rooms to finish a hundred ordinary jobs. This job was not ordinary. He understood that plainly enough. He walked into it anyway, unhurried, a man descending toward something that belonged to him by right long before it had ever belonged to the one currently holding it, and found, taking the fissure's first narrow step downward, that three days of walking had settled every last doubt he'd carried into this ravine down to a single, uncomplicated certainty.

Mikel's office held only a single lamp burning by the time the campus outside his window had gone fully dark, its light falling across a map he'd unrolled flat against his desk an hour ago and hadn't yet rolled back up. He had marked six locations on it so far, each one circled in the same careful red ink he used for grading, each one tied to some fragment of a myth that mentioned, however obliquely, a maker who judged without leaving grief behind. A shrine on a coastline he'd never visited. A festival, three centuries dead, that had once honored a nameless smith of laughter. A footnote in a text so old its author's name had been lost twice over, mentioning a sword forged from a star's own amusement.

None of it added up to a destination yet. He didn't mind that as much as he'd expected to. It was simply an honest gap in his research, one more unanswered question to set alongside the others until enough evidence arrived to answer it, exactly the way any real scholarship actually got done.

He circled a seventh location, a stretch of coastline three countries from anywhere he'd ever traveled, and sat back to study the shape the seven marks made together. No pattern yet, three different fragments pointing toward three different centuries with equal, maddening confidence. He logged the discrepancy in the margin rather than letting it frustrate him, and let the rest wait for tomorrow. Tonight he only needed to see the shape of what he didn't yet know, calm rather than desperate, certain that whatever he was searching for wanted, in its own patient way, to eventually be found by someone willing to keep looking.

Gabriel stood before the old star-map with Raphael and Iofiel on either side of him, the chamber otherwise empty at this late hour, its usual traffic of councilors and messengers long since gone quiet for the night. Two points of light held their patient places on the map's slow-turning surface, the same two he'd tracked for weeks now. A third had joined them recently, smaller than the other two, restless in a way neither of them was, a bright and shifting glow that seemed to dance between the other lights rather than hold any fixed position of its own.

"They're close now," Raphael said, studying the third light with open curiosity. "Closer than they've ever been. Should we intervene, now that all three are finally within reach of each other?"

Gabriel shook his head, his eyes not leaving the map. "We've spent this whole time waiting for them to find each other. We're not going to ruin it by showing up right when they finally have." He let the words settle before he continued, quieter now. "They need each other more than they need us tonight. Let them have that first."

Iofiel said nothing. She hadn't spoken since they'd entered the chamber, her attention fixed on the third light with a stillness Gabriel recognized from months of watching her study fragments no one else on this council had ever taken seriously. She didn't look away from it now, not even when Raphael glanced her way, waiting for some comment that never came. Whatever she was thinking, she kept it to herself a while longer, and Gabriel let her, understanding well enough by now that some conclusions took their own time arriving, regardless of how closely a person watched for them.

Raphael studied the third light a moment longer himself, Gabriel thought he saw some private calculation working behind an expression that was usually placid. "I've healed enough soldiers coming back from that stretch of Earth to know something down there has been changing for weeks," he said finally. "I hadn't put a shape to it until tonight. Whatever this third signal is, it isn't like the other two. It doesn't move the way a soul in danger moves." He glanced at Gabriel. "Should I have Sariel keep watching, at least, even if we don't act ourselves?"

"Sariel's watched enough for one season," Gabriel said. "Let him rest. I'll know if the shape of this changes before anyone needs to send a report to tell me."

Milo Ray sat on a low stone wall at the edge of a park he'd visited more times this year than he could easily count, his guitar resting across his knees the way it always rested there, and felt, for the first time in longer than he cared to measure, that the waiting had finally run its course. He had spent so many quiet afternoons on so many benches, feeling two men move through their separate lives without ever telling either of them he was listening. He had told himself, each time, that the moment for something more wasn't here yet. Tonight, sitting with the evening cooling around him, he understood plainly that it finally was.

He opened the battered case at his feet and settled the guitar into it, closing the latches one at a time with a care he rarely bothered giving the instrument on an ordinary evening. He had packed it away like this a thousand nights before, always with the understanding that he'd take it back out again the next afternoon, on the next bench, for another stranger who needed a song more than he needed to know why. This packing felt different. He let himself feel the shape of what was coming the way he felt most things — not as a plan, exactly, but as a note he already knew the sound of before he'd played it.

He rose from the wall, slung the case over one shoulder by its worn strap, and looked up at a sky gone properly dark, stars showing through it in the same patient scatter they always showed. He smiled the particular smile he saved for moments he'd been waiting on a long while, bright and a little wicked, the kind of smile that promised the world was about to get considerably more interesting than it had been an hour ago.

A stray cat wound once around his ankles before wandering off toward the fountain, unbothered by his sudden movement, and Milo watched it go with the same easy patience he gave most uninvited company. He thought, briefly, of every bench he'd sat on this year, every stranger he'd made laugh without telling them why it mattered, every quiet correction he'd made to some small piece of the world that would otherwise have gone on hurting a little longer than it needed to. None of that had been wasted time either, he understood now, any more than the waiting itself had been. It had simply been a different kind of readiness, the kind that didn't look like readiness from the outside.

"Well," he said, to no one in particular and to everyone at once, "about time." He shifted the case's weight, testing it against his shoulder the way a man tests a familiar tool before real work. "Let's get the band back together."

Mikel heard it first as a single note, faint and bright, carried on a wind that had no business reaching his office window from wherever it had actually started. He looked up from the map, certain for a moment that he'd imagined it, and found himself smiling anyway, unable to explain why a stray sound on the evening air had settled something in his chest that six unfinished map markings hadn't managed all night. He didn't know what it meant. He didn't need to, not tonight. It left him with the plain, unreasoning sense that somewhere out there, someone else had heard it too, and that he was not quite as alone in this search as he'd told himself he was an hour ago.

---

Far south, descending a passage that grew colder with every step he took into it, Thaeriel heard something he had no way to explain either — not a note this time, but a fragment of laughter, distant and impossible, arriving from some direction the passage's walls should never have allowed a sound to travel from at all. He stopped walking for the length of one breath, listening for it to come again. It didn't. He continued on anyway, something loosened in his chest that hadn't been loosened a moment before, and found the descent easier for it. He could not have explained why a stray sound this far underground left him with the certainty that he walked toward Hell alone in name only, and did not try to explain it to himself further. He simply let it be true, and kept walking.

---

Above them both, and above the park where Milo Ray still stood with the case slung over one shoulder, a single held note continued to sound, patient and unbroken, in no hurry at all to fade. Two roads ran on in different directions that night, one down into darkness, one lit by a single lamp over an unfinished map. Whatever came next for the three of them would not arrive tomorrow, or the week after. But it was, at last, coming, and for the first time since their separate roads had begun bending toward each other, none of the three walked them entirely alone.
