# B1N03 – What Remains Unnamed

Kurel found Daith in the scriptorium early, before either of them had said a word about the argument that had carried into a second full day without resolution, and set a single folded sheet down on the table between them. "I've drafted a version we can both actually submit," he said. Daith read it through twice before looking up, and found, to his own surprise, that Kurel had transcribed everything — every stanza, every line, the full weight of the hymn rendered exactly as precisely as Daith himself would have demanded, right up until the final stanza. There, in place of the corrupted word and everything around it, Kurel had noted, in a neat editorial hand, a single word of his own: *illegible*. Their own working transcription, the one they had argued over for two days, still sat untouched in its usual place — this was only the fair copy meant for Gabriel's eyes.

"It isn't illegible," Daith said, more observation than accusation at this point, some of the fight gone out of him after two days of the same argument circling itself without resolution. "'Illegible' isn't entirely a lie," Kurel said, meeting his eyes without flinching from the half-truth of it. "Legible to whom is the part we're leaving unspecified." Daith turned the folded sheet over once more, weighing the compromise against everything he still believed about complete records and the people who eventually needed them. He did not approve of it, not fully. Searching for a real objection sharp enough to voice, he found instead only the shape of one — a single word standing in for a line and a half of script neither of them had managed to defend in two days of trying — and underneath the objection he could not quite finish, something closer to relief than he had expected exhaustion to leave behind. He was not ready to call that erosion agreement. He was willing, at least, to call it honest enough to accept a compromise he might have rejected outright on the first day.

"Gabriel will ask why," Daith said finally, setting the sheet back down between them. "He will," Kurel agreed. "And I'll tell him the fragment's condition made full recovery impossible. Which is true, as far as it goes." Daith studied him a moment, weighing the carefully bounded honesty of that phrasing against everything he had spent two days insisting a record owed the people who relied on it. "As far as it goes," he repeated, not quite able to keep a note of dry skepticism out of his own voice. "That's doing a great deal of work for four words, Kurel." Kurel's expression did not change. "It's doing exactly as much work as it needs to," he said. "No more."

The corrupted line itself, before Kurel struck it from their own record, had carried a single fragment that neither of them had managed to translate cleanly in two full days of trying — not a name, nothing so definite as that, only the barest indication of something, some unspecified presence whose referent the grammar did not identify. Every rendering Daith tried against it collapsed the moment he tried to pin the reference down to anyone or anything in particular. Daith had read the fragment a dozen times across the argument, and each time, saying it aloud, felt a faint pressure gather somewhere behind his own sternum — fainter the first few times, sharper by the last, though the syllables themselves refused, every time, to settle into anything he could actually transcribe — gone again the moment he stopped speaking. He had no ready explanation for the sensation and had not mentioned it to Kurel. Kurel had read the line more times than that, and had said, at one point during the worst of yesterday's disagreement, that some lines simply could not be understood yet.

Daith had dismissed the comment then as more of Kurel's particular reverence talking. He found himself less certain that Kurel's reading of the situation had been wrong, looking at the redacted stanza and the careful, deliberate blank space where an explanation should have gone.

He tried, briefly, to imagine what kind of answer might have satisfied him two days ago, before any of this had started — a clean translation, a defensible record, nothing left ambiguous for some future reader to mistake for a settled conclusion. He could still imagine wanting that. He found he could no longer imagine it being the right thing to want, not for this particular fragment, not after three separate attempts had failed to produce anything either of them could honestly call a translation. Daith began to suspect, watching the blank space where it had been, that Kurel's instinct had never actually been about protecting the hymn from scrutiny at all. It looked, from where Daith now stood, more like an attempt to avoid handing some future reader an answer neither of them could honestly justify yet.

That thought unsettled him more than the argument itself had. Daith had spent his entire career believing that thoroughness was, in every case, the safer choice — that an unresolved gap in the record could create problems for later readers. He was not prepared to abandon that belief wholesale. He was prepared, for the first time in two days, to admit it might not hold universally, and that admission cost him more than any argument Kurel had made aloud.

Their own working transcription still needed attending to before either of them could call this settled, and Kurel did that marking himself rather than letting Daith log it as a simple correction. He drew the archive's own copy toward himself, taking up his pen with a care that looked, to Daith's eye, almost ceremonial — not the brisk efficiency either of them brought to ordinary corrections, but something slower, more deliberate, as though the act of striking the passage from their own record required its own kind of respect. He drew a single line through the corrupted words, once, without hesitation once the pen actually touched the page, and set the sheet aside to dry with the same careful attention he had given every reading of it before this one.

"You could have let me mark it," Daith said, watching him. "I know," Kurel said. "I wanted it to be mine." He offered nothing further by way of explanation, and Daith, for once, did not press him for one.

Daith watched him set the pen down afterward and study the completed page a moment longer than the correction alone required, his expression unreadable, as though some private accounting were taking place behind it that Daith had no way to follow from the outside. It occurred to him, watching, that this was probably the closest either of them would ever come to a kind of small ceremony for a word neither of them had managed to understand — not a funeral, not quite, but something adjacent to one, marked by a man who Daith now suspected took the responsibility of it more seriously than he had initially given him credit for.

"Does it bother you," Daith asked, "leaving it like this? Unfinished?" Kurel considered the question longer than Daith expected before answering. "Less than finishing it wrong would," he said. "I've seen names forced into meanings they didn't actually carry, just because someone needed an answer before the available evidence could support one. I'd rather carry the discomfort of not knowing than hand someone a certainty I manufactured myself." Daith had no immediate response to that, and did not attempt to manufacture one.

Daith carried the finished translation to Iofiel's chambers himself, Kurel beside him, and watched her read it through in the particular unhurried way she read anything that mattered enough to warrant her full attention. Gabriel stood near the window, arms crossed, waiting for her assessment rather than reaching for the pages himself. Daith had rehearsed, on the walk over, several versions of how he might answer if Iofiel pressed the question directly, none of which had felt entirely comfortable to imagine saying aloud. He had settled, in the end, on saying as little as the moment allowed and letting Kurel carry whatever weight the answer actually required, since the redaction had been Kurel's choice to make in the first place.

"The final stanza," Iofiel said eventually, tapping the redacted line. "You're certain this is genuinely illegible, and not simply difficult?" Daith felt Kurel go very still beside him, though neither of them answered immediately.

"The fragment's condition made full recovery impossible," Kurel said finally, choosing each word with visible care. It was not, strictly, false. Daith said nothing to contradict it, and said nothing to confirm it either, letting the silence do whatever work it needed to do. Gabriel studied them both a moment longer than the answer alone seemed to warrant, as though weighing some private calculation of his own, before he finally spoke. "Then we work with what we have," he said. "If something more surfaces later, bring it to me directly." He did not press further, and Iofiel, after a moment's visible hesitation, let the matter rest as well, setting the translation aside among the day's other reports.

Daith watched her hesitation a moment longer than the exchange itself required, wondering whether she suspected more than she had chosen to say aloud. Iofiel had spent longer with the Codex Mortalis than either of them, and it seemed possible to Daith, watching her set the report down with more care than the gesture strictly needed, that she understood exactly how much a redaction like this one could actually be hiding. If she did, she gave no further sign of it, and neither Gabriel nor anything in his own bearing suggested he intended to revisit the question either, at least not today.

"That's it, then," Kurel said quietly, once they had left the chamber and started back down the corridor. "For now," Daith said. "Gabriel isn't the kind of man who forgets a loose thread entirely. He's the kind who files it away until it becomes useful, or urgent, whichever comes first." Kurel considered that a moment. "Then we've bought time, not resolution," he said. "I can live with that," Daith answered. "I wasn't sure I'd be able to say that two days ago."

They walked back to the scriptorium together in a silence that felt, for the first time in two days, less like a standoff and more like something almost companionable. "You still think I was wrong to hold the seal," Kurel said eventually, not quite a question. "I think you were reckless with something we're supposed to be accountable for," Daith said. "I also think, if I'm being honest, that I might have made the same call in your position. I don't love admitting that." The corner of Kurel's mouth lifted, something close to a smile. "I don't need you to love it," he said. "I just needed you to actually consider it, eventually. You did."

Neither of them fully yielded the ground they had defended over the past two days, and neither, walking back into the scriptorium's familiar quiet, particularly needed to anymore. Daith thought he understood now, in a way he had not quite managed before today, why Kurel had been protecting the record from an unjustified conclusion and why he had been willing to fight so hard over a single word. Kurel, for his part, seemed to understand just as clearly why Daith's own instincts had never been simple stubbornness either, whatever heat their disagreement had generated along the way.

They settled back into their usual places at the table without further comment, the earlier tension between them replaced by something quieter and considerably less combative than anything Daith had expected to feel by the end of this particular assignment. He picked up his own pen to begin logging the completed translation into the archive's permanent record, and found himself, for the first time in two days, entirely unbothered by what the record did and did not contain. Some things, he was beginning to accept, simply were not his to finish alone.

The struck-through working transcription went into the archive's deepest records that evening, filed alongside thousands of other fragments the centuries had left imperfect, its final stanza a single ruled line over words for which neither of them had managed to establish a defensible reading, one small unresolved entry among countless others the archive had preserved without assigning them unsupported interpretations. Daith doubted anyone else in the archive would think twice about a struck line on a damaged text; such things were common enough that, as far as he could tell, no one but Daith and Kurel would ever know exactly how much care that particular line had actually required.

It would remain there, Daith supposed, until further evidence made a defensible reading possible — not lost, not destroyed, only unresolved. Someday, perhaps, someone might possess enough evidence and understanding to finish what the two of them had chosen, deliberately, to leave undone. Until then, no conclusion would be entered in the archive.

Daith closed the record drawer himself that evening, the small metal click of it settling into place feeling, for reasons he did not examine too closely, more final than the sound alone should have carried. Whoever eventually opened this particular drawer again, he thought, it would not be him. That would have to be someone else's discovery to make.
