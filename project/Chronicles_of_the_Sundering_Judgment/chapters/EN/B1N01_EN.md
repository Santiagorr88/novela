# B1N01 – What the Choir Won't Sing

Daith had expected another routine translation when Gabriel's summons reached the archive, another fragment of the Codex Mortalis waiting to be catalogued and filed alongside the thousands that had come before it. What Iofiel actually handed him, once he and Kurel had been shown into her scriptorium, was a single sheet of corrupted hymn-script, its edges scorched, its middle stanzas legible only in patches. "We need it translated in full," Iofiel told them both, her own exhaustion plain in the careful way she set the fragment down rather than simply passing it across. "Whatever it says, Gabriel needs the complete text, not a summary. This one matters more than most."

Daith had already begun assessing the fragment's condition before she finished speaking, the ordinary professional habit of a man who had spent centuries reducing chaos like this into something a court of law could actually use. His eyes moved automatically across the scorched margins, cataloguing the extent of the damage the way he catalogued everything — by degree, by category, by what could reasonably be salvaged and what could not. Whatever this hymn actually contained, the shape of the work in front of him looked, at first glance, entirely ordinary.

Kurel, beside him, made no such immediate move. He only looked at the fragment for a long moment, unhurried, the way a man might look at a stranger across a crowded room before deciding whether to speak to them at all. Daith had worked beside him long enough to recognize the particular stillness that came over Kurel before he touched anything he considered significant, though he had never fully understood what separated a significant fragment from an ordinary one in Kurel's own private accounting. Kurel rarely explained it. He simply knew, or seemed to, and the archive had learned over the centuries to trust that instinct even when it slowed the work considerably.

"You already don't like it," Daith said, more observation than question. Kurel did not answer immediately, his gaze still fixed on the scorched parchment. "I don't know what it is yet," he said finally. "That's different from not liking it. Give me the afternoon before you ask me again."

Daith let the remark go, unwilling to spend the first minutes of a new assignment arguing about a feeling Kurel had not even bothered to justify yet. He had learned, across their years working the same archive, that pressing Kurel before he was ready rarely produced anything worth the friction it cost. Better, in his experience, to let the man reach whatever conclusion he was reaching in his own time, and address it directly once he actually had something concrete to say about it.

They worked side by side once Iofiel left them to it, and within the first hour Daith understood exactly how differently they intended to approach the same page. He transcribed letter by letter, mechanical and precise, cross-referencing each surviving character against known liturgical script the moment it appeared legible, building the translation outward from certainty toward the gaps rather than the reverse. Kurel did nothing of the sort. He read the hymn aloud instead, low and unhurried, several times over, before he ever let his fingers touch the parchment itself, as though the words needed to be heard before they could safely be handled.

"You're wasting time," Daith said eventually, not unkindly, more observation than accusation. "The transcription won't move any faster for being recited first." Kurel did not look up from the page. "It isn't about speed," he said. "A name that resists being spoken doesn't reveal itself to a man in a hurry. I'm not reciting it for my own benefit, Daith. I'm listening for what it does when it's said aloud, and you can't hear that from a transcription table."

Daith let the comment pass without argument, chalking it up to the particular reverence Kurel brought to every fragment that crossed his hands, a habit Daith had learned to tolerate over their years working the same archive even when he privately considered it inefficient. He returned to his own methodical pass at the page, confident the two approaches would converge on the same translation eventually, whatever the difference in how each of them arrived there.

It was not an unfamiliar arrangement between them. They had worked side by side on dozens of fragments over the years, Daith's precision and Kurel's patience settling, more often than not, into a rhythm that produced work neither of them could have managed alone. Daith caught details Kurel's slower method sometimes missed in its deliberate unhurriedness; Kurel caught meaning Daith's mechanical precision sometimes flattened past recognizing. Kurel's holds, in Daith's experience, rarely outlasted a single reading — a pause, a second look, and then the work moved again. Daith saw no particular reason today should behave any differently, whatever small friction had already crept into the first hour of it.

He worked in silence for a while after that, filling in the more legible stretches of the hymn's opening stanzas, cross-referencing each fragment of surviving script against the archive's older liturgical records with the same steady confidence that had carried him through every translation before this one. The work went slowly, as damaged fragments always did, but it went the way work always went — forward, incrementally, toward a completed and defensible record.

The difference stopped being merely stylistic within the hour. Daith reached a clean stretch of legible text near the hymn's middle and moved, without pausing to consult Kurel, to seal the section and mark it complete — standard procedure for any passage that met the archive's threshold for confident translation. Kurel's hand closed over the parchment before Daith's seal touched it. "Not yet," he said, quiet but immovable. "There's a name in there. I want to hear it again before you lock it into the record."

"It's legible, Kurel. That's the entire threshold. I'm not required to wait for your instincts on top of it." Daith kept his voice level, though something in Kurel's refusal had already begun to needle at the edge of his patience. "A name that resists being pronounced isn't something procedure gets to overrule," Kurel said. "You seal it now, and whatever it is stays sealed the way we first understood it — wrongly, if we're wrong. I'd rather be slow than certain and mistaken."

Daith withdrew his hand from the parchment, though not the argument itself. "Show me, then. If there's something here that actually merits holding the seal, show me the passage instead of asking me to take it on faith." Kurel obliged, turning the fragment so the disputed section faced them both, tracing the relevant line with one careful finger. To Daith's eye it looked no different from the dozen surrounding lines he had already sealed without incident, the same careful liturgical hand, the same familiar cadence of praise and invocation that ran through most of the Codex's older hymns. He said as much.

"Read it aloud," Kurel said. "Not scanned. Spoken." Daith obliged more out of exasperation than genuine curiosity, reading the line at his usual brisk pace, and heard nothing in his own voice worth remarking on. "There. Nothing. Whatever you're hearing, Kurel, I'm not hearing it too." Kurel's expression did not change, patient in a way that read, to Daith, as its own quiet form of stubbornness. "Then read it again. Slower this time. I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to actually listen before you decide there's nothing to hear."

Daith read it again, if only to close the argument rather than out of any real expectation the second reading would differ from the first. This time, faint but unmistakable, he heard his own voice catch on the final syllable of the line's last word, some small, involuntary hitch he could not immediately account for. He read it a third time to be certain, and the hitch repeated itself, precisely where it had the second time, though the exact sound of the word itself seemed, absurdly, to shift slightly between attempts. He set the parchment down without comment, some of his earlier certainty visibly shaken, though he was not yet willing to say so aloud.

Kurel watched Daith set the parchment down and read, in the sudden care of the gesture, that whatever certainty had carried his colleague through the last hour had just taken a real blow. He had heard the same shift happen on his very first pass through this stanza, hours ago, though he had said nothing of it then, unwilling to raise an alarm before he understood what exactly he was raising it about. Watching Daith's composure falter against evidence Daith himself had just produced seemed, to Kurel, confirmation enough that his own instinct had not been misplaced.

He had learned, over centuries of handling fragments like this one, to trust a quiet certainty like this before he trusted any transcription built on top of it. Names carried weight in a way ordinary language did not, and a name that resisted a clean, repeatable pronunciation was rarely resisting by accident. He did not yet know what this particular resistance meant. He knew only that Daith's own instincts, usually so reliable in matters of procedure, had nothing built into them for a fragment that behaved this way, and that gap seemed, to Kurel, worth taking seriously in its own right.

"The past does not forget," Daith said, recovering his composure quickly, an edge of stubbornness in his voice that Kurel had learned, over their years working together, not to mistake for actual certainty. "Neither do I. We transcribe what's here, entire, or we don't transcribe it at all." Kurel did not release the parchment. "What's here includes a word that corrupts itself every time someone tries to say it out loud. Are you transcribing that 'entire' too, Daith, or pretending you didn't just hear it happen?"

Daith had no immediate answer for that. Kurel pressed on, quieter now, some of the earlier heat gone out of his own voice. "Each name is a key, Daith. Each key, a universe. I'm not refusing to transcribe out of stubbornness. I'm refusing because I don't know what door we're about to open, and neither do you."

Daith's jaw tightened, some private calculation visibly working itself out behind his eyes before he spoke again. Kurel had seen that particular stillness in him before, usually in the moments just ahead of Daith conceding a point he did not want to concede. "You think I don't understand what's at stake in getting this wrong," he said finally, an edge of something almost defensive in it. "I understand it exactly as well as you do, Kurel. I've spent longer in this archive than you have." Kurel let his own expression soften, fractionally, at that. "I never said you didn't understand it," he said. "I said we disagree about what getting it wrong actually looks like. You're afraid of an incomplete record. I'm afraid of a complete one we don't actually understand yet. Those aren't the same fear, even if they both sound like caution from the outside." He watched the distinction land, or seem to, some of the tension easing out of Daith's shoulders even if his expression gave up nothing further.

Daith held his silence a moment longer before answering, ground given without the argument itself being conceded. "Then tell me what we do with a hymn that doesn't even want to be read."

Neither of them had an answer ready. Kurel let the question sit between them on the table alongside the fragment itself, spoken aloud now instead of merely implied, and read in Daith's continued silence something that looked, for once, like the same uncertainty Kurel himself had been carrying since his first pass through the stanza. It was, in its own small way, a rare thing to see in a man who rarely allowed himself to appear uncertain about anything at all. Whatever the hymn actually contained, it had already proven it would not yield to procedure alone, and Kurel suspected neither of their approaches, alone, was going to resolve this tonight.

The scriptorium had gone quiet around them at some point during the argument, the ordinary evening business of Iofiel's other assistants long since finished for the day, leaving the two of them alone with a hymn neither one seemed willing to simply abandon. Kurel set the fragment down between them, careful, as though the parchment itself might still be listening. "We have time before Gabriel needs this," he said. "Not much. But some. Enough to be careful instead of merely fast." Daith looked at the scorched edges a long moment before answering, some of his earlier impatience visibly spent. "Then we use it," he said, "however little of it there is." It was not agreement, not exactly. It was the closest thing to it Kurel had gotten from him all evening, and for now, with the light in the scriptorium beginning to fade and the question of what to actually do with an unwilling hymn still entirely unresolved, he was willing to take it. Whatever came next, they would at least be arriving at it together, however far apart their reasons for getting there still ran.
