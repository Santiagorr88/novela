# B1V04 – What Desire Promises

Vual had found him three days ago, and had not approached him once since.

He preferred it this way. Other hunters of his kind struck the moment a trail went warm, eager to close the distance before anyone else could claim it, and Vual had never understood the appeal of rushing something that only improved with patience. He watched Corin from across the garage lot where the man worked, from the far end of the same diner counter twice, from the shadowed side of a street Corin walked down every evening without ever once looking up from his own thoughts. Each observation added a small, specific detail to the portrait Vual was building, and each detail made the eventual approach that much richer. A soul worth claiming, in his experience, was always worth savoring first.

He had never shared Sariel's particular discipline, the cold, silent efficiency that turned every hunt into an equation to be solved as quickly as possible. Vual found that approach almost insulting to the work itself, a failure to appreciate what made a soul interesting in the first place. Corin was interesting. He carried himself like a man quietly convinced something in his own life did not add up, a tension visible in the set of his shoulders whenever he thought no one was watching, in the way he sometimes stopped mid-motion at the garage as though waiting for an explanation that never arrived. Vual had seen that particular tension before, in other reincarnated souls at other decades, and he had learned to recognize it as the earliest sign of a mind beginning to suspect its own foundations. He intended to be there when the suspicion finally broke through.

Other hunters chased volume, closing whatever trail they found as quickly as the work allowed, moving on to the next before the last had even finished paying out. Vual had built his own long reputation on the opposite instinct entirely. He took perhaps a tenth of the souls other seducers of his rank claimed in any given century, and considered every one of them worth ten of the rushed, half-savored conquests his rivals collected instead. Conquest, to Vual's mind, was never the moment of claiming itself — that part barely qualified as an ending at all. The true pleasure lived in the long unfolding that preceded it, watching a soul's own uncertainty do most of the work for him long before Deseum ever needed to move.

He had learned of the trail through the usual quiet arrangements Hell kept for business like this, whispers passed along a chain of informants who traded in precisely the kind of information Vual paid well to receive. A soul lost in Miguel's purge of the neutral plane, a demon named Krass by the account that reached him, had reincarnated somewhere in the mortal world within the narrow window such things allowed. Vual had followed threads thinner than this one before and found nothing at the end of them. This one had led him, within a matter of weeks, directly to a garage on the edge of an unremarkable town and a man who had no idea what he had once been.

He had felt no particular sympathy for Krass on hearing the account of the death itself, an execution dressed up as a battle on a plane that had never mattered strategically to either side. Sympathy had never been the currency Vual traded in. What interested him instead was the shape of the death — sudden, unjust, and cut short before whatever loyalty Krass might once have felt toward Hell had any chance to sour or fade on its own terms. A soul severed that cleanly, that unfairly, tended to carry the wound forward into its next life without ever understanding what the wound actually was. That kind of soul made for the easiest listening of all, already halfway convinced something owed it an explanation before Vual ever said a single word.

He crossed the lot toward Corin on the fourth day, choosing the moment with the same unhurried care he had chosen everything else about this particular hunt — early evening, the garage nearly empty, Corin alone at the counter with a rag in his hand and nowhere urgent to be. Vual made himself easy to notice without making himself alarming, a stranger with an engine problem and enough charm to seem entirely ordinary. "You're the one people say actually listens to what a car's telling him," he said, leaning against the counter with the loose, unbothered posture of a man who had nowhere better to be. Corin looked up, wary in the mild, reflexive way most strangers earned from him, nothing more. "Depends who's asking," Corin said. Vual smiled, and let the conversation begin exactly where it needed to.

He did not waste the opening on small talk about engines, though he let Corin believe, for a few minutes, that this was exactly what it appeared to be. Vual asked easy questions and listened with genuine attention to the answers, cataloguing every small hesitation Corin let slip without meaning to — the way he underplayed his own competence, the way certain questions about his past drew shorter answers than questions about anything else, the way his eyes moved, just once, toward some middle distance whenever the conversation touched on anything resembling identity. None of it was accidental to Vual's ear. He had spent three days building a portrait of exactly where this man's foundations felt thinnest, and he intended to press there directly rather than waste time on anywhere else.

He shifted the conversation without appearing to shift it at all, the transition smooth enough that Corin would not notice the ground moving beneath him until it had already moved. "You strike me as someone who notices things other people miss," Vual said, watching the words land. "The kind of person who catches a wrongness in a running engine before anyone else in the room even hears it." Corin gave a small, uncertain laugh, the kind that admitted more than it meant to. "I guess," he said. "Or people just don't listen as carefully as they think they do." Vual let the silence that followed do some of his work for him, patient, unhurried, watching Corin's own uncertainty deepen it further without any further push from him at all.

He steered the conversation once more, lightly, toward the strange incident at the bar Corin had mentioned only in passing, framed as idle curiosity rather than the deliberate probing it actually was. "People talk about that night still, from what I hear," he said. "Something about a room full of strangers calming down all at once, no reason anybody could point to." Corin's hand stilled briefly on the rag he had been folding and refolding since the conversation began. "It wasn't anything," he said, too quickly to be entirely convincing. "I don't even remember doing much of anything." Vual noted the quickness of the denial the way he noted everything else, filing it alongside the averted eyes and the shortened answers, one more piece confirming exactly where the man's foundations ran thinnest. He did not press the bar incident further. He had learned what he needed from the asking itself, and a target pressed too early on the exact wound Vual intended to open later tended to grow defensive in ways that only made the later opening harder.

"Something doesn't fit, does it," Vual said finally, warmth in the words and no explicit threat attached to any of it. "Like you're wearing a life that isn't quite yours." Corin's expression shifted before he could stop it, something guarded and defensive surfacing fast. "How would you know that," he said, no longer bothering to disguise it as a question. Vual smiled, the expression carrying nothing but calm certainty. "Because I have the other half of the answer," he said. "You only have to want to hear it."

He let the offer sit there, unadorned, and watched Corin's resistance measure itself against the pull of it. Vual did not press further yet — he had learned, across centuries in this particular trade, that an offer worked better left to do its own convincing than forced along by more words than it needed. What he offered was never phrased as power outright, never anything so crude. He offered certainty instead, the promise of an explanation for a wrongness Corin had clearly been carrying alone for longer than he would admit, an identity solid enough to finally make sense of a life that no longer felt entirely his own. It was, in Vual's long experience, always certainty that closed the distance fastest, far more reliably than fear or flattery ever had. Corin said nothing for a long moment, and Vual watched the nothing itself as carefully as he would have watched any answer.

"I don't need an answer right now," Vual said, softening the offer just enough to make it feel like mercy rather than pressure, another technique long since worn smooth from use. "Most people don't want the truth handed to them all at once anyway. It's easier carried a little at a time." Corin's jaw tightened, some private argument visibly working itself out behind his eyes. "And what happens if I don't want it carried at all," he said, the closest thing to defiance he had managed since the conversation began. Vual only smiled wider at that, unbothered, genuinely pleased by the resistance rather than annoyed by it — a soul that pushed back a little always held its shape better once it finally gave in. "Then you go on wondering," he said simply. "Same as you have been. I'm not in any hurry, Corin. Are you?"

Vual felt Deseum stir at his side before Corin gave him any words at all, the chain responding to something in the man that had already begun, quietly, to reach toward what was being offered. It was not consent exactly, not yet — Vual had learned long ago that full consent was rarely necessary at this stage, only a genuine, unguarded want, and Corin's silence carried exactly that kind of want beneath its hesitation. A single fine thread extended from the chain, invisible to anyone without Vual's own trained eye, and settled itself lightly against whatever in Corin was already leaning toward an answer. Corin shifted where he stood, a small, involuntary motion, one hand rising briefly to his own chest as though something there had shifted with him.

"You felt that," Vual said, not quite a question. Corin's brow creased, uncertain and a little unsettled. "Felt what," he said, though the way he said it suggested he already knew he was lying about not knowing. Vual let it go for now, satisfied with the thread already in place and unwilling to risk overplaying a hand that was, for the moment, playing itself. He watched Corin's hand return, unconsciously, to the same spot on his own chest a second time, a small, telling gesture Corin himself did not seem to register at all. "Think about what I said," he told Corin instead, straightening from the counter with the same unhurried ease he had arrived with. "I'll be around."

Corin did not answer right away, and Vual took the silence as exactly the kind of answer he had come for. There was no need to extract a promise from him tonight, no need to press for anything so definite as agreement. Deseum did not require consent phrased in words. It required only a want left open long enough for the chain to find purchase in it, and Corin, whatever he told himself about the strange stranger and his stranger questions, had left that want open wide enough for Vual's purposes several times over already.

He left the garage without hurrying, the way he had arrived, and did not look back at the door the way a less patient hunter might have, unable to resist confirming the effect of his own work. He did not need to look back. The thread told him everything he needed to know about what he had left behind him, a faint, steady pull settling into place exactly where he had intended it to settle, patient and unremarkable enough that Corin would likely mistake it, for now, for nothing more than his own thoughts circling back to a stranger's unsettling questions.

Vual walked on into the evening without any particular urgency, entirely satisfied with a first contact that had gone exactly the way three days of careful watching had told him it would go. He did not need Corin to understand yet what had actually happened between them, or to notice the thread now settled quietly into him, thin as a held breath and just as easy, for now, to overlook. That understanding, when it finally arrived, would arrive on Vual's schedule and no one else's.

He would return within the week, he decided, giving the thread time enough to settle properly before he asked anything more of it. A soul rushed too soon toward its own unraveling tended to panic and pull away, and Vual had no intention of losing this particular thread to impatience after three days of watching and one careful evening of work. Some prizes were worth losing to haste. This one, he had already decided, was not going to be one of them.
