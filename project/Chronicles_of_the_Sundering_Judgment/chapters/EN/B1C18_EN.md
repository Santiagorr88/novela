# B1C18 – Three Were Forged

## The Labor of Wisdom

Iofiel held the brittle fragments of the *Codex Mortalis* suspended within a sphere of preservative light, each shard of ancient parchment turning slowly in place like something caught mid-thought. Memnón hummed low in her free hand, its ancient-memory working straining against pages that had not been meant for eyes like hers. She had spent three days coaxing the text toward legibility, and the effort had begun, by the second day, to cost her more than she had initially budgeted for.

The scriptorium around her had gone dark hours ago, its usual archivists dismissed for the night, leaving only the sphere's pale glow and the tall shelves of catalogued memory stretching up into shadow on every side. She had chosen this hour deliberately, as she chose most things deliberately — the Codex responded better, in her long experience with older texts, to attention undivided by the ordinary business of a working scriptorium. Whatever the fragments had to say, they would say it more readily to a room that had nothing else asking for its attention.

The Codex resisted her the way old, stubborn things resisted anything that asked them to change. She could feel it in Memnón's resonance each time she pressed further — a text this old, forged before whatever schism first split Heaven from what it had once shared with Hell, had never been written for a Guardian of Divine Memory to read aloud in a scriptorium centuries after it had been written. She pressed anyway, matching her own patience against the parchment's, certain that patience, in the end, would be the deciding factor.

She had catalogued fragments of considerably younger texts across her long service, records that yielded their contents readily enough once properly coaxed. This one fought her at every stage, each small concession costing more of Memnón's working than the last, until she had begun, by the third day, to wonder whether the resistance itself was a kind of message — whether the Codex withheld itself not from carelessness but from some older instinct toward self-preservation, a text that understood exactly how dangerous its own contents might prove to whoever finally read them whole.

Her hands had begun, sometime in the last hour, to show the particular tremor she associated with a working carried past its comfortable limit. She noted it the way she noted any physical cost of her craft, with a scholar's detachment rather than any real alarm, and pressed the sphere's light a fraction brighter to compensate. Whatever this text ultimately cost her, she had already decided, somewhere in the small hours of the first night, that the cost would be worth paying. She had not yet learned enough to know whether that decision had been wise.

## Before It Goes Any Further

Gabriel found her there on the third evening, entering the scriptorium without announcement in a way she had long since stopped finding intrusive. He carried what looked to her like the same particular weight she had noticed settling onto him since Miguel's descent into Solmire's judgment and Belial's return with Lament fully confirmed — a weight that had not visibly lightened in the days since, and showed no sign, from what little she could read of him tonight, of lightening soon.

He crossed the scriptorium's length without his usual measured pace, and she noted the difference without remarking on it. Whatever had brought him here at this hour, it had not left him time for the careful, deliberate stride she associated with him in calmer seasons. He stopped a short distance from the sphere, close enough to see the fragments turning slowly within it but not so close as to disturb the working itself, a courtesy she appreciated more than she said aloud.

He did not ask what she had found. He asked only whether she had eaten, whether she had slept, whether the strain visible in her posture had a limit she intended to respect before it became something worse than fatigue. She answered honestly enough — not yet, not truly, and no — and he did not press further, some old understanding between them holding that she would stop working when the work itself demanded it and not a moment before.

"Send for me the instant it's ready to be read," he said, already turning back toward the door, his voice carrying less than its usual careful cadence, which she took as a sign of exactly how much else was currently demanding it elsewhere. "Not before. I have nothing to offer this except my own exhaustion, and you have enough of that in the room already."

She studied him a moment before he reached the door, weighing whether to press him on whatever else had clearly followed him here tonight. She decided against it, some instinct telling her that what she took to be his burden seemed, for now, his alone to carry, the same way whatever the Codex was about to yield belonged, for now, to her.

He left before she had answered, before the text had yielded anything concrete enough to share, and Iofiel, watching the door close behind him, felt an odd gratitude that he had not stayed to see whatever came next. Whatever this text was about to tell her, she suspected it was hers to carry first, and his to receive only once she had already survived the carrying.

## The First Words

She returned to the sphere once the scriptorium had settled back into its familiar quiet, Memnón's hum steadying into the rhythm she had spent three days building toward. The resistance in the text began, gradually, to give way to something closer to cooperation, one line near the fragment's opening finally stabilizing into legible script, its letters glowing with a faint silver light that had not been there an hour earlier.

*Before the Divine Word gave form to law—*

She read the line twice before allowing herself to fully register what it implied. The phrasing alone unsettled her more than any single fact she had yet extracted from the Codex's stubborn pages. It gestured, plainly and without apology, toward a time before whatever authority currently governed Heaven's own law had existed to govern anything at all. She had spent centuries preserving memory for a living, and had never once encountered a claim quite this foundational, or quite this dangerous to have surfaced in a room with no one but herself to witness it.

She set Memnón's working carefully, resisting the urge to rush the remaining fragments now that one had finally yielded. Whatever else this text intended to reveal, she judged, it would reveal itself on its own terms or not at all — she had learned that lesson three times over across the preceding days, each attempt to hurry the process costing her more of her own strength than the modest gain in speed had ever justified. She steadied her breathing, let Memnón's hum settle back into its patient rhythm, and waited for the next line to surface.

## The Prophecy

The rest of the line surfaced in the minutes that followed, each new word arriving with the same reluctant clarity as the first, until the full sentence finally settled, complete, into the space above the sphere.

*Three were forged before time; one to judge, one to suffer, and one to create.*

The words did not merely appear before her. They impressed themselves directly upon her mind with the force of thunder, each of the three verbs arriving in turn. Judge. Suffer. Create. She had translated prophecy before, had handled fragments of considerably darker implication across her long service to the Codex's slow reconstruction. Nothing in that service had prepared her for how simply, how devastatingly plainly, this particular sentence chose to state itself.

She read it a third time, searching out of long habit for the ambiguity that usually accompanied text this old — the alternate readings, the deliberately unstable grammar that let older prophecy mean several things at once depending on who asked it to speak. She found none here. The line read to her as though it wanted to be understood exactly as written, and that plainness troubled her nearly as much as the claim itself. Prophecy this old, in her long experience with it, was rarely so willing to be plain.

## What the Words Already Meant

She understood the first two implications before she had even finished absorbing the sentence itself, the connections arriving with a speed that frightened her more than the prophecy's content alone would have. Solmire was the sword that judged — she had no need to search further than Miguel's own blade, wielded now with an irreverence that had already begun to trouble every angel who had witnessed it in Zaphor'el and beyond. Lament, the spear of suffering, could only be the weapon Belial had carried back through the portal he had torn open, radiating a pain that had announced itself to half of Heaven's command before anyone had known its name.

Two of the three, already loose in the world. Two of the three, already shaping the war Heaven and Hell were currently fighting, whether either side yet understood the shape of what they were actually fighting over. The third weapon — the one forged to create, whatever creation meant in the hands of something old enough to predate the Divine Word itself — remained entirely unaccounted for, and the forgers behind all three remained a silence the Codex had offered nothing yet to fill.

She turned the two confirmed connections over in her mind, testing them against everything she already knew of Solmire and Lament, searching for any detail that might weaken the association rather than strengthen it. She found none. Every report she had read of Miguel's blade in Zaphor'el, every account of the portal Belial had torn open, folded neatly into the prophecy's own language, judgment and suffering arriving in the world in exactly the shapes the Codex claimed they had been forged to take. She had hoped, turning the connections over a second time, to find some flaw in her own reasoning. She found instead only further confirmation, and confirmation, in this particular case, offered her considerably less comfort than doubt would have.

## The Cost and the Question

The knowledge settled into her with a weight she had not adequately prepared herself for, and she lowered herself slowly into the chair behind her, Memnón's hum finally quieting as she released her hold on the working that had carried her this far. Whatever strength she had spent across the last three days, she understood now, had been the smaller expense. This — sitting with what she now knew, unable to set it back down, unable to share its full weight with anyone who had not read it directly from the page themselves — was the larger one, and she had only just begun to pay it.

She thought of Gabriel's instruction to send for him the instant the text was ready, and found herself hesitating over the decision in a way she rarely allowed herself to hesitate over anything. He would need to know. He would need to know soon, before this knowledge finished settling into shapes she could no longer control the telling of. But the third question the Codex had left her with — the identity of the weapon still unaccounted for, and the identity of whoever had forged all three before time itself had fully taken hold — offered her nothing yet to hand him beyond the shape of the question itself.

She weighed, sitting there with the sphere's light dimming slowly around her, exactly how much of what she now knew was actually hers to share tonight, and how much would only sharpen into something more dangerous with a few more hours of her own careful consideration first. Gabriel carried enough already, by his own account not an hour earlier. She would not add an unfinished revelation to that weight if a single night's further study might finish it properly instead. The decision cost her something to make, weighed as it was against her own promise to send for him the moment the text was ready — but the promise, she reasoned, had been made before either of them understood how much the text still had left to withhold.

She rose from the chair slowly, testing her own strength before trusting it fully, and crossed to where the last unread fragments still waited within the sphere's dimming light. Whatever remained of tonight's working, she judged, would go toward preparing herself for tomorrow's return to the text rather than forcing anything further from it now. She had learned that lesson once already this week, at some cost. She had no wish to relearn it twice.

*And the third? The one that creates?*
