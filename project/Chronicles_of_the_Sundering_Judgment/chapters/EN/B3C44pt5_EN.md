# B3C44.5 – What the Cube Found

Agares, in Amy's long experience of him, never simply told her what he wanted. He folded it inside a longer observation instead, and left her to do the unfolding herself.

"Foras marched on the fortress a fourth time this week," he said, turning a folded dispatch over in his hands without opening it, the bifid edge of his voice splitting the sentence into two overlapping tones that never quite agreed with each other. "And a fourth time, his soldiers watched him do it rather than following him into it. I find that distinction considerably more interesting than the marching itself." He let the dispatch rest, unopened, on the table between them. "Asmodeus wants an accounting of who in Hell's current disarray still commands genuine loyalty, and who commands only a fear too thin to survive close inspection. I could simply ask the soldiers. Soldiers, asked directly, tell a commander whatever keeps the asking from continuing." His smile widened, unhurried, entirely too pleased with itself. "You, Amy, don't need to ask. You need only give someone enough rope to discover, on his own, which conviction he's actually been standing on."

"And Flauros."

"Flauros ensures the rope has a cost attached, for whoever declines to use it honestly." Agares's smile didn't move, though something underneath it did — what Amy read as the particular satisfaction of a man who enjoyed watching a mechanism he'd designed run exactly as intended. "I find the two of you complement each other rather more usefully than either of you would admit unprompted. Bring me the truth, Captain. Arranged however you like. I've never once found a lie worth the trouble when the truth, told in the right order, does considerably more damage on its own."

---

The outpost sat a half-day's walk from the fortress Foras still hadn't managed to claim — a low garrison of soldiers too junior or too unlucky to have been called into the fortress march itself, left behind to hold ground nobody currently believed mattered. It had never been built to house a permanent watch. Amy could read that much in the architecture alone: walls raised in haste during some earlier, better-organized war, a drilling yard too small for the numbers now crowded onto it, a mess hall feeding twice the soldiers its original planners had ever accounted for. Hell's disarray had a shape to it once a person learned to look for the shape rather than the noise, and this garrison wore that shape plainly — men reassigned twice in as many months, ranks improvised out of whoever happened to still be standing when the last commander stopped being able to give orders.

She walked its perimeter once before choosing anyone, reading the garrison as she read most things — a structure built on assumptions its own inhabitants had mostly stopped examining. Most of the soldiers she passed wore Foras's colors with the loose, unbothered confidence of men who had simply never been asked to justify the wearing. A handful wore them differently — a fraction too neat, a fraction too deliberate, the over-correction of someone convincing himself of a loyalty he suspected, somewhere underneath the performance, he no longer fully possessed. She catalogued four of those before she found the one she wanted: a soldier posted alone at the garrison's outer perimeter, young enough that his loyalty hadn't yet had time to calcify into either genuine conviction or practiced performance, which made him, in her experience, the most honest kind of witness Hell's current disarray had left to offer.

"You've sworn to Foras," she said, settling *Laberynthus* into her palm without raising it, without any gesture that resembled a threat at all. "I'd like to understand why."

"He's the strongest claimant left standing." The soldier said it the way a man says something he's repeated often enough to believe it's his own thought rather than a borrowed one.

"Is he." She turned the cube once, unhurried, watching the light along its edges catch and hold. "I've watched four separate marches on that fortress fail this week alone — every one of them his. I wonder whether 'strongest' is the word you'd choose if you'd arrived at the thought yourself, rather than inherited it from whoever told you first." She let the question sit, offering nothing further to fill the silence it opened. "Certainty is the easiest wall to topple, soldier — not because it's weak, but because no one ever checks whether it was built on anything at all until someone finally asks."

He didn't answer immediately, and she read the delay as data rather than defiance — a mind reaching for a foundation it had never actually needed to locate before tonight.

---

She could tell Flauros found her patience harder to watch than the soldier found her questions.

"You've been at this an hour." *Aflame* uncoiled restlessly against his hip, tasting the air the way it always did when he'd gone too long without using it. "I could have the truth out of him in a fraction of the time you've spent watching him think."

"You could have something out of him in less time. Whether it's the truth is a separate question." She didn't look away from the soldier, whose attention had begun, under the cube's patient pressure, to turn inward in a way she recognized as the actual work beginning. "Fear tells you what a man thinks you want to hear. I'm not interested in what he thinks I want. I'm interested in what's actually underneath the thing he's been telling himself."

"Philosophy." Flauros said the word the way another man might spit something out of his mouth. "I've never once needed to understand why a man burns to make him burn."

"No," she agreed, entirely unbothered by the observation. "You've never needed to. That's precisely why Agares sent both of us, and not simply you." She let the small cruelty land exactly as she intended it to, and turned back to the soldier before Flauros could decide whether to answer it.

---

The wall, when it finally came down, didn't come down clean.

"I don't—" The soldier's voice cracked on the second word, and Amy watched the collapse she'd engineered arrive at a shape she hadn't actually engineered at all. "I don't follow Foras because I believe he'll win. I never believed that. Kaeth believed it." His hand moved, unconsciously, toward a scar along his forearm he hadn't drawn attention to before now. "He died believing it, second march on the fortress, the one before this week's. I stayed because—" He stopped, and Amy watched something in him arrive, for the first time in the conversation, at a truth he hadn't planned on finding. "I stayed because leaving would mean admitting his dying was for nothing. Not because I believe in Foras. Because I can't yet stand to believe Kaeth didn't."

She turned that over in her own reckoning, and found it a far more interesting answer than the one Agares had actually sent her to fetch. A soldier's fear of his commander was cheap, common currency in a fractured court like this one — but grief mistaken for loyalty, worn long enough that the wearer could no longer tell the two apart, was a different order of finding entirely, one she hadn't been sent looking for and now found she didn't especially want to hand over intact.

"That's not fear," she said, mostly to herself.

"No," the soldier agreed, and something in his voice had gone hollow in a way that told her the wall, having come down, wasn't going back up on its own. "It's not."

---

"Enough." Flauros had run out of patience somewhere in the telling, and *Aflame* came free of its coil with the impatience of a weapon that had been waiting far longer than its master had. "Whatever grief he's carrying, it's not the accounting Agares wants. He wants numbers — who stays, who breaks, who's worth Foras's next march and who isn't. Grief doesn't answer that question."

He didn't wait for Amy's agreement before he used it. The whip found the soldier's forearm, close enough to the old scar to make the point without opening it fresh, and the burn it left didn't scorch skin so much as intention — the soldier's breath caught not on pain exactly, but on the sudden, undeniable sense that whatever he'd been holding back had just been named and priced. Amy watched his carefully guarded composure buckle beneath it, not toward new information, but toward the plainer, uglier accounting Flauros had actually been sent to extract. "Six," he said, the word dragged out of him rather than offered. "Six of us, maybe seven, still here because of men like Kaeth. The rest — " He stopped, breathing hard, and didn't finish the sentence, but Amy understood the shape of the silence that replaced it: the rest stayed for Foras, and not one of them for a reason that would survive a second, harder look. Flauros drew one more line of heat along the same arm, shorter this time, more question than punishment, until the soldier gave up the six names as well, one at a time, each syllable costing him something Amy suspected he wouldn't finish paying for by morning.

It was, she thought, watching the numbers finally surface, effective. It was also a cruder instrument than the truth had actually required. The numbers had been sitting in plain reach the moment she understood what the grief was protecting; Flauros had simply burned past the protecting to get to them faster, and left the soldier with nothing standing between himself and what he'd just admitted.

---

"You didn't need the fire," she said, once Flauros had let the whip go slack again, the soldier left slumped and emptied against the perimeter wall behind them.

"I got the numbers Agares wants. You were still admiring the shape of his sorrow."

"I was finding the numbers underneath the sorrow, which is a different labor than yours, and takes rather longer to do without breaking what you're standing on." She let *Laberynthus* dim in her palm, watching the soldier's hollow, burned-through stillness with something that wasn't quite regret and wasn't quite satisfaction either. "You can only burn a source once, Captain. I could have used him again — grief like that doesn't heal fast, and a man returning to the same wound tells you more the second time than the first. Now there's nothing left in him worth a second visit."

"I don't need a second visit. I needed tonight's answer." Flauros recoiled *Aflame* against his hip, entirely unmoved by the accounting she'd just laid in front of him. "Keep telling yourself the difference matters. Agares won't."

"Perhaps." She didn't argue it further, filing the disagreement away in the same place she filed everything that hadn't finished resolving itself yet — not settled, simply set aside for whichever future night it would matter again. She suspected, watching Flauros turn back toward the garrison's gate without waiting for her, that it would.

---

She crossed the drilling yard once more on her way out, and found the garrison's mood had shifted in the short span of the interrogation — nothing so obvious as panic, nothing Flauros's fire had been loud enough to cause on its own, but a quieter recalculation moving through the soldiers she'd catalogued earlier, the ones wearing Foras's colors a fraction too neatly. Word of a burned man traveled faster than any order Foras himself had issued in weeks, and she watched two of the over-correcting four exchange a look that told her more than either of them would have told her directly — not fear of Flauros exactly, but the unease of men who had just watched a comfortable performance of loyalty cost someone else something real. It was, she judged, watching the look pass between them and neither man hold her eye afterward, the look of someone starting to wonder what his own performance might eventually cost him. She filed that too, a second finding Agares hadn't asked for and would likely value more than the first.

---

She reported the numbers to Agares before the night was through — the true accounting of Foras's garrison, thin fear on one side of the ledger, and beneath it, a smaller pocket of soldiers bound not to Foras at all but to the memory of men who'd already died believing in him. Agares received it the way he received most things, with a pleasure that showed itself only in the stillness of his listening.

"Grief dressed as loyalty," he said, turning the report over once in his mind, for the shape of the lie it wasn't quite telling. "That's a far more useful category than fear, Captain. Fear breaks predictably. Grief doesn't break at all — it simply waits for someone patient enough to ask it the right question." He looked at her a moment longer than the report strictly required.

She offered him the numbers without the name Kaeth attached to any of them, and said nothing further to invite the question she could feel forming behind his stillness. "I gave you what Asmodeus asked for," she said. "Whether I gave you the whole of what I found is a separate question, and I've never seen the use in answering questions I wasn't actually asked."

Agares's smile widened, slow and unreadable, the particular patience of a man content to let a debt sit uncollected until the night it would matter more to call it in. "No," he said. "I don't imagine you have."
