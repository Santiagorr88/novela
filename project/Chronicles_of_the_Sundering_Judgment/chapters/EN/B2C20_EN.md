# B2C20 – Report from the Abyss

Sariel timed his return the way he timed everything that mattered, waiting until the shift change put the thinnest possible stretch of Uriel's patrols between himself and the observatory's private stair. He still had to cross two open courtyards he would once have crossed without a second thought, and found, doing it now, that his usual economy of movement had gone slightly, stubbornly wrong — a half-second's lag between decision and motion that hadn't been there before the Tower, a weight behind his ribs that made the climb up the final flight of stairs cost him more than it should have.

He counted the crossing in smaller increments than he usually allowed himself, one courtyard at a time, one patrol interval at a time, refusing to let the growing distance to the observatory become a single overwhelming span he'd have to cross all at once. It was a trick he'd learned centuries ago, on hunts that ran longer than his body wanted to carry him, and he was faintly, distantly aware that needing it tonight, for a walk this short, told him something about his own condition he'd rather not have had to learn this way. He reached the observatory's door on legs that held, which was, he told himself, all he'd actually needed of them tonight.

Gabriel was already waiting when he entered, and whatever composure Gabriel usually kept in place for council sessions had clearly not survived the sight of him. "You're hurt."

"I'm here," Sariel said, which was not quite the same claim, and both of them understood the distinction well enough that neither bothered pointing it out.

Gabriel studied him a moment longer before stepping aside to let him properly into the room, taking in the details Sariel hadn't bothered trying to hide — the faint, dried line of blood at one nostril, the stiffness in how he carried his own weight, the particular grey exhaustion that no amount of controlled composure could fully disguise from someone who'd known him this long. "Sit," Gabriel said, gesturing to the chair beside the star-map rather than his usual place standing before it. "Whatever you've brought back can wait the extra minute it takes you to actually sit down for it."

Sariel didn't argue. That alone, more than anything he might have said aloud, told Gabriel plainly enough how much the crossing back had cost him.

---

He gave his report the way he gave every report, flat and exact, the emotional weight of it carried entirely in the facts rather than in how he chose to deliver them. He described the Tower's screaming archive first, the hidden study behind its concealed shelf, the annotations that grew more desperate the deeper Belial's obsession had carried him into the Codex Mortalis. Belial had not forged Lament, had not earned it through any craft or divine favor either side of this war might have recognized as legitimate. He had stolen it — planned the theft with the same patient, transactional precision he brought to everything, sacrificed Aamon, Marshal of the Infernal Legions, to buy the final approach, and walked away from the Weeping Sepulcher with a weapon that had never once belonged to Heaven, Hell, or anyone still living to claim it honestly.

He set the details out in the order he'd found them, refusing to let his own exhaustion compress the account into something less complete than it needed to be. Gabriel deserved the whole shape of it, not a summary shaped by how tired the telling made him. He drew a small, folded scrap of parchment from where he'd kept it hidden since the Tower and set it on the table between them — the unsigned warning he'd copied by hand beside Belial's own frantic notes, in his own careful script, the debt it promised anyone who claimed ownership they didn't rightfully hold. He watched Gabriel's expression sharpen at the sight of it in a way it hadn't sharpened at any of the rest.

"The Codex Mortalis names it plainly enough, in its own way," Sariel said. "The weapons are relics of an age that predates the war we're fighting now. Neither side made them. Neither side has ever had the right to them, whatever either realm has told itself these past centuries to justify the fighting."

Gabriel was quiet a long moment, the particular quiet of a man receiving confirmation of something he'd feared without yet knowing how large the fear actually was. "Then the war we've spent centuries treating as sacred," he said finally, "was never anything more than two sides squabbling over stolen property neither of them understood." He said it without theater, without the raised voice the claim might have earned from someone less exhausted by the shape of what it actually meant, and the flatness of it landed harder in the quiet room than any dramatic declaration could have managed.

He turned away from Sariel for a moment, one hand resting against the edge of the star-map's frame, and Sariel watched him stand with it in silence, saying nothing further for long enough that the silence itself became its own kind of answer. Sariel had delivered difficult reports before, to commanders who absorbed them and moved immediately to the next order. He had never once watched a report cost someone quite this much before they'd even finished deciding what to do with it.

---

Raphael had said nothing since Sariel entered, and Iofiel, standing near the star-map with the same intent, cautious attention she gave everything that threatened to become a crisis, was already turning the implications over aloud — the cycle, the sword, the pattern she'd been circling since the council first argued over a lost commander. "If the weapons predate the war," she said, mostly to herself, working through it out loud the way she always did when a theory finally found solid ground to stand on, "then everything we've built our understanding of this conflict around has been resting on a foundation neither side actually laid. The cycle's own instability, the way it let something in that signature from the Grey City resist forgetting completely — none of that fits comfortably inside a war that's only ever been Heaven against Hell. It fits considerably better inside something older."

Sariel let her voice wash past him without fully tracking every word of it, too tired to hold an unfolding argument together the way he ordinarily would have. He assumed, without examining the assumption closely, that the feeling was simply exhaustion catching up with him at last.

Raphael crossed the room instead of joining the debate, and knelt in front of Sariel's chair with the particular, focused attention of a healer who had stopped listening to the room the moment something in front of him demanded closer study. "Something is still attached to you," he said, quiet enough that it took Sariel a moment to realize the words were meant for him specifically and not for the wider conversation. "Not a wound. Something that came back with you from the Tower."

Sariel hadn't mentioned the vision beyond its bare outline. He hadn't thought there was anything further worth mentioning. "I felt it happen," he admitted. "I didn't realize it had followed me out."

---

Raphael pressed Veritas flat against his own chest rather than reaching toward Sariel directly, his eyes half-closed, his expression carrying a concentration Sariel had never once seen him wear in all their years of shared service. The staff's light dimmed rather than brightened, drawing inward instead of radiating out the way Sariel had always seen it work in every healing he'd ever watched Raphael perform. "It's alive, in the small way residue like this is alive," Raphael said, mostly to himself. "Still trying to work its way further in. Given enough time, it would have finished what the vision started, and I don't think either of us would have liked what finished looked like." His hand, closed around the staff's haft, had begun to tremble, faint but unmistakable.

"What are you doing?" Iofiel had crossed the room too, her earlier theorizing forgotten entirely in the face of whatever she was now watching unfold.

"Something I don't do," Raphael said, not looking away from the staff. "Veritas doesn't only heal. It can hold a thing in place instead of dissolving it — the same function that could, if I let it, imprison a soul rather than simply mend one. I've never once used it that way in front of anyone. I don't like this part of what I can do." His hand tightened further, the tremor deepening rather than easing. "But leaving it inside him would be worse."

---

The residue came free of Sariel in a way he felt more than saw, a cold, foreign pressure lifting from somewhere behind his sternum, gone so completely and so suddenly that its absence felt, for one disorienting moment, like a wound in its own right. Raphael held it a long moment, visible now as a faint, writhing distortion caught somewhere between the staff's head and his own closed fist, before setting it down on the table with the exact, careful economy of a man setting down something he did not entirely trust to stay where he'd put it.

"Have you ever done that to something with a mind of its own," Iofiel asked, "not just a residue like this?"

Raphael didn't answer directly. "I hope I never have to find out what that feels like," he said, and left the inert fragment sitting sealed on the table between them, its faint distortion gone still now rather than fading, contained rather than dissolved. He said nothing further, his own stillness carrying none of the satisfaction Sariel would have expected from a task completed well.

Sariel touched the spot on his own chest where the pressure had sat a moment before, and felt nothing there at all — no ache, no absence, no evidence it had ever existed. That, more than any lingering pain could have, unsettled him. A wound he could measure. This left him with only Raphael's word that anything had been there to begin with, and a hunter's old, uneasy instinct that the things he couldn't measure had always been the ones worth worrying about most.

---

Gabriel let the room settle before he spoke again, and when he did, his voice carried the particular weight of a man reorganizing an entire understanding of the war he'd spent his existence serving. "If the weapons belong to neither side," he said, "then Miguel's soul is not the only anomaly this council needs to find. Whatever forged those weapons predates us. If it left one mark on this war, it likely left others." He looked to Iofiel, then to Raphael, weighing something Sariel couldn't fully follow in his current state. "We are no longer simply searching for a lost commander. We are searching for whatever else that older power touched, and we need to understand it before Hell finishes understanding it first."

"The two signals," Iofiel said. "The one near Navarion, the one in the Grey City. If they're connected to this at all—"

"Then they matter considerably more than a council divided over a sword ever accounted for," Gabriel finished. He crossed to the star-map, studying the two familiar points of light with an attention that carried more weight now than it had the first time he'd stood before them. "We've been treating this as a search for one lost commander and one dangerous anomaly, two separate problems that happened to share a season. If Sariel's right about what he found, they may be pieces of the same much older question, and I have no intention of continuing to treat them as though they aren't until something forces the correction on us."

Iofiel moved to stand beside him at the map, her own attention already sharpening toward the practical shape of the problem rather than lingering on the theory behind it. "Then we watch both signals properly this time. Not from a distance, and not through secondhand reports months after the fact. If either one shifts again the way the Grey City did, I want this council to know within the hour, not to learn about it from a rumor that's already traveled halfway across a continent by the time it reaches us."

"Agreed," Gabriel said. He turned back to Sariel, something apologetic entering his expression for the first time since Sariel had arrived. "You've given this council more in one night than months of careful debate managed on its own."

---

"Do you want me to return to the Tower," Sariel asked, because it needed asking plainly, and because some part of him had already begun bracing for the answer before Gabriel gave it.

"No." Gabriel said it without hesitation, without the diplomatic softening he sometimes wrapped around difficult orders. "You've paid enough for this phase of it. What comes next isn't infiltration. It's patience — watching, waiting, letting the two signals we already have tell us more before we risk anyone else the way I risked you." He studied Sariel a moment longer, plainly searching for some sign of relief that didn't fully arrive. "That should sound like good news."

"It does," Sariel said, and meant it, mostly. Weeks of watching sounded, on its face, like the safest assignment this mission had handed him yet, and some old, tired part of him was grateful enough for that safety not to examine the rest of what he was feeling too closely tonight.

He rose from the chair carefully, testing his own balance before he trusted it fully, and found it holding better now than it had on the climb up. Iofiel had already returned her attention to the star-map, and Raphael stood a little apart from both of them, still watching the sealed fragment on the table's surface with an expression Sariel couldn't quite name. He wasn't certain he'd rest easily himself, whatever Gabriel intended to call the coming weeks.

He said nothing about the small, unfamiliar restlessness already settling into him at the word *patience* — the strange, quiet discovery that danger, for a man built the way he was built, had always been easier to carry than waiting had ever once promised to be. He kept that discovery to himself, and said nothing further to correct whatever impression his silence left Gabriel with.
