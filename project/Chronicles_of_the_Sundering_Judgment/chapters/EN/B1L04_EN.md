# B1L04 — The Descent

*(Lament Arc, Chapter 4 of 6 — POV Belial, brief cuts to a mortal fisherman and a low-ranking angelic sentinel)*

Letting the grief in had been the easy part. He understood that now, three days deeper into the plain than he had ever gone, with the crystal underfoot grown finer, the shards smaller and more numerous, until walking felt like wading through something closer to black sand than stone. Letting it in had been a single decision, made once, at a threshold. Continuing to let it in, step after step, for as long as the plain demanded, was a discipline he had no practice at, and his body kept relearning that lesson the hard way.

The visions did not knock. They arrived the way weather arrived, total and unannounced. A mother, somewhere and somewhen he had no names for, singing to a child who had already stopped breathing, the song continuing long after there was anyone left to hear it. A general — not unlike himself, he noted with a discomfort he refused to examine — standing over a field of soldiers who had trusted his strategy, understanding a half-second too late that trust had been the wrong thing to give him. A silence so vast it had a shape, the specific quiet of a species realizing, all at once, that it was the last of itself.

None of it was his. All of it, for the length of each vision, felt exactly as his as anything he had ever actually lived.

He wanted, with an intensity that frightened him more than the visions did, to fight it. Every instinct he had ever trusted told him to raise Ruach, to find the shape of this grief and put a mace through it the way he'd put a mace through every obstacle that had ever stood between himself and something he wanted. He did not draw it. That refusal cost him something with every step — a small, grinding act of will, holding a door open when every fiber of his pride screamed to slam it — and he could feel the cost accumulating in him like pressure behind a dam that had never in its existence been asked to hold anything back.

Something, somewhere, felt that pressure too.

---

Tomas Herrera had fished the same stretch of coastline for thirty-one years, and in thirty-one years the water had never once done what it did that morning. His son was with him that day, still young enough to be more useful with the nets than the wheel. There was no storm. There was no wind worth mentioning. And yet the swell rose beneath the boat in a single, wrongly smooth motion, as though the sea itself had taken one long, unsteady breath, and somewhere beneath the waterline a sound traveled through the hull that Tomas felt in his teeth more than heard — a low groan, ancient and enormous, coming from no fault line any chart of his had ever marked. On the shore behind them, gulls that had been feeding a moment before rose all together, silent, and did not come back down.

His son gripped the rail, knuckles white. "What was that?"

Tomas kept both hands steady on the wheel and made his voice do something his stomach wasn't doing. "The sea having a bad day, that's all." The joke didn't survive its own delivery; his voice cracked halfway through it, and his son heard the crack before he heard the words. For a moment the boy was more frightened by his father's fear than by the sea itself.

He would tell the story later, to people who would nod and not quite believe him, that for perhaps four seconds the whole ocean had felt like it was grieving something, and that he had felt monstrously, unaccountably sad, and that the sadness had passed as suddenly as it came, leaving him alone on a boat on a flat sea with his hands shaking for no reason he could name.

He never learned there had been an earthquake, three hundred miles inland, along no fault anyone could later explain.

---

Six hundred miles above the same stretch of ocean, a low-ranking sentinel assigned to watch for exactly this kind of anomaly felt it too, and reported it because reporting anomalies was the entire content of his post.

"Something moved," he said into the watch-channel. "Not physical. It had a direction — like it was heading somewhere, not just happening."

His superior, unhurried, did not look up from the ledger he was annotating. "Another residual echo from the last battle. We get three a week."

"This one had a direction," the sentinel said again, because he did not know how else to make the distinction land. "Echoes fade where they start. This one was going somewhere."

The superior logged it anyway, without urgency, one line among a hundred others that week that would not be read again until it was already too late to matter.

---

Belial did not feel the tremor reach the mortal world. He felt only its cause — the moment his resistance, straining against a grief he refused to fully surrender to, found a fracture point and let something through, a discharge he had no name for and no intention to examine. It cost him. He staggered, one hand catching against black glass gone suddenly, dangerously fine underfoot, and for the length of a single unguarded breath he was not Belial, Lord of Pride and Power, was not anyone with a name or a court or a war to return to — was only a thing standing very small in a place built to remind every proud creature exactly how small it was.

He did not call it compassion, afterward. He would never have permitted himself the word. But something in him had, for the first time in longer than his memory reliably reached, touched another creature's pain without immediately asking what it cost him to feel it, and that unguarded instant — small, involuntary, already fading — left a mark he suspected would outlast the visions that caused it.

He straightened. The crystal beneath him had gone from black sand to something closer to glass, smooth and cold and strangely warm at once, and past it, through air gone thick as held breath, he saw for the first time a shape on the horizon that was not weather, not memory, not another borrowed grief.

A door. Or the ghost of one — a threshold cut into the plain itself, marked by nothing but the fact that the light bent differently around it, grief pooling at its edges the way water pools at the lowest point of a room.

He was diminished. He could feel it in the unfamiliar unsteadiness of his own legs, in the strange new caution that had crept into a stride that had never once in his existence needed to be careful. But he was closer than he had ever been, and closer, for a creature built entirely on the conviction that pride outlasted every cost, was the only arithmetic that had ever mattered.

He walked toward the door.
