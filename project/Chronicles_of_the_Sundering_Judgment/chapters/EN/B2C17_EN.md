# B2C17 – A Thief's Legacy

The trail Sariel followed deeper into the Tower had nothing to do with sight or sound. It was ambition he tracked now, the particular psychic residue a mind as hungry as Belial's left behind wherever it lingered too long over a text — a mark on the archive's accumulated silence, distinct from the ambient screaming the way a single voice stands apart from a crowd. He had learned, across centuries of hunting things that didn't want to be found, to read exactly this kind of residue, and Belial's had settled into this place deeply enough that even months later it remained plain to a mind trained to look for it.

The trail ended at a hidden study three levels below the one where the vision had found him, a chamber concealed behind a shelf that swung inward at a touch, its hinge worn smooth by a use its builders had clearly never intended anyone else to discover. Sariel stepped through into a room untouched by the archive's general chaos, shelves here arranged with the particular, obsessive order of a mind that had wanted nothing left to chance.

The screaming hush that filled the rest of the Tower faded almost entirely once the shelf sealed shut behind him, and the quiet that replaced it felt, if anything, more unsettling than the din he'd left behind — a silence too deliberate to be accidental, the kind of quiet a room earns only when someone has gone to considerable trouble to keep it that way. He moved through it carefully, still favoring the side where the vision's aftermath had left a dull, persistent ache behind his ribs, cataloguing the room's contents with the same patient thoroughness he brought to every space he'd ever needed to search without leaving evidence that he'd searched it.

---

The fragments of the Codex Mortalis sat displayed across a reading table exactly where Belial had apparently left them, undisturbed since whatever night he'd last studied them here. Sariel approached slowly, reading what remained of the ancient text with the careful, hungry attention of a man who understood, better than most, exactly how much a single surviving fragment could sometimes cost to obtain. The pages spoke only in fragments and riddles of weapons neither Heaven nor Hell had ever fully claimed as their own creation, offering nothing so plain as an origin, only the persistent, unsettling suggestion that one existed somewhere beneath the text's careful evasions.

Belial's own annotations crowded the margins in a hand grown increasingly urgent as the pages progressed, question after question scrawled beside passages the original text answered only in riddles. Sariel had expected arrogance in those margins. He found instead something closer to desperation — a mind working frantically to understand a power it had already decided to seize, regardless of whether understanding ever actually arrived in time to matter.

He traced the progression across a dozen fragments, watching the handwriting itself change as the study wore on — confident at first, almost dismissive in its brevity, then gradually cramped and urgent, entire pages given over to the same question rephrased a dozen different ways. Whatever Belial had been searching for in these pages, he had clearly not found it cleanly. He had found enough to act on, and had acted, and Sariel understood, reading the last and most frantic of the annotations, that acting without fully understanding was very likely the reason Belial now carried a permanent, unwanted cost he had never once anticipated when he'd first set out to claim his prize.

---

It was the different hand that caught his attention properly — older, more cautious, its ink faded to a shade Belial's fresher annotations hadn't yet begun to match. It sat crowded into a corner Belial's own notes had left untouched, as though whoever wrote it had known enough to leave a warning where a careless reader might miss it, and a careful one wouldn't.

*Whoever claims this without being its rightful owner will find no glory. They will find a debt.*

Sariel copied the line into memory with the same careful precision he gave everything worth remembering, and became aware, in the same instant, that he was no longer alone in the chamber.

"You've found the old margin note." The voice belonged to a minor archivist, elderly even by the standards of a realm that measured age differently than mortals did, his robes marking him as one of the Tower's permanent staff rather than a visitor passing through. He'd entered without Sariel noticing, and stood now examining the reading table with what struck Sariel as the mild, proprietary interest of a man checking on furniture rather than a man guarding forbidden knowledge. "Most who come through here miss it entirely. Your lord's attention rarely wandered that far from the text itself."

Sariel kept his borrowed indifference carefully in place. "Who else has consulted this collection, before my lord?"

"Before Lord Belial?" The archivist gave a short, dry laugh, entirely unguarded — a laugh that read, to Sariel, like a man too bored with his own work to suspect the question carried any real weight behind it. "No one, in centuries. This isn't the kind of material anyone reads twice for pleasure. It gets read twice because the first reading never actually made sense."

"And the older hand," Sariel asked, careful to let the question trail after the first as though it barely mattered. "Do the Tower's records say whose it was?"

"No name attached to it that I've ever found, and I've looked, if only out of my own idle curiosity over the years." The archivist considered the question with more genuine interest than he'd shown the rest of the exchange. "Older than the collection itself, if the ink's any judge. Whoever wrote it wasn't cataloguing anything. They were warning off whoever came after them, and didn't much care whether anyone ever read it." He shrugged, already losing interest again, and drifted back toward the doorway without waiting for Sariel to respond further. "Take your time. No one's come looking for this dust in longer than either of us has probably been alive."

Sariel said nothing more, and let the archivist go, filing the answer away as carefully as he'd filed the margin note itself.

---

The records of the heist sat further back in the study, in a locked drawer Belial's own annotations had clearly indicated the location of without ever bothering to properly conceal, arrogance winning out, in the end, over caution even here. Sariel read through them with the same flat, professional attention he'd have given any hunt's final piece of evidence, and felt something cold settle into his chest as the full shape of it assembled itself in front of him.

The plan itself had been built in careful stages, each one recorded with the dispassionate precision of a man documenting a campaign rather than confessing to a theft — a false treaty offered to buy passage past the Sepulcher's outer wards, a manufactured rivalry set to draw its lesser guardians away from their posts at the exact hour Belial needed them absent, and finally the sacrifice itself, timed to the precise moment the deception could no longer hold on its own. Nothing in the notes read like improvisation. Every step had been mapped in advance, contingencies included, the whole operation assembled with the same patient thoroughness Sariel recognized from his own centuries of careful hunts.

Belial had not simply found Lament through diligence or divine favor. He had orchestrated its theft, deliberately and at real cost — the sacrifice of Aamon, Marshal of the Infernal Legions, offered up to whatever guardian had once protected the Weeping Sepulcher's approach. The notes named the Sepulcher's location plainly, along with the exact sequence of deceptions Belial had used to breach its final ward, none of it recorded with any pride in craftsmanship, only the flat, transactional satisfaction of a thief cataloguing a job well executed.

Sariel read the account of Aamon's sacrifice twice, the second time more slowly than the first. Belial's notes gave a commander of Aamon's standing exactly three lines — no mention of the legions he'd marshaled, no acknowledgment of the rank he'd spent centuries building, only the bare fact of the sacrifice and a brief, clinical note on how effectively it had opened the Sepulcher's final approach. Sariel had killed a great many things in his long service, and had rarely troubled himself afterward over what any of them had once commanded before he ended them. He found, reading Belial's cold three lines about a marshal reduced to a single tactical expenditure, that this particular death bothered him more than most of his own ever had.

---

Sariel sat back from the drawer's contents and let the larger truth of it settle properly into place. He had come here half expecting to find evidence of some terrible, singular genius — a mastermind who had earned his prize through understanding no one else possessed. He found instead a thief, patient and ruthless but fundamentally ordinary in his methods, who had simply been willing to spend what others weren't to take something that had never once belonged to him.

That understanding reframed everything Gabriel had sent him here to learn. The weapons were not artifacts of either side's own making, prizes any general might have legitimately claimed through conquest or divine right. They belonged to neither Heaven nor Hell at all. Belial had not won Lament. He had stolen it, the same way any common thief stole anything too valuable to walk away from honestly, and the war both realms had spent centuries fighting over its use had never once involved a party who actually owned what was being fought over.

He thought of Gabriel's suspicion in the cold observatory, the careful, unfinished shape of it — that whatever had killed Malthus predated even Hell's own knowledge of itself, that a trail worth finding waited somewhere in this very Tower. Gabriel had guessed at the size of the mystery without yet guessing at its nature. Sariel now carried both, and understood, sitting alone in a dead man's study three levels beneath Hell's oldest archive, that some truths cost considerably more to carry than they had ever seemed to cost simply to seek.

---

Sariel took nothing else from the study — no fragment pocketed, no page torn free, nothing that might mark him as having been here at all should the study ever be searched again. But before he stepped back through the concealed shelf, he paused a final moment over the spot where the unsigned margin note had waited, faded and easy to overlook, for however many silent centuries had passed since someone first thought to write it.

Gabriel had not asked him to remember a warning meant for thieves. Nothing in his instructions had mentioned it at all. But some old instinct, the same patient certainty that had made him good at tracking souls too well hidden for anyone else to find, told him plainly that the warning would matter again before this was finished — that a debt promised to whoever claimed something without rightful ownership was not the kind of detail a hunter of his experience could afford to trust to memory alone. He drew a small scrap of blank parchment from the lining of his forged papers, where it had been folded flat and unnoticed since he'd crossed into Hell, and copied the line onto it in his own careful hand, letter for letter, before folding it away again somewhere no search of a records clerk would think to look.

It was, he understood, the first decision he had made on this entire mission that Gabriel had not specifically authorized. He found he did not regret it. A hunter who only ever followed instructions to the letter caught nothing that hadn't already been named for him in advance, and Sariel had built a long, quiet career on being considerably better than that.

Then he stepped back through the shelf, sealed it behind him with the same careful patience he'd used to open it, and turned his attention toward the long, dangerous walk back out of Hell's oldest and hungriest archive.
