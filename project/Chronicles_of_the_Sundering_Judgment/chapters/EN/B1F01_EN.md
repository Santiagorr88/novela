# B1F01 – The Price of Absence

## The Vultures Circling

Malthus felt the shift in Hell's court within days of Belial's return with Lament, long before anyone in Foras's own household dared to say the word weakness aloud, and long before Foras himself seemed to notice, or at least to acknowledge, that anything in the court's temperature had changed at all. It arrived the way these things always arrived among their kind, not as open challenge but as a subtle recalculation in how others held themselves in his lord's presence — a fraction less deference in a bow, a fraction more confidence in the messengers who once would have waited to be summoned rather than simply appearing. Malthus had served Foras loyally through centuries of courts no calmer than this one, and loyalty, in his considered experience, was no excuse for blindness to what it was meant to protect against.

Foras remained, in every practical sense, exactly the commander he had always been. The court's perception of him was a separate matter entirely, and Malthus had learned long ago that perception, left unattended, had a way of becoming its own kind of reality regardless of the truth underneath it. He intended to attend to it. He simply had not yet decided how.

He had watched courts like this one turn on far less than a single humbling defeat. A commander's strength, in Hell's particular arithmetic, was worth exactly as much as the court currently believed it was worth, and belief had a way of eroding faster than the underlying reality ever actually changed. Malthus had seen loyal households scatter within weeks of a perceived weakness, not because their lord had genuinely failed them, but because enough individual demons had each independently calculated that the safer wager lay elsewhere. He did not intend to let that particular arithmetic run its course unchallenged, not while there was still time to intervene before the court settled on a consensus he could no longer easily undo.

He walked the length of his own chambers turning the problem over, the familiar cold stone underfoot doing nothing to settle the low, persistent unease that had followed him since the first whispers reached his ears, and settled at last on the same conclusion he generally reached whenever a threat proved too abstract to strike at directly — that concrete action, however small, would tell him more than continued watching ever could. Foras had built this territory across centuries of careful, patient work, and Malthus had spent nearly as long standing at his side through every part of it. He was not prepared to watch it unravel now over a single battlefield loss that had nothing, in truth, to do with Foras's own competence at all.

## The Ritual

He summoned Corvin, one of his own subordinates, to the ritual chamber that evening, the young demon's nerves plain in the careful way he held himself at the circle's edge. The chamber itself had served this exact purpose for longer than Corvin had likely been alive, its walls scored with the accumulated marks of centuries of similar bindings, each one a small permanent record of a loyalty Malthus had judged worth sealing this deeply. Malthus traced the pact-circle without hurrying, chalk and blood and ash arranging themselves into the old, familiar pattern Maleth demanded of him, each line drawn with the unhurried precision of a man who had performed this particular ceremony more times than he could easily count.

Corvin watched the circle take shape with a wide, careful attention that suggested, to Malthus's eye, someone determined not to miss whatever significance the moment carried, though Malthus judged, from the young demon's stance, that most of that significance still eluded him. That was, in its own way, exactly as it should be. Understanding tended to arrive during the ritual itself, or shortly after, never fully beforehand. Malthus had learned that lesson from his own first binding, centuries ago now, and had let every supplicant since arrive at it the same unhurried way, without shortcuts, whatever their impatience.

"Ash doesn't go back to being wood," Malthus said, tracing the circle without pause. "And blood, once it's spilled, doesn't choose who it serves. Think on that while you can still choose yourself." Corvin's voice, when he answered, carried only the thinnest thread of steadiness. "I'd rather owe you a handful of ash than burn whole with whoever comes after." Malthus nodded, satisfied enough by the answer that he saw no need to explain the parable any further. He rarely explained his parables twice. A subordinate who required a second telling rarely survived long enough in his service to need a third.

## What Corvin Feared

Malthus drew the blade across his own palm and let three drops fall onto the ash Corvin had scattered at the circle's center, watching the dark liquid spread and catch against the grey until the two substances bound into something neither one alone. He pressed Corvin's hand briefly to the mark, feeling the younger demon's pulse hammering hard enough to register even through the contact, and released it the moment the sigils ringing the circle flared once, low and steady, and settled into a faint, permanent glow that told him plainly enough the pact had taken. Corvin's breathing, ragged through the whole of it, eased only once the glow had fully settled, something in his posture loosening that had been rigid since he first entered the chamber.

Malthus judged, watching that particular shift, that whatever fear had driven Corvin to seek a binding this permanent had eased alongside it, though he could not say with certainty what exactly had been driving it in the first place — only that a court unsettled enough to make every existing loyalty feel suddenly negotiable tended, in his long experience, to breed this same raw fear in demons young enough not to have weathered a realignment before. He suspected Corvin had seen, as most of Foras's household had seen by now, what tended to happen to demons caught on the losing side of one — not always death, though death was common enough, but something considerably slower and more thorough in the cases that lingered.

"You could have waited," Malthus said, not unkindly, once the ritual's last traces had settled. "Seen which way the court actually turned before committing yourself." Corvin shook his head. "Waiting is how everyone loses, in the end. I'd rather bind myself to a captain I trust now than gamble on guessing right later." Malthus said nothing further to that, though he filed the answer away as exactly the kind of clear-eyed pragmatism he valued in the demons who served under him.

He considered, watching Corvin gather himself in the aftermath of the binding, how rarely he actually heard reasoning this honest from anyone below him in rank. Most demons who sought him out for bindings like this one dressed their fear in language far more flattering to themselves — ambition, opportunity, calculated advancement. Corvin had simply named the fear directly, and Malthus found he respected that honesty above the polished justifications he had heard from supplicants performing this same ritual, for this same reason, across centuries of it.

"Then we understand each other," Malthus said, extending a hand to help the younger demon to his feet. "Ash given willingly to the fire burns clean. Ash forced from wood still bearing sap only smokes and chokes the one who lit it." Corvin took the offered hand, steadier now than he had been at any point since entering the chamber. "Then mine burns clean," he said. "For whatever that's worth, coming from someone who only decided an hour ago." Malthus allowed himself something close to approval at that. An hour was, in his own long experience, often exactly as long as honest loyalty actually needed to properly settle.

## The Quiet Sounding

The first real sign came three days later, delivered in the particular silence Malthus had learned, across centuries in Hell's court, to read as considerably louder than it appeared. A minor trade route through Foras's territory, ordinarily unremarkable, had gone quiet — no formal inquiry, no messenger demanding explanation, only the absence of the usual traffic that should have passed through it by now. Silence of this specific shape meant exactly one thing in Hell's court: someone was testing the edges of a territory they intended to eventually claim, carefully enough to leave no accusation behind if the testing came to nothing.

Malthus traced the disturbance back through his own informants over the following day, patient in the way ritual work had always trained him to be. What he found confirmed the shape of his suspicion without yet confirming its source — a presence moving through Foras's holdings with none of the clumsiness an ordinary opportunist would have shown, careful enough that only someone actively looking for it would have noticed the pattern at all.

He worked through the informants methodically, one careful conversation after another, weighing each report against the last for whatever small contradiction or confirmation it might offer. A merchant who traded regularly through the disrupted route reported an unfamiliar face asking careful, seemingly idle questions about shipment schedules three days before the traffic actually stopped. A minor functionary at the territory's edge mentioned, almost in passing, a stranger who had spent an unusual amount of time studying the boundary markers without ever crossing them. Neither detail alone would have told him much. Together, laid alongside the silence itself, they painted the outline of someone conducting a careful, deliberate survey rather than any opportunistic scavenging.

He returned to his own chambers that evening with the outline still incomplete, missing only the one detail that would tell him precisely whose hand was actually behind it. He did not doubt, gathering the fragments together, that the answer would arrive soon enough. Whoever was testing Foras's borders this carefully had not done so carelessly enough yet to leave an obvious signature behind. That kind of carelessness, in Malthus's long experience, usually arrived eventually regardless, once enough time passed for confidence to dull whatever discipline had governed the opening moves.

## Vepar's Signature

He found the confirmation he needed the following evening, examining a discarded ward-seal left behind at the edge of the disrupted trade route, the kind of small carelessness even a careful operative occasionally left in their wake. The seal's particular construction, the specific arcane signature woven through it, matched nothing Malthus had encountered from any of Foras's other rivals across his long service. He recognized it immediately for what it was — the unmistakable craft of Vepar's own household, the same cold, methodical precision that ran through everything Vepar's people touched.

Malthus held the fragment a long moment, turning it over in his hand with the same careful attention he brought to any ritual component, weighing what its presence actually confirmed. Vepar's own household was sounding Foras's territory directly, not some minor opportunist testing weak borders on their own initiative. That distinction weighed heavier than the disruption itself. A minor scavenger could be dealt with quietly, contained without escalation. An operation bearing Vepar's household signature, probing this openly even in this careful, deniable fashion, represented something Malthus could no longer afford to simply monitor from a comfortable distance.

He had crossed paths with Vepar's household more than once across his long service, always at some careful remove, the two commanders' territories bordering each other closely enough to guarantee occasional friction without ever quite tipping into open conflict. Vepar himself he had met only rarely, and had come away from each encounter with the same settled impression — a mind that calculated advantage the way water calculated its own path downhill, patient and inevitable, never in any particular hurry because it never doubted it would eventually arrive. A household like his rarely tested a rival's borders without purpose. Whether Vepar himself had personally ordered this particular probing, Malthus could not yet say with certainty; the timing nevertheless suggested a deliberate calculation somewhere behind it.

That the calculation had arrived so quickly after Belial's return with Lament told Malthus something further worth noting — Vepar's own informants were plainly as sharp as Malthus had always assumed them to be, quick enough to translate a single battlefield humiliation into an immediate, concrete opportunity before the rest of Hell's court had even finished whispering about it. Whatever time Malthus still had to act before that probing became something more aggressive, he suspected it would not last long.

## The Resolution

He returned to his own chambers that night carrying the ward-seal fragment with him, setting it on the table where he did his clearest thinking, and sat with the decision a long while before finally allowing himself to settle on it. Foras remained, whatever the court's current perception suggested, the commander Malthus had sworn his loyalty to long before tonight's small, patient work became necessary. That loyalty demanded more of him now than quiet vigilance. It demanded action, taken before Vepar's careful, deniable probing escalated into something far harder to contain.

He would not wait for Foras to ask it of him. Waiting, as Corvin had rightly observed only days ago, was how everyone eventually lost. Malthus intended to move first, quietly and precisely, before Stolas — Vepar's chosen hand for precisely this sort of work, if Malthus's read on the signature was correct — could advance any further into ground that still, whatever the court currently whispered, belonged to Foras alone.

He would not bring this to Foras yet, either. His lord carried enough already, weighing the court's shifting loyalties against the larger war Hell was still fighting against Heaven, and Malthus saw no benefit in adding an unconfirmed suspicion to that burden before he had something concrete enough to act on. Better, he decided, to handle the opening moves of this quietly himself, the way he had handled every threat to Foras's household across the long centuries of his own service, and bring his lord only the finished picture once it was actually worth his attention.

He set the ward-seal fragment carefully into the small case he kept for evidence of this weight, sealed it, and allowed himself, for the first time since the whispers first reached him, something close to settled resolve. Vepar's household had made its opening move. Malthus intended to make certain it was also its last uncontested one.
