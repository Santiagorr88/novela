# B3C42 – The Final Verse

Captain Iofiel, Wisdom Keeper of the Silent Choir, had spent longer centuries than most angels could easily comprehend studying the Codex Mortalis in this very reading room, and had never once, across that long tenure, seen its ancient script behave the way it was behaving now.

The great library around her held its usual patient hush, shelves of living text stretching upward into a vaulted ceiling that seemed, on quieter days, to simply continue without ever actually ending. She had come to this exact reading table most mornings for longer than she cared to measure, had grown accustomed to the Codex's slow, ordinary drift, verses shifting and reordering themselves according to whatever ancient logic none of the Choir's keepers had ever fully deciphered. What she watched now carried none of that ordinary drift. The script moved instead with clear, deliberate purpose, entire passages reorganizing themselves in patterns too precise to be anything but intentional.

She had earned her rank as Captain of the Choir's small standing guard long before she'd ever been named Wisdom Keeper, a title she still carried formally even now, decades into a scholarly tenure that had long since eclipsed her earlier military service in most other keepers' eyes. She had spent decades learning to read danger in far simpler patterns than the ones currently unfolding across the Codex's ancient pages. She found the old instinct rising in her now regardless, the particular attention of a soldier assessing an unfamiliar threat, even as the scholar in her recognized, watching the script settle into its new configuration, that whatever this actually was, it carried nothing threatening in it at all.

She set down the smaller reference text she'd been cross-checking and gave the Codex her full attention, the particular stillness she'd learned across her long service settling into her posture. Whatever this was, it deserved more care than her usual morning study. She thought, watching the script continue its deliberate reorganizing, of every rumor that had reached the Choir across the last several weeks — a war ended without warning, a silence spreading where terror had reigned only days before, fragments of report too inconsistent yet to properly catalogue. She had assumed, reading those early fragments, that the full account would eventually arrive through the usual formal channels. She understood now, watching the Codex apparently decide to tell her itself, that she had assumed wrong.

---

A page near the volume's end, one that had remained stubbornly blank through more ages than Iofiel had personally witnessed, began to fill.

She watched it happen with something closer to reverent astonishment than the composed professional interest she generally brought to her work, glowing gray script the color of storm-light flowing onto the page in slow, deliberate strokes, no visible hand guiding it, no quill or instrument she could point to as the actual source of the writing. She had catalogued a great many strange phenomena across her long tenure as Wisdom Keeper. She found herself, watching this one unfold, unable to look away even to blink.

She had studied this particular blank page more times than she could easily count across her long service, had run every diagnostic her considerable scholarship could devise trying to determine why it alone, among the Codex's thousands of pages, had never once filled with a single word. She had eventually filed the mystery away, reluctantly, as simply one of the volume's many unanswered questions, a small, persistent gap in an otherwise complete record. She understood now, watching that gap finally close, exactly what it had actually been waiting for.

She studied the particular quality of the ink a long moment, recognizing something in its steady, unhurried energy that she'd only ever encountered once before, in fragments too old and too faded for most keepers to properly identify anymore. The signature was unmistakable once she'd actually placed it — calm, balanced, entirely without hurry, the same essence she'd read described in the Choir's oldest records as belonging to one of the three Forgotten. Azael. She had never met the sage directly. She had studied enough fragments of his old writings, faded and incomplete as most of them were, to recognize his particular stillness anywhere.

She thought, watching the last few lines settle into place, of every scholar before her who had spent centuries searching for even a fragment of the Forgotten's own direct testimony, records so old and so carefully guarded that most keepers went their entire tenure without ever handling one personally, treating even the faintest surviving scrap as though it might crumble under too eager a hand. She had handled perhaps three such fragments across her own long service, each one treated with the particular reverence reserved for artifacts considerably older than the Choir itself. She understood, watching an entire fresh verse resolve itself before her, that she was about to become the first keeper in longer than the Codex's own records could reliably measure to read Azael's testimony while the ink itself was still settling.

---

Iofiel read the new verse aloud once it had finished filling the page, her voice carrying through the library's vast quiet with the careful, measured cadence she reserved for texts she judged worthy of proper ceremony.

"Three forgers who were one," she read, "shaped by the same hand from the same original clay. Ereloth, who forged creation and joy from nothing at all. Thaeriel, who judged suffering into its proper, necessary shape. Azael, who held the balance neither of his brothers could hold for themselves." She paused, studying the next passage a long moment before continuing. "They broke the world between them, in an accident born of love turned careless. And it was Azael who became the loom on which all three were finally rewoven — his sacrifice a transformation, not an ending. He did not vanish from what he built. He became it."

She paused a moment before continuing, weighing the exact phrasing of what she'd just read against every doctrine she'd spent her long tenure helping catalogue and preserve. She had studied the tale of the Forgotten's original fracture in a hundred different fragmentary accounts across her service, none of them ever quite agreeing on the precise number of beings actually responsible for it. She read on, finding the verse's next passage naming a fourth figure, carefully, deliberately separate from the three it had just described. "And there was one more," the Codex continued, "born of neither the fracture nor the forging — a bearer, a witness, who carried the light of judgment without ever needing to have shaped the original wound himself. Michael. Not a fourth forger. The catalyst without whom the reconciliation could never have been completed." Iofiel found herself pausing over that distinction a long moment, weighing the careful precision of the phrasing word by word, and understood, studying it, that whoever — whatever — had actually written this verse had taken considerable care to draw that particular line correctly. She thought of every careless account she'd already corrected in lesser records across the Choir's holdings, scribes who had assumed, without checking, that any fourth figure standing beside three primordial forgers must simply be a fourth forger himself. She found herself, reading this far more careful version, already composing the amendment she'd need to send those lesser archives once this new verse had been properly catalogued.

The final passage settled into something quieter, plainer, the ornate cadence of the verse's opening giving way to a simplicity that struck Iofiel, after so many decades spent studying the Codex's usual dense, formal prose, as considerably more moving than any grander language could have managed. "Judgment, creation, and balance were never three separate forces," she read, her own voice carrying a weight she hadn't consciously chosen to give it. "They were always one integrated whole, wearing three faces because three faces were easier for smaller minds to recognize. The final judgment was that there shall be no more final judgments."

---

Iofiel lowered the Codex slowly once she'd finished reading, and found, in the stillness that followed, a single tear tracing a slow path down her own cheek.

She sat with the sensation a long moment, studying it the way she might have studied any unfamiliar phenomenon, and understood, examining it honestly, that it carried nothing of grief in it at all. She had wept exactly twice before across her entire long tenure as Wisdom Keeper, both times in genuine sorrow over losses the Codex itself had recorded in careful, unflinching detail — a garrison lost without warning, a small kindness the volume had noted just before its subject's final entry closed. This felt considerably different, a peace so complete and so unexpected that her own body had apparently needed some physical outlet for it before her composed exterior had even finished registering the feeling properly.

She thought of every prior verse she'd catalogued across her long service, records of wars and judgments and losses too numerous to hold in memory without the Codex's careful assistance. She had grown accustomed, across that long tenure, to reading history primarily as a record of cost — what had been lost, what had been paid, what price each fragile peace had actually extracted from those who'd fought hardest to secure it. This verse read differently. It read like an actual conclusion, plain and complete, rather than simply another entry in the same unbroken chain.

She thought, too, of every keeper before her who had spent a career cataloguing judgment after judgment, verdict after verdict, without once encountering a verse that dared to declare judgment itself obsolete. She wondered how the rest of the Choir would receive it, whether the older keepers — the ones who had built entire careers on the careful weighing of sin and consequence — would find this new law as freeing as she currently found it, or whether some among them would grieve, quietly and privately, the loss of a framework they'd spent centuries mastering.

She set the Codex down carefully on its reading stand, its pages already beginning to settle back into their familiar, patient stillness, and allowed herself, for the length of one held breath, to simply sit with the plain, overwhelming fact of what she'd just witnessed. She would need to report this properly, in time — the Choir's other keepers would want the full account, would want to study the new verse's exact phrasing with the same careful attention she'd have brought to any other significant addition to the Codex's long history. She would need to draft the formal notation carefully, cross-reference it against every prior account of the First Sundering currently held in the Choir's archives, and prepare herself for the considerable stir its arrival would likely cause among keepers who had built entire careers on doctrines this single verse had just quietly rendered obsolete, some of whom would not receive the news gracefully, whatever peace it had apparently brought to her own reading of it.

For now, in this quiet moment before that work properly began, she allowed herself simply to feel it: a peace complete enough that a stoic keeper's single tear seemed, for once, the only honest response available to her.

She glanced once more at the newly filled page, its gray script already settling into the same patient stillness as every other verse the Codex had ever recorded, and understood, closing her eyes briefly against it, that she would carry the memory of watching it arrive for as long as her own long existence continued. She had spent centuries studying history secondhand, careful scholarship built on records other keepers before her had already gathered and preserved. She had witnessed something today that belonged, for once, entirely to her own firsthand account, and found a quiet, private satisfaction in that distinction, one she suspected would take her a good while yet to fully understand, longer still to properly explain to keepers who hadn't stood in this exact room to see it happen.
