# B2C36 – Three Anomalies

Sariel arrived at the chamber still looking like a man who hadn't fully finished believing what he'd just watched, his usual measured composure worn thin in a way Gabriel had rarely seen from him across however many years the two of them had served together. Dust from the tunnels beneath Navarion still clung faintly to the hem of his robe, a fine gray residue he hadn't bothered brushing off before answering the summons. His hands, when he finally stilled them at his sides, still shook faintly, the ordinary tremor of a body that hadn't yet finished processing that the danger had passed. Gabriel had summoned him back from the crossing the moment his passage registered, unwilling to wait even a single ordinary hour for a report this important to reach him secondhand.

"Tell me everything," Gabriel said, gesturing him toward a seat he didn't take. "From the beginning. Don't smooth any of it for my benefit. I'd rather have the full truth than a comfortable summary."

Sariel didn't smooth it. He described the ambush beneath the university — a sealed door, a fire-wreathed portal, eight armed soldiers and the cold, efficient officer who'd commanded them, an entity Sariel described as more disciplined and considerably more dangerous than the low-ranked scouts Belial usually spent so freely. He described the words the enforcer had used, plain and chilling in their calm: two names offered up like prizes already claimed, an order to capture rather than kill. He described the two men who had stood back to back against that ambush without ever having met before that exact night, one an unarmed scholar dragged into a fight he had no training for, the other a hunter who moved through violence the way most men moved through a familiar room. He described Mikel's voice rising above the chaos with an authority Sariel didn't recognize from an ordinary man — the voice, by every measure his long service had taught him, of someone who had commanded far more than a single desperate fight in his life. He described Arin's economy in violence, precise and merciless and entirely without waste, judgment made physical in a way Sariel had only ever read about in the oldest records of this war. He described the strange, wordless synchrony that had briefly overtaken both men mid-fight, something he'd felt reach him even from his own hidden distance, unmistakable and impossible to explain properly to anyone who hadn't felt it firsthand. It had lasted only a handful of heartbeats. He'd remember the shape of it regardless, he suspected, for considerably longer than that.

"And then," Sariel said, his voice carrying a weight Gabriel had rarely heard him use for anything, "a third arrived. Ancient. His power wasn't light, and it wasn't shadow. It was pure stillness. He ended the entire conflict with a whisper." He paused, searching for words that plainly weren't coming easily to him. "I've stood witness to a great deal in my service to this council, Gabriel. I don't have anything in my own experience to properly measure what I watched happen in that corridor. I've read the old accounts of the Sundering more times than I can count. Nothing in them prepared me for actually standing near it."

---

Gabriel let the words settle fully before he finally spoke again. Two signals, one loud enough to wake half of Hell, one quiet enough to nearly escape his own council's notice entirely, had converged at last on the exact ground he'd sent Sariel to watch. And now a third, unaccounted for in every report, every fragment, every careful theory this council had built across months of half-blind investigation. He thought of every session this council had spent arguing over a single lost commander and a single dangerous anomaly, treating them as two separate problems that happened to share a season. That assumption no longer held. It was, he understood now, a considerably more embarrassing miscalculation than he'd ever have admitted to Uriel's face.

"The two anomalies I've been tracking since before the Grey City," Gabriel said, mostly to himself. "They've found each other. And something else found them both at the same moment."

"I don't have a name for him," Sariel said. "I don't think he offered one, from what little I overheard. Whatever he is, he wasn't there to be questioned. He was there to end something before it finished happening."

"Did he speak?"

"Some. I kept my distance, the way you ordered, and most of it was too quiet for me to catch cleanly. What I did hear didn't sound like evasion, Gabriel. Not the ordinary kind. It sounded more like mercy — like he was protecting them from an answer neither of them was ready to carry yet. I've heard evasion enough times to know the difference."

---

Iofiel had been standing near the chamber's old star-map through the whole of Sariel's report, and Gabriel watched her step forward now, her voice carrying less of its usual certainty than he was accustomed to hearing from her. She had spent months studying fragments most of this council had long since dismissed as unreliable, and had never once managed to prove the pattern she saw in them to anyone else's satisfaction. "Do you think it's the same figure," she asked, "the one the oldest fragments of the Codex keep circling without ever quite naming? The one neither of us has ever managed to place with any confidence?"

Gabriel considered the question with the full weight it deserved before he answered it. "I think we've spent centuries calling something a myth that was simply waiting for the right moment to stop being one." He let that sit a moment before he continued, aware of how little comfort the rest of what he had to say would offer either of them. "And I don't think we're going to enjoy the question that follows from it. How many more of our myths turn out to be exactly this?"

Iofiel didn't answer that. Gabriel hadn't expected her to. Some questions had no answer worth giving yet, and this one, he suspected, would sit unresolved over this entire council for considerably longer than either of them would have liked.

"What do we tell the rest of the council?" she asked instead, her voice steadier now, some of the old, practical Iofiel returning to it after the uncertainty of a moment ago. "Uriel will want to know why we're not treating a being of that power as an immediate threat. Zadkiel will want to know why the law never accounted for something like this. Both of them are going to want answers I don't currently have to give."

"We tell them what we actually know, and nothing we don't," Gabriel said. "That a third being intervened tonight, that he saved rather than harmed, and that neither of us can tell them with any honesty what he is or what he wants." He allowed himself a small, tired breath before he continued. "I expect that answer to satisfy exactly no one. I intend to give it anyway. This council has earned considerably less patience from me than it once had, after these last few weeks. Uriel can bring his objections to me directly, if he has any left worth bringing."

---

Gabriel compared it all against the war this council had spent so long treating as the only war worth fighting, and understood something clearly now that had only been half-formed in him for weeks. Uriel's push for open war against Hell. Zadkiel's careful, exhausted defense of a law that no longer explained half of what this world was actually producing. Both of them had been fighting a battle that belonged, Gabriel understood now, to a conflict considerably smaller than the one actually deciding this world's fate. He thought of the fractured council, the abstentions, the stalemate that had left this entire chamber unable to agree on anything beyond its own paralysis. None of that internal fracture, he understood without any comfort in the thought, was going to matter in the end.

"The real war isn't happening in this chamber," Gabriel said, half to Sariel, half to the empty air around them both. "It never really was. It's happening on Earth, in a university basement neither Uriel nor Zadkiel has ever heard the name of, between two men and a third neither of us can even properly describe yet." He looked toward the star-map's slow, patient light. "This council has been arguing about the wrong war for months. I include myself in that. I spent as long arguing the same wrong argument as anyone else in that chamber."

---

"Does this change anything about Solmire?" The voice came from near the chamber's entrance, low and careful, and Gabriel turned to find Camael standing there, silent until now, his presence in the room a quiet reminder of exactly how divided this council had grown in the days since the Rite of Questioned Command had failed to resolve anything cleanly. Camael hadn't spoken to Gabriel directly since the vote, hadn't so much as met his eyes across the chamber in any of the sessions since. The question, plain and practical as it was, was the first thing either of them had offered the other since that vote, and Gabriel found himself grateful for it in a way he hadn't expected to be.

"No," Gabriel said, meeting his gaze steadily, unwilling to let whatever distance had grown between them shape an answer this simple. "We keep watching for it the same way we decided to watch after the battlefield, patient rather than desperate. A sword that refused Belial's hand isn't going to reveal itself to panic. It never has."

"You're certain of that."

"I'm certain of very little tonight," Gabriel admitted. "I'm certain enough of that particular thing to keep saying it out loud, and I'd rather give you an honest uncertainty than a false comfort. You've earned better than comfortable lies from me, whatever else has passed between us lately."

Camael held his gaze a moment longer, something unreadable in his expression, and nodded once before he turned to go, offering nothing further to the conversation beyond the plain fact of having asked at all. Gabriel watched him leave and found he couldn't fully name what he felt at the sight of it — not reconciliation, not yet, but not the finality of the vote either.

---

Gabriel dismissed the rest of the chamber a while later, and found himself alone at last before the old star-map, its slow light tracing the same patient orbits it had traced for longer than this council had kept written records to measure it by. He found Navarion's small, unremarkable point of light without difficulty now, having studied its position more times across these last weeks than he'd studied almost anything else in this entire long war. It looked, tonight, no different than it had looked a month ago, an ordinary point among countless others, giving no outward sign of everything that had just happened there. He wondered, studying it, how many other ordinary points across this star-map held changes just as large, waiting for someone to notice them.

He didn't pray to anything larger than the map in front of him. He had learned, across centuries of careful, disciplined faith, exactly which prayers this cosmos actually answered and which simply went unheard. He had come to trust, somewhere across this long war, that a prayer worked best directed at someone close enough to actually receive it. He sent his hope now directly to the one person he trusted to receive it, unspoken and entirely without ceremony.

*Hold on, brother. Whatever you've become tonight, whatever you're still becoming. Hold on long enough for the rest of us to catch up to what you already understand.*

The star-map offered no answer. Gabriel hadn't expected one. He stood there a long while regardless, watching Navarion's small light hold its patient, unbroken place among all the others, thinking of the desert where he'd last spoken to his brother face to face, of every year since spent hoping for exactly this kind of proof. It cost him nothing to believe, standing there, that somewhere down on that small and troubled world, his brother had heard him anyway. He let himself believe it.
