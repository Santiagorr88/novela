# B2C21.6 – The Grief They Mistook for Softness

Marbas had already stripped the dead soldier's arm to the socket by the time word of Malthus reached his workshop, and he did not stop working while the messenger delivered it. The limb belonged to one of the crossroads survivors, a foot soldier whose nerve had finally given out that same night, hours after the mystery that had killed his captain, and Marbas had been rebuilding the ruined joint with a patience he reserved exclusively for problems that had a solution. Flesh, in his long professional opinion, was an amateur's design — inconsistent tolerances, no redundancy worth the name, the kind of system that failed catastrophically the instant its one load-bearing part gave out. Malthus had built his career on rituals that depended entirely on his own continued heartbeat. Marbas had built his on components that could be swapped.

"They're saying the mortal didn't even draw a blade," the messenger said, hovering at the workshop's threshold the way most of Foras's staff hovered near Marbas's tools, close enough to deliver the message, not one step closer than that. "Just power. Raw and unexplained."

"Unexplained isn't the same as unbuildable." Marbas set the new joint's final pin with a small, satisfied click, watching the fingers curl and release on command. "Give me the mortal in a room for an afternoon and I'll have a working theory. Give Malthus a mortal in a room and he'd have had a candle, a circle, and a very confident guess." He did not say it unkindly. He had genuinely liked Malthus, in the specific, limited way one colleague likes another whose methods he considers charmingly obsolete. He simply saw no reason to let the man's death interrupt an afternoon's honest work.

The messenger left without asking whether Marbas intended to attend whatever mourning the court eventually staged for the captain. Marbas noted the omission and found he did not particularly mind it. Grief was, in his considered opinion, another poorly engineered system — expensive to run, no measurable output, prone to the exact kind of catastrophic single-point failure Malthus himself had just demonstrated. He preferred to spend the same hours calculating what Malthus's death had actually changed, which was a question with an answer he could use.

Foras was weaker tonight than he had been in decades. Marbas had heard the phrase secondhand from three separate sources already, each one repeating it with what Marbas took for the particular relish of people who enjoyed watching a structure they didn't build show its first crack. He turned the fact over the way he turned over any piece of salvage, looking for the part worth keeping. A weaker commander was, among other things, a commander with less attention to spare for auditing exactly how his captains spent an afternoon. Malthus's shattered legion sat unclaimed three sectors east of Marbas's own, soldiers with no one currently signing their requisitions. Marbas had use for soldiers. He had, more precisely, use for soldiers nobody was watching closely enough to ask questions about.

---

Raum had been three hours inside another man's dream when Malthus died, and he did not learn of it until he'd finished, because Lethae did not release its subjects early simply because the waking world had grown suddenly more interesting.

The dream belonged to a foot soldier Raum had chosen for no better reason than that the man's fear ran unusually clean, easy to read, easy to bend without the muddying static most soldiers carried in their sleep. He had built, across those three hours, a small apocalypse out of the man's own private material — a battlefield the soldier recognized, populated with faces he recognized, all of it burning in a light that came from nowhere the soldier's mind could name. Raum did none of it for cruelty's own sake, whatever the wider court assumed of him. He did it because a mind that had watched its own ending, even a false one, moved differently afterward — flinched less at real fire, obeyed faster, doubted its own survival less than it doubted its commanders. He considered it, privately, the kindest cruelty available to him.

What troubled him, surfacing at last into the workshop's low light with the soldier still twitching through the dream's last minutes behind him, was not the content of the vision. It was the shape it had taken without his choosing it. He had built endings for other men a thousand times and rarely felt the material resist him. Tonight the fire in the dream had kept curling back toward a single dais, a single seated figure he had not placed there on purpose, and every path Raum tried to bend the vision away from it had simply led back around to the same still point. He did not believe, watching the soldier finally settle into ordinary sleep, that Lethae had shown him anything as clean as a prophecy. He believed something closer and less comfortable — that his own hands, without his permission, had spent three hours rehearsing an ending he hadn't consciously chosen to imagine.

He learned about Malthus an hour later, from a runner too tired to soften the delivery, and found the coincidence sat in his chest like a held breath he couldn't quite release.

---

Marbas found Raum still in the workshop, the soldier gone, the room carrying the particular hush Raum's work always left behind it — not silence exactly, but the sense of a sound recently finished. "You've heard," Marbas said, and didn't wait for confirmation. "Which means you've also already worked out what it's going to cost the rest of us if we don't move on it first."

"I've worked out that I built an ending tonight I didn't ask my own hands to build," Raum said, "and that it kept walking back toward Foras no matter which direction I sent it. I'd call that a warning, if I believed in those the way the court likes to pretend I do."

"You believe in whatever's useful to believe in at the moment you say it. So do I. That's not an insult, it's professional respect." Marbas leaned against the workbench, arms folded, the posture of a man about to propose something he'd already half-decided regardless of the answer. "Malthus's legion is sitting three sectors east with nobody's name on its requisitions. I intend to have my name on them by morning — new implants, faster obedience, soldiers who don't need a captain's charisma to hold formation because they won't have the option to disobey in the first place. It's an improvement Foras should have authorized years ago. Grief buys me the window to authorize it myself and apologize for the paperwork later."

"Foras predicted this exact conversation the same night Malthus died." Raum said it without heat, in the register Marbas had learned to expect from him, a man reporting weather rather than arguing a position. "I wasn't in the room. I don't need to have been. You're doing precisely what a grieving commander expects his hungriest captain to do the instant he looks weak enough to test — and a man who's already rehearsed the shape of a thing rarely finds himself surprised by it arriving."

"Then he'll forgive me for confirming his own good instincts." Marbas's smile carried no real warmth, but no real malice either — the expression of a craftsman who had already decided the risk was worth the yield. "I'm not going to war with him, Raum. I'm reorganizing an asset that's currently rotting from neglect. There's a considerable distance between the two, and I intend to stay entirely on the correct side of it."

"There's a considerable distance between most disasters and the reasonable-sounding first step that starts them." Raum studied him a moment, something unhurried and a little sad in the attention, the look of a man watching a scene he already knew the end of and had no real power to rewrite. "I've watched enough finales to recognize the shape of one starting badly disguised as an improvement. Do it if you're going to do it. I won't stop you, and I won't warn him first. I'd simply rather not stand close enough to be mistaken for part of the wreckage when it lands."

---

Marbas worked through what remained of the night himself rather than delegating a single hour of it, moving down the line of Malthus's surviving soldiers one at a time with the small tools he trusted more than any hand but his own. Each implant went in the same way: a shallow incision along the base of the skull, a sliver of Tekrion's plating fitted flush against bone, then the brief, unpleasant moment where the soldier's own eyes went distant and searching before they settled, blank and steady, onto whatever Marbas told them to see. He felt no particular cruelty in the work. He felt the same clean satisfaction he felt closing any circuit correctly — a system that had been leaking obedience through a dead man's absence, sealed shut, made to answer reliably to a single hand again. By the fourth soldier his own hands had found their rhythm; by the fortieth, dawn was already threading grey through the workshop's high windows, and the whole line stood in loose formation behind him, new plating fused along their forearms, their eyes carrying the faint mechanical stillness that meant the implants had already begun overriding whatever confused loyalty had been left in them since their captain's death. Marbas walked the line once, satisfied, already composing in his head the report he intended to file — efficiency, continuity of command, an asset saved rather than an authority overstepped. He had built the justification with the same care he built anything, confident it would hold weight regardless of who read it first.

Foras read it first. He arrived at the workshop's threshold without escort, without any of the ceremony Marbas half expected a confrontation to require, and studied the reprogrammed line the way he studied anything worth understanding fully before responding to it — unhurried, cataloguing, his face giving away nothing. "Forty of Malthus's soldiers," he said, "reassigned to your authority, before the man's ashes have finished settling."

"Reclaimed," Marbas said. "Not reassigned. Nobody was using them, my lord. I'd have thought efficient use of an idle asset was precisely the kind of initiative you'd want rewarded rather than questioned."

"I want captains who understand the difference between initiative and theft committed while my attention was elsewhere." Foras's voice carried none of the heat Marbas had half braced for, and somehow that absence sat considerably worse than shouting would have. "You didn't ask, because you calculated, correctly, that I was too occupied with Vepar's courtesies to notice a workshop three sectors from my own dais. That calculation was accurate. It does not make the theft smaller. It makes it considerably more deliberate."

Marbas said nothing for a moment, recalculating in real time the way he recalculated any system that had just returned an unexpected result. "What would you have me do with them, then? Hand them back to sit unclaimed until some lesser rival claims them instead, and calls it a victory over both of us?"

"You'll keep them." Foras let that land a moment before continuing, watching Marbas's calculation stall out entirely at the answer he clearly hadn't planned for. "Under my seal, filed properly, with every upgrade you perform on them logged and reported to me before it happens rather than after. You wanted the asset. You'll have it, on terms that remind you precisely whose court you built it inside of." He drew Noesis free with no more theater than he'd have used to draw breath, and pressed its point, briefly, against the back of Marbas's own hand — not deep enough to properly wound, only enough to make the point land somewhere a report couldn't soften it. Noesis had never had faith to work with in Marbas — he'd burned that particular resource down to nothing decades ago, on purpose, the way he burned anything he judged inefficient — so it took the nearest thing it could find instead. Marbas felt the cold thread of it work loose something he hadn't known was tangled there, some small private certainty about his own cleverness, and watched it drain out through the shallow cut before Foras withdrew the blade. "That's your punishment. I still need you competent more than I need you humbled."

Marbas said nothing further, and found, in the quiet that followed, that the confidence he'd walked into the confrontation with had not vanished so much as narrowed — still present, still useful, simply no longer quite so certain of its own unlimited room to maneuver.

---

Raum heard the shape of it secondhand within the hour, the way most things in the court eventually reached him, and found himself standing at the edge of Foras's hall some while later without having entirely decided to come there. Foras was alone on the dais, purple mist drifting slow around his shoulders, and looked up at Raum's arrival without any particular surprise.

"You didn't warn him," Foras said. "I already know that. I'm not here to ask why."

"I built an ending last night I didn't choose the shape of, and it kept walking back to you no matter how I turned it." Raum offered the fact plainly, the way he offered most things, without asking to be believed. "I came to see whether the shape was accurate."

"It was accurate enough." Foras studied him a long moment, something that might have been respect moving behind the stillness, though Raum would not have staked much on naming it precisely. "You saw a captain testing me and said nothing, and you did not test me yourself. That's a distinction I intend to remember, the next time I'm deciding who to trust with something that actually matters." He looked back toward the hall's far dark, unhurried, in a way Raum read as already elsewhere, in calculations of his own. "Go rest. Whatever you built last night cost you something to build. I can see it in how you're standing."

Raum inclined his head and left without further ceremony, carrying the small, uncomfortable comfort of having guessed correctly about a thing he'd never wanted to be right about in the first place.

---

Foras sat alone afterward in the hall's settling quiet, turning the whole small episode over the way he turned over anything worth keeping. Marbas had tested him, exactly as predicted, exactly as every captain eventually would in a court that ran on this kind of arithmetic. He had answered it the same way he intended to answer Vepar's own quiet insult when its hour finally came — not with noise, not with anything that could be mistaken for panic dressed up as discipline, but with a cost precise enough to be felt and small enough to leave the asset standing afterward, still useful, still his. The small rehearsal had steadied something in him he hadn't fully realized needed steadying — proof, however minor, that the patience he'd been composing for a much larger debt still worked exactly as designed when tested on a smaller one first.

He wondered, briefly and without much real curiosity, whether Vepar was doing something of the same tonight, somewhere in his own still domain — running his own small tests on his own captains, confirming the same slow patience by the same careful degrees. He would likely never know. It hardly mattered. Whatever answer Vepar eventually sent his way, Foras intended to be ready for it long before it arrived.
