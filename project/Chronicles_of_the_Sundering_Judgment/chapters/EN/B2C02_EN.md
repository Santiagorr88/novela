# B2C02 – The Ghost of the Balkans

The city had been dead for six years and still hadn't finished falling down. Arin Cross moved through it the way a man moves through a house he grew up in after the lights have gone — not blind, exactly, but trusting his feet more than his eyes, placing each step on rubble that had long since stopped surprising him. Somewhere above the shattered rooflines a bird he couldn't name called twice and went quiet, and he registered the silence after it more than the sound itself, the way he registered everything now: as an absence with a shape.

The air here tasted of concrete dust and old rain, a taste he'd stopped noticing as unpleasant years ago. It was the taste of clarity, if clarity had a flavor — a place with nothing left to pretend about, where the war had already finished arguing with itself and only the aftermath remained to be catalogued. He liked it here, in the specific, joyless way he liked anywhere the pretending had stopped. It made the work simpler.

He had been three days on the trail of one man, and the trail had narrowed, over those three days, from a name on a list to a description to a set of habits — which door, which hour, which stretch of collapsed avenue the target used because he believed, wrongly, that no one who mattered was still watching this city closely enough to notice a pattern. Arin noticed patterns. It was, at this point in his life, close to the only thing he was still reliably good at.

The trail had cost him three days to walk down to a single fixed point, and he'd spent all three of them the same way he spent every job like this — moving through the wreckage of the city's outer districts on foot, never the same route twice, cataloguing which buildings still had roofs and which had simply learned to stand without one. He didn't rush this part. Rushing a hunt was how a hunter became, eventually, the hunted, and Arin had buried enough careless men in unmarked ground to have no interest in joining them. Patience wasn't a virtue to him. It was a tool, the same as any other, and he kept it as sharp as the rest.

He found the man in the cellar of what had once been a granary, sitting on an overturned crate with a bottle in one hand and a rifle he wasn't holding properly in the other, the particular slack posture of someone who had convinced himself that six years of silence meant the debt had quietly expired. A single lantern threw long shadows up the cellar's cracked walls, and the smell down here was different from the smell above — mildew and old grain dust instead of concrete, a smell that belonged to whatever this place had been before it became a hiding hole for a man running out of places left to hide. Arin watched him from the doorway for eleven full seconds — not out of hesitation, but because a decade of habit had taught him that the eleventh second sometimes told him things the first ten didn't — and then stepped into the light.

The man's eyes came up slow, then fast, then slow again as recognition arrived and immediately tried to talk itself back down. "You're a long way from anywhere that pays you," he said, and laughed — a wet, unconvincing sound, the laugh of a man deciding, in real time, whether bravado would work better than begging. "Nobody sent you here. Nobody even remembers this place."

"I don't work for anyone who'd need to send me," Arin said. His voice came out flat, unhurried, carrying none of the heat the moment seemed to call for. "I keep my own ledger."

"Ledger." The man spat the word like it tasted bad, gathering something that might have been courage from the bottle in his hand. "You think what happened here fits in a *ledger*? You weren't here. You don't get to—"

"Your actions created a debt," Arin said, cutting through it, not louder, only more final, the way a door closing is more final than a door left open. "I am here to collect."

Something in the temperature of the cellar seemed to drop, though nothing physical had changed — no wind, no draft through the broken grating overhead. The man felt it anyway; Arin watched him feel it, watched the bravado curdle into something closer to the truth of him, which was fear, plain and unadorned, the kind that had been waiting six years for exactly this visit and had never quite believed it wouldn't come.

It ended in under four seconds, and it wasn't a fight. The man scrambled for the rifle he'd never been holding properly to begin with, and never came close to raising it. Arin didn't draw a weapon; he'd never needed one for this kind. His hands did what they had done more times than he let himself count, precise and economical and entirely without the heat of vengeance — not a punishment delivered in anger, but a correction, applied the way a tool is applied, because the thing in front of him had been out of alignment with something larger than either of them for six years and someone, eventually, always came to true it back. There was no wasted motion in it, no flourish, nothing that could be mistaken for satisfaction taken in the doing. When it was finished there was no triumph in his chest, either — no relief, no release, nothing that the word *justice* usually promised and almost never delivered. There was only the particular, familiar quiet of a task completed exactly as it needed to be, and the sound of his own breathing, unhurried, in a cellar that had gone very still around a debt that no longer existed to be collected.

---

He was halfway up the broken stairs to the street when he heard footsteps behind him — light, uneven, someone who didn't know how to move quietly and wasn't trying to. He turned. A woman stood in the granary's ruined doorway, thin in the way this whole city was thin, her arms wrapped around herself against a cold that had nothing to do with the weather. She'd clearly been watching from somewhere. She looked at him the way people in this city had learned to look at anyone with the bearing of a soldier — unsure whether gratitude or fear was the safer bet, hedging toward neither.

"Is it done?" she asked, and her voice shook on the second word.

"It's done."

"Am I—" She stopped, started again, quieter. "Are we safe now?"

Arin considered the question longer than its size warranted, because he owed her, if nothing else, an answer that wasn't a lie dressed up as comfort. "From him, yes." He held her eyes so she'd know he meant it, and so she'd know what came next was true in exactly the same measure. "From the rest of the world, that I can't promise you."

She nodded, slowly, like she'd expected exactly that answer and was grateful anyway to hear it spoken plainly instead of softened. She didn't thank him. He hadn't expected her to. He watched her go back the way she'd come, and stood a moment longer in the doorway of a granary that had stopped storing anything but grief a long time ago, and thought that it was, of everything he'd said in this city in three days, the only sentence that had cost him anything to say.

---

The emptiness came for him on the walk back through the rubble, the way it always did, on schedule, indifferent to how clean the work had been. He had expected — once, years ago, before he'd learned better — that this part would feel like something. Satisfaction. Relief. The particular closure a man is supposed to feel when a debt gets paid in full. Instead there was only a hollow at the center of him, precise in its dimensions, shaped like something he had never held and somehow still missed with his whole body — a weight his right hand kept almost-remembering and never quite reaching, an ache located not in his chest but lower, in his arm, in the specific muscle memory of a grip around a shaft that had never once, in this life, actually been there.

He flexed his fingers against nothing and felt, distantly, beneath the ordinary ache, something else — a resonance, faint and ugly, sorrow twisted hard enough into something that wore the shape of pride. It came from very far away. It always did, lately, more often than it used to. He didn't know what it was. He knew only that it felt, in some way he couldn't yet articulate, like the actual reason he did any of this — that the men on his list were never really the point, only the nearest available shape for a purpose he hadn't found the true target of yet.

---

He found the kind of bar this city still had — half-lit, half-empty, the other half of both quantities occupied by men drinking with the specific dedication of people who'd run out of better ways to spend an evening. Arin took a seat at the end of the counter and ordered nothing he intended to finish. He wasn't here for the drink. He was here because after work like tonight's, sitting alone in a room with four bare walls felt worse than sitting alone in a room with other people in it.

The man two stools down was already well past sober, the loose, philosophical drunk of someone with nowhere urgent to be and every reason not to want to be sober enough to remember it. He'd been a soldier once — it showed in the way he still sat, spine straight even slumped, eyes tracking the door out of habit long after the danger that had taught him to do it was gone. He glanced at Arin twice before he spoke, the second glance longer than the first.

"You've got that look," the veteran said, not quite to him, not quite to the room.

"What look."

"The one where you did something today you can't put down yet." He turned the glass in his hand, not drinking from it, just turning it, watching the light move through what was left inside. "You ever get the feeling you've done this before? Not the war. Not — soldiering, generally. I mean *this*. This exact thing. Like you're not remembering a memory, you're remembering a repeat."

Arin didn't answer right away. "Everyone repeats themselves in a war," he said finally. "That's most of what war is."

"That's not what I mean." The veteran's voice had lost some of its looseness, sharpened by something closer to conviction than drink usually left behind. "I don't mean déjà vu. I mean it's like remembering something that never actually happened to *me*. Like the memory belongs to somebody else and I'm just — carrying it. Renting space for it." He laughed, short and humorless. "Sounds insane, saying it out loud."

Arin said nothing at all. Somewhere behind his sternum, in the same hollow that never quite closed, something shifted its weight — not pain, not quite recognition either, something in between the two that he had no word for and no intention of reaching for one tonight. It was the first time, he realized, that another human being had put language to the exact shape of what he'd been carrying for longer than he could properly measure, and had done it without knowing they'd done it at all.

The veteran didn't seem to need an answer. He went back to turning his glass, and Arin sat beside him a while longer, not drinking, saying nothing, letting the question hang unresolved in the space between them — the same way, he thought, that everything about himself had been hanging unresolved for as long as he could remember being anyone at all.
