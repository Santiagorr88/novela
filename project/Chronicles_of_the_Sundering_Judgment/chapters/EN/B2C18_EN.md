# B2C18 – The Path of Laughter

The long drive back from the coast felt shorter than the journey out had, though Mikel knew well enough that the distance hadn't changed — only the shape of what he was carrying home with him. He kept the notebook on the passenger seat within easy reach the entire way, the inscription's careful copy safe inside it, and found himself, more than once, glancing over at it at red lights the way a man checks on something he isn't yet convinced will still be there the next time he looks.

He had left the mainland searching for a weapon. He returned searching for something he had no proper name for yet, only a shape borrowed from an anvil's worn inscription — *I cannot be claimed by wrath, only by laughter.* It was not a smaller question than the one he'd left with. If anything, it felt considerably larger, an entire register of human and divine experience he had spent his whole career treating as beneath serious historical inquiry, now sitting squarely at the center of everything he needed to understand.

He thought of every lecture he'd ever given on the mechanics of judgment in the ancient world — verdicts, punishments, the careful architecture societies built to decide who deserved what. He had never once, in nine years of teaching, devoted a single class session to joy as a serious historical category, the same way no serious military historian devoted a lecture to laughter on a battlefield. It had never occurred to him that the omission itself might be the mistake, that an entire register of human experience could simply be filed away as unserious by scholars too committed to gravity to notice what they'd left out.

---

He returned to his university office two days later and found it exactly as he'd left it, dust settled thin across a desk he'd abandoned mid-thought weeks ago, and began, almost immediately, tearing his own research apart and rebuilding it from an angle he'd never once considered legitimate before Turein. He abandoned the military history entirely, at least for now, and opened a search that would have looked, to any colleague glancing over his shoulder, like the beginning of a complete professional collapse — trickster gods, holy fools, laughing buddhas, mercurial smiths who forged with mischief instead of gravity. He cross-referenced traditions that had never once been placed in conversation with each other in any serious academic context he could find, searching for the single thread that might connect divinity, creation, and unguarded joy.

The work consumed him in a way even the sword's darker mythology never had. He caught himself, more than once, forgetting entire meals, surfacing from an afternoon's reading only when the light through his office window had shifted enough to startle him into checking the time. It was obsessive in a way he recognized, distantly, as unhealthy, and he found he no longer had much interest in stopping simply because it was unhealthy.

He built a working list on the back page of his notebook, adding to it every evening as the day's reading yielded some new fragment worth keeping — a coyote spirit who taught by breaking rules rather than enforcing them, a laughing buddha whose belly held the weight of every burden his followers set down in his presence, a mercurial smith from a tradition three continents removed from Turein's own who forged, according to the surviving fragments, "with a hammer that never once struck out of anger." The pattern held wherever he looked for it, faint but unmistakable, connecting traditions that had never once been asked to speak to each other before. He filled four pages with names and fragments before the week was through, and understood, reading back over the list, that he was no longer chasing a single myth. Whether these scattered, unrelated figures were echoes of one single thing or simply humanity reaching for the same comfort by a dozen separate roads, he wasn't yet prepared to say. The list alone was enough to keep him reading.

---

The archivist found him there on the fourth evening, arriving with what Mikel took to be the pretense of returning a book he hadn't actually requested, and stood in his doorway a long moment before saying what she seemed to have come to say.

"This isn't comparative folklore anymore, Ardon." She said it without her usual dry humor, her curiosity plain enough now that she'd stopped bothering to disguise it as professional interest. "This is an obsession with one very specific thing. Are you ever going to tell me what it actually is?"

Mikel looked up from the sprawl of open books across his desk, and found, for the first time in this entire strange and lengthening ordeal, that he didn't immediately reach for a deflection. "I don't know how to explain it yet myself," he said, "without it sounding like I need a different kind of help than a librarian can give me. Give me a little longer to be sure before I try."

She laughed at that, but carefully, the particular laugh of someone who has heard the honesty underneath a joke and isn't quite sure yet what to do with it. "When you do know," she said, "you already know where to find me." She didn't press further. Instead she set the book she'd brought down on the corner of his desk, spine facing him, and tapped it once. "This is the third time this month you've had me pulling cross-references on local folklore collections nobody's touched in decades. There's an uncatalogued regional set in the sub-basement, filed under 'miscellaneous,' that's never made it into the digital system at all. If whatever you're chasing has roots anywhere near here, it's more likely to be sitting down there than in anything you'll find searching properly indexed material." She left him to his sprawling, obsessive research without another word about it, the book's spine still facing him from the corner of the desk.

He sat with the exchange a while after she'd gone, dwelling on the strange, unfamiliar lightness of having answered a question honestly instead of simply managing it away. It was a small thing, in the end. It did not feel small at all.

---

The trail that finally moved him forward arrived exactly where she'd told him to look. He spent two full days working through the sub-basement's uncatalogued shelves before he found it, a slim collection of local legends filed under a heading he'd walked past a hundred times without ever once stopping to read it properly: *Folktales of the Navarion Valley.*

The stories inside described a wandering musician who appeared, in one guise or another, throughout centuries of local record — a figure whose laughter, the tales insisted, could mend what grief had broken, whose songs carried a strange power to coax life back into fields that should have long since failed. One account, dated nearly a century back, described a farmer who had lost his entire harvest to blight, and who swore afterward that a stranger with a guitar had sat with him one whole night on the ruined field, playing nothing in particular, until something in the man's chest simply stopped aching long enough for him to sleep. Another, older still, spoke of a plague year in which the sick who heard the musician's songs through their fevers reported dreams free of the terror the rest of the town endured nightly. The academic tradition had filed him away as a beloved local quirk, a piece of regional color no serious folklorist had ever thought worth cross-referencing against anything larger.

Mikel read every surviving fragment twice, his pulse climbing steadily with each new detail, and found himself circling a single, stunned realization he didn't yet have the nerve to say aloud. Every account agreed on one detail without exception: the musician never once appeared angry, no matter how dark the circumstance that had drawn him. He wondered, turning the last fragment over in his hands, whether he had finally found the same laughing, ungovernable presence the inscription on Turein had promised him existed — not a myth confined to one impossible island, but something considerably larger, and considerably closer, than he had let himself imagine.

---

The collection had been sitting, uncatalogued in any meaningful sense, inside his own university's archive the entire time. He sat back from the desk, the full weight of that specific coincidence settling into him slowly, and thought of Turein — the ancient forge, the impossible sea crossing, the whole mythic weight of a search he'd assumed could only be answered somewhere far from anything as mundane as his own daily walk to work.

He had always kept the two halves of his life carefully separate in his own mind: the ordinary scholar who lectured on medieval sieges, and the man haunted by dreams he couldn't place, chasing a mystery that felt, by its very nature, like it belonged somewhere else entirely. Sitting here now with the Navarion folktales spread open in front of him, he understood, for the first time, that the separation might not be as clean as he'd always assumed. This particular thread, at least, hadn't required an ocean crossing to find. It had been sitting in his own archive, filed under a heading he'd walked past a hundred times.

He sat with that thought long enough for the office around him to grow properly dark, the campus outside his window settling into the particular hush it took on once the evening classes let out and the last stragglers finally drifted toward home. He had spent a small fortune and no small measure of his own safety crossing an ocean to stand in front of a cold forge on an island that barely wanted to be found. He wondered, not without a certain bleak humor, how much more of this search might turn out to be closer to home than he'd assumed, and how much of it would still demand he go looking considerably farther before it was through. He didn't yet know which this local thread would turn out to be. He only knew it was worth chasing further before he decided.

---

He worked later than he meant to that night, and left the archive only once the building's other lights had already gone dark around him, the folktales' scattered fragments still occupying most of his attention as he crossed the quiet campus toward the faculty parking beyond it. The night had turned cool enough that his breath showed faintly under the walkway lamps, and the campus at this hour belonged almost entirely to him, the ordinary daytime bustle stripped away to leave only empty benches and the distant hum of traffic somewhere beyond the gates.

A street musician had set up near the main gate anyway, guitar case open in front of him, playing something low and unhurried to an audience of essentially no one at this hour. Mikel had passed buskers at that same gate a hundred times before without giving any of them a second thought, and tonight was, on its surface, no different from any of the others.

Mikel walked past without truly registering him at all, too deep in his own thoughts to spare more than the vague, unconscious awareness a man gives to background noise. He did not see the musician's fingers still on the strings for one full second as he passed, the song catching and holding on a single unresolved note before picking back up exactly where it had left off. He did not see the look that crossed the musician's face either, gone again almost before it had fully arrived — unreadable, brief, and entirely wasted on a man who never once turned to notice it.

Mikel reached his car without incident, and drove home with the Navarion folktales still occupying most of his mind, giving the musician at the gate no further thought at all once he'd passed him.
