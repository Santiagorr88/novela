# B1T03 – The Riddles With No Answer

## The Direct Path

Cassiel returned to Turein three days after his first uncertain crossing, better provisioned this time and considerably more prepared for the island's strange geography, and made straight for the clearing where he had found the resonance before, trusting his own memory of the route over anything Silens might tell him along the way. The path looked familiar for the first quarter hour, the same spiraling undergrowth he remembered from his last visit, the same shifting quality to the light overhead that no longer surprised him the way it once had.

He noticed the wrongness only once the terrain around him had already begun repeating itself — the same fallen log crossing his path a second time, then a third, each encounter identical down to the particular angle of moss along its upper surface. Cassiel stopped walking and studied the log a long moment, testing whether his own memory might simply be mistaken about how many times he had actually passed this exact point. It was not mistaken. He had walked, by his own careful count, in a complete circle, arriving back at nearly the same spot he had started from despite having moved, as far as he could tell, in a single consistent direction the entire time.

He crouched beside the log and examined it more closely, searching for some detail that might distinguish this encounter from the two before it — a fresh mark, a subtle shift in the surrounding growth, anything that would confirm his own count had simply erred somewhere along the way. He found nothing. The moss lay in precisely the pattern he remembered from twenty minutes earlier, undisturbed by his own passage in a way that struck him, the longer he studied it, as considerably stranger than the repetition itself. Ordinary terrain bore the marks of a man's passing. This bore none at all, as though his own footsteps across it had somehow failed to register.

He tried the path again immediately after, more out of stubbornness than any real expectation of a different outcome, and found himself back at the same log within a quarter hour, the count now standing at four identical encounters against a single unbroken line of travel. Whatever governed this particular stretch of ground, it plainly did not respond to simple persistence. He would need, he understood, an entirely different approach than the one that had served him across every other assignment of his long career.

## What the Island Actually Guarded

He sat with the failure a while before attempting the path again, turning the problem over with the same unhurried patience he brought to any wounded soul Silens ever led him toward. Turein guarded whatever it guarded with no creature Cassiel had yet encountered, no physical barrier blocking any route he had tried. It offered instead exactly what his first failed crossing had just demonstrated — paths that folded back on themselves, echoes of half-remembered laughter that led, whenever he chased them with any real urgency, straight into dead ends that hadn't existed moments before. Riddles, he thought, turning the word over carefully. Not guardians. Riddles with no answer he had yet found, and perhaps none waiting to be found at all in the way he understood the term.

He recalled, sitting there, how badly his own urgency had cost him on his first visit, the disorientation that had nearly cost him his bearings entirely. He had assumed, afterward, that the lesson had already been learned. Sitting now beside a log he had circled three times without noticing, he understood that the lesson evidently required repeating.

He thought, too, of every echo of laughter he had caught drifting through the undergrowth since he first set foot on the island this morning, faint and fossilized in the fabric of the place the same way it had been on his first visit. He had chased one of them briefly, an hour ago, curiosity overriding his own better judgment for exactly long enough to lead him into a dead end that had not existed, as far as he could tell, moments before he arrived at it. He did not chase the next one he heard. Whatever answered that particular kind of urgency here, it plainly was not interested in rewarding it.

He allowed himself, sitting beside the log, a rare moment of genuine uncertainty about whether he was even the right person for this particular assignment. Other Captains under Orifiel's command might have brought sharper instincts to a puzzle like this one, faster reflexes, some quality better suited to solving a riddle through force of will alone. He had never been especially quick. He had only ever been patient, and patience, so far, had earned him nothing here beyond four identical encounters with the same fallen log.

## Reading the Cadence

He rose eventually and tried the path again, deliberately slower now, abandoning any effort to hold a straight bearing in favor of simply attending to what the island itself seemed to be doing around him. The spiraling growth pattern, he noticed on this slower pass, followed a rhythm he could almost anticipate if he stopped trying to walk through it and instead let his own pace bend to match it — not resistance, exactly, but a kind of patient accommodation, the same quality of attention he brought to a soul too disoriented by its own death to be guided anywhere by force.

The path opened differently under that patience, small variations he had missed entirely on his first rushed attempt now visible at the edges of his awareness — a break in the undergrowth here, a shift in the quality of light there, each one a small, quiet invitation to continue rather than an obstacle to overcome. He followed them without hurrying, and found, for the first time since his initial crossing, that the ground beneath him actually seemed to be leading somewhere rather than simply circling back on itself.

He passed the same fallen log a fifth time roughly an hour later, and felt, examining it once more, a small measure of satisfaction rather than the frustration the sight had provoked earlier in the day. This time he recognized a detail he had missed on every previous pass — a narrow gap in the undergrowth just beyond the log's far end, easy to overlook at any pace faster than the one he was now keeping, that had not been visible to him at all during his earlier, hurried attempts. He took the gap without hesitation, and did not encounter the log again.

The terrain beyond it opened more generously than anything he had walked through so far, the spiraling growth pattern loosening by degrees the deeper he went, as though the island's resistance eased in direct proportion to how little he tried to force his own pace against it. He tested the theory deliberately at one point, quickening his stride for no more than a dozen steps to see whether the ground would punish the impatience the same way it had punished his first attempt. It did. The path ahead blurred subtly, some quality he could not quite name suggesting he was about to lose his bearings again, and he slowed immediately, watching the same subtle wrongness recede the moment he did.

He walked the remainder of the afternoon at exactly the pace the island seemed to require of him, testing the boundary occasionally and finding it consistent every time he tested it. Patience, whatever else it demanded of him, was at least a lesson he could apply reliably once he understood its terms.

## The Right Kind of Patience

It occurred to him, walking this slower path, that his own temperament might be the reason Turein had not simply turned him away entirely on his first visit the way it apparently turned away anyone moving with real urgency. He had built his entire service around exactly this kind of unhurried attention, the patience most of his fellow Captains seemed to find tedious in him, the willingness to sit with a wounded soul for an hour rather than rush it toward wherever it needed to go. Turein, whatever else it actually was, seemed to reward precisely that quality and punish its absence with the same indifferent consistency.

He did not consider himself especially suited to grand assignments, had never once sought the kind of glory he associated with the more aggressive colleagues who pursued it across open battlefields. Walking Turein's slow, spiraling paths now, he wondered whether that particular temperament, so often overlooked in favor of louder virtues, might be exactly what a place like this had been quietly waiting to test.

## What Selm Tried Instead

Selm, still watching from a careful distance he had maintained since the previous night, grew tired of a different kind of patience somewhere past midday — not the hunter's patience that had carried him through two days above a ridge waiting for a single clean shot, but the passive, purposeless waiting this assignment had reduced him to, with no decisive moment building toward anything he could actually act on. He decided, against his own better judgment, to test the terrain himself rather than simply continue observing the scout's slow progress. He chose a direct line toward the same interior the scout was clearly working his way toward, trusting speed and directness the way he had trusted them across centuries of exactly this kind of terrain, certain his own instincts would carry him through whatever obstacle the island actually presented.

The obstacle, when it came, arrived without any warning worth the name — the same path repeating itself beneath his feet, a stand of trees he was certain he had already passed reappearing directly in front of him a second time, then a third. Selm stopped, genuinely startled by the repetition in a way few things had managed to startle him across his long service, and reassessed the ground with the same flat professionalism he brought to any failed approach. Brute persistence, whatever its usual value against ordinary terrain, had accomplished nothing here beyond returning him, almost exactly, to where he had started.

He tried a different bearing next, angling sharply away from his original line in case the terrain's resistance was somehow tied to the specific direction he had chosen rather than his manner of moving through it. The same stand of trees greeted him within the quarter hour regardless, patient and unmoving in a way that told him, more clearly than anything else so far, that direction had never been the actual variable at issue. He stood a long moment weighing his own frustration against the accumulating evidence, unwilling yet to concede that the terrain itself had bested him but running short on alternative approaches worth trying.

He considered, briefly, simply cutting a path through the undergrowth by force, trusting raw destruction to succeed where careful navigation had not. He abandoned the idea almost as quickly as it arrived. Whatever governed this ground had already demonstrated, twice now, that it did not respond to speed or aggression in any way he recognized, and he saw no particular reason destruction would fare any better than the direct approach already had.

## Converging Without Knowing

He forced himself, reluctantly, into something closer to the scout's own unhurried pace, testing whether patience where speed had failed might actually yield different results. It did, marginally, the terrain growing slightly more cooperative the less he fought against its own strange rhythm, though the concession cost him more discipline than he cared to admit even to himself. He knew how to wait for an opening. He had never once needed to simply keep pace with ground that offered him nothing decisive to wait for at all, and found that particular absence considerably more unsettling than any physical danger the island might have presented instead.

He caught himself, twice, drifting back toward his own natural stride without meaning to, some old instinct reasserting itself the moment his attention wandered even slightly from the discipline the terrain demanded of him. Both times the ground answered with the same subtle blurring Selm had encountered during his own failed approaches, and both times he forced himself back into the slower rhythm before the wrongness could compound into anything worse. It was, he reflected grimly, an uncomfortable kind of lesson to be learning this late in his service — that the ground ahead answered only to a patience with no target and no opening to wait for, the one variety of stillness his entire career had never once required of him before.

Cassiel remained unaware of the hunter behind him, moving with his own patient, deliberate care somewhere ahead. Selm, though conscious of the scout's position as he had been since the previous night, did not understand that Cassiel had arrived, by an entirely separate path and for entirely separate reasons, at the exact same hard-won rhythm the island seemed to demand of anyone who wished to move through it at all. Both of them kept the same slow cadence through Turein's spiraling terrain that evening, moving steadily closer to whatever waited at the island's center, neither aware that the other had learned the same lesson from it.

The light overhead shifted again as the evening wore on, moving through its usual strange colors, a phenomenon Selm had already learned to simply expect rather than question further. Whatever waited ahead, it was drawing closer at exactly this same unhurried pace, one measured step at a time.
