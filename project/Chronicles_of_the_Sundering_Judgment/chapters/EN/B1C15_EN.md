# B1C15 – A Fissure in the Light

## The Emergency Council

Gabriel had called the Council within hours of the scout's return, and he watched the chamber fill now with the particular grimness of people who appeared to know, in outline, what they had gathered to discuss. Belial had beaten them to the tomb. Lament was no longer a rumor in the Codex Mortalis — it was a weapon sitting in an enemy's hand, capable, in theory, of matching Solmire itself. The question in front of them was no longer whether the weapon existed. It was what to do about the fact that it already did.

Uriel spoke first, as Gabriel had known he would, his voice carrying the particular heat of a man who seemed to treat hesitation as its own kind of failure. "Every hour we wait is an hour Belial spends learning to wield it," he said, both hands flat against the Great Table of Covenant as though he might will the room into motion through sheer contact with the stone. "Strike now, before he masters it, and we face a dangerous weapon in unpracticed hands. Wait, and we face it in hands that know exactly what they're doing." Raphael answered from across the table, quieter but sounding no less certain in his own register. "And if we strike before we understand what that weapon actually does, we hand him a battlefield demonstration of its full power at our own expense," he said. "Caution isn't the same as paralysis, Uriel. We don't even know what Lament costs to wield, or what it does to the one who wields it."

Gabriel let the exchange run its familiar course, the same two positions they had argued through since long before this particular crisis, and found his own attention drifting, against his will, toward the chair that remained conspicuously empty at the table's head.

He had convened enough of these gatherings by now to recognize the particular rhythm this one had already fallen into, the same two arguments returning in slightly different clothes over a dozen different crises. It rarely resolved anything cleanly. It did, at least, force every angle of a problem into the open before a decision hardened around it, and Gabriel had learned to value that much even when the argument itself grew tiresome to referee. What troubled him today was not the argument. It was how little of his attention he actually had left to give it.

The scout's report sat closed on the table in front of him, a thin folio he had already read twice before the Council convened. Belial had reached the tomb first. The guardian that had turned Belial away on his own first attempt had, by the second, simply let him pass — some threshold of understanding met, some price paid that the report could only speculate about from a careful distance. Lament was gone from the Weeping Sepulcher and gone, now, from any hand but his. Gabriel had read the details three times over and still felt no closer to understanding what, precisely, that meant for the war they were all still fighting.

Zadkiel's voice cut through the argument before it could sharpen further into something less productive, the same measured, procedural weight Gabriel remembered from the Council that had first authorized the exploration itself. "Before either of you commits this body to a decision," Zadkiel said, "I want it formally noted that we are deliberating a war footing in the continued absence of our field commander." His silver eyes moved once around the table, settling nowhere in particular, judgment radiating from the look itself rather than from any accusation in his tone. "Not as a rebuke. As a fact this Council will need on record, whatever we decide today."

No one answered him directly. Gabriel felt the weight of the observation land where Zadkiel appeared to have intended it to, a formality that was not really a formality at all. He was still searching for some way to address it himself when the chamber doors opened, and the conversation the Council had been having about Miguel's absence became, all at once, a conversation it no longer needed to have.

Miguel crossed the threshold like a man walking through water, each step arriving a fraction slower than it should have, his hand closed hard around Solmire's hilt in a grip that looked less like readiness than dependence. Gabriel had not seen him this gaunt since the earliest days after the sword's forging, hollows carved beneath his eyes that no amount of rest seemed able to fill anymore. His gaze swept the chamber as he approached the table, catching on nothing and no one for more than a moment, as though he were tracking something in the room that the rest of them could not see.

Gabriel rose halfway from his seat, some instinct toward his brother overriding the Council's business entirely for a moment, and then sat back down at Miguel's small, sharp shake of the head. Whatever this was, Miguel seemed intent on enduring it standing, and Gabriel had learned, across too many months now, when to let him.

It had not always been this way between them. Gabriel could still remember, with an ease that felt almost cruel now, a version of Miguel who would have crossed the chamber himself to sit at Gabriel's side before any Council business began, who would have made some low, private joke meant for no one but his brother before turning to face whatever the room demanded of him. That version of Miguel had been retreating by inches for longer than Gabriel liked to admit, each retreat small enough on its own to explain away, until the sum of them had become this — a man standing at the head of his own war council, surrounded by allies, and utterly, visibly alone.

No one else in the chamber moved to greet him either. Uriel gave him the barest nod, already half-returned to his own argument. Raphael's eyes lingered a beat longer, as though he were making some private assessment he did not share aloud. Zadkiel alone met Miguel's arrival without any adjustment to his own bearing at all, watching him take his place with the same even, unhurried attention he had given every other detail of the meeting so far.

## The Echo of Laughter

Uriel resumed his argument as though the interruption had cost him nothing, laying out Belial's likely movements with the same relentless precision he brought to every strategic assessment, and Miguel tried, for a while, to follow the shape of it. The words kept arriving a half-second delayed, oddly distant even at this range, and beneath them, faint but unmistakable, Miguel heard it again — the low, patient laughter that had chased him through every restless hour since the forge-dream first found him. It had never fully left. It had only been waiting for a silence quiet enough to be heard in.

He fixed his eyes on Uriel and, for one impossible instant, saw something else laid over the archangel's familiar features — a flicker of amusement that did not belong to Uriel at all, a faceless smith's mouth curling at the edges as though the whole Council, the whole desperate strategy session, were some private joke only the smith found genuinely funny. Miguel blinked, and Uriel's face was only Uriel's face again, mid-sentence, showing no sign that anything had happened. His pulse hammered against his own ribs. No one else in the chamber appeared to have seen anything at all.

He gripped Solmire's hilt tighter, willing the sword's familiar hum to drown out whatever this was, the way it had drowned out smaller doubts before. It offered him nothing this time, silent and inert against his palm, no comfort in its usual quiet certainty. He was alone with the laughter and the borrowed smile both, and no amount of gripping the hilt seemed able to change that. He tried, for a moment, to fix his attention on the actual words still moving around the table — casualty projections, a proposed staging ground, the ordinary machinery of war he had spent centuries fluent in — and found the words sliding past him regardless, thin and distant against the much louder thing crowding the inside of his own skull.

Somewhere beneath the panic, a colder thought surfaced, one he wanted gone and couldn't shake. This was the same laughter from the dream that had first found him weeks ago, the same voice that had left him doubting whatever certainty Solmire's forging had once given him. He had told no one about it, had turned Gabriel away at his own door rather than explain it, had almost gone to Raphael and stopped himself out of something that felt, in the moment, indistinguishable from shame. Sitting with it now, in front of the entire Council, he understood exactly how much that silence had cost him. It had cost him this — a private horror with nowhere left to go except straight through the only thing in the room solid enough to break.

"Silence," Miguel said, low at first, almost swallowed by Uriel's ongoing argument. When no one stopped, when the laughter kept threading through every word still being spoken around him, he said it again, louder, and then a third time with his fist already falling. "*Silence!*"

The blow landed on the Great Table of Covenant with a sound like the chamber itself had cracked open. A spiderweb of fissures raced outward from the point of impact, splitting the pristine marble that had stood unblemished through every Council this hall had ever hosted, and the entire room fell into a silence far more total than the one Miguel had been demanding. There was no command left in him by the time the blow landed, nothing that resembled the voice of a field commander at all — only rage with nowhere left to go, grief that had run out of quieter shapes to wear, spending itself against the one surface in the room solid enough to answer it.

## What Raphael Already Knew

Raphael watched the cracks spread across the table's surface, and felt something settle into place inside him that was worse, in its own way, than what looked like shock rippling through the rest of the Council. He had voiced his own quiet doubt about Solmire's nature once before, back in the earliest days after its forging, when doubt had still felt like an indulgence rather than a warning, and he suspected that none of them, himself included, had truly wanted to sit with the answer long enough to pursue it. He had let the question go unspoken since, filed away under things too inconvenient to raise twice. Watching Miguel's fist come down now, watching the man buckle under a weight none of the rest of them could fully see, Raphael understood that he had not been wrong to ask it. He had only been wrong to stop.

He said nothing. There was no need to remind anyone in this chamber what he had once wondered aloud, and even less use in it now, with the damage already spreading across the table in front of them exactly the way his old doubt had once threatened it might. He only watched Miguel, his own horror tangled through with something colder and more familiar: confirmation of a fear he'd carried since Solmire's forging, arriving years too late to do anyone any good.

He thought, briefly, of every small moment since then where he might have raised the question again and chosen not to — a healer's habit, he supposed, of tending to the wound already in front of him rather than the one still gathering somewhere out of sight. He had told himself, each time, that the moment was not the right one, that Miguel carried enough already without one more voice questioning the gift that had made him what he was. Watching the cracks now spreading across the Great Table of Covenant, Raphael understood there had never been a right moment coming. There had only been this one, arrived without his permission, exactly as late as it had always been going to be.

## The Unraveling

Miguel did not stay to explain himself. He turned from the fractured table and tore out of the chamber without a backward glance, his stride nothing like the slow, careful entrance he had made only minutes before, leaving behind a Council that appeared too stunned to immediately follow and a wound in the room's oldest piece of furniture that no one moved to address. Gabriel rose fully this time, though he made no move toward the door his brother had just gone through, some older instinct telling him that whatever pursuit Miguel might need right now, it was not the kind any of them in this chamber could offer him.

He crossed instead to the table itself and knelt beside it, tracing one splintered line with two fingers, feeling the rough, broken edge where smooth marble had been only minutes before. The stone had held through every crisis this Council had ever weathered, through arguments louder and angrier than anything raised here today, through decades of disagreement that had never once left a mark on it. It had taken Miguel's single blow to finally split it. The damage felt, to Gabriel, like something far larger than a table — a mark of exactly how deep the fracture in his brother actually ran, forced now into plain view where the whole Council had to reckon with it together. Uriel and Raphael had gone quiet behind him, their argument abandoned along with everything else the meeting had originally intended to resolve. Zadkiel's silence, when Gabriel finally glanced back at him, showed no sign of satisfaction at having been proven right. It only carried the plain, settled weight of a Council that now had two things on the record instead of one — the absence Zadkiel had asked them to note before Miguel arrived, and the ruin they had all just watched him leave behind once he had. Whatever signs of quiet concern the Council had shown until today, Gabriel understood, standing over the broken table, that the time for keeping it quiet had just run out.
