# B3C28.6 – What No One Came Back For

Naamah kept her own accounts personally, a habit most of Hell's court found beneath a Duchess and one she had stopped bothering to explain to anyone whose opinion mattered to her keeping it. Numbers didn't flatter. They didn't perform for her the way people did, didn't reshape themselves around what she wanted to hear, and she had learned early that the surest way to catch a lie was to compare it against a ledger nobody had thought to lie to. She was reviewing the week's requisitions, three days back from the Valley's edge and still pleased with how cleanly Vepar's month had folded into her own account of it, when a line near the bottom of a supply manifest stopped her hand.

*Authorization: Commander Aamon.*

She read it twice, the way she read anything that didn't belong. Aamon had been dead longer than some of her own captains had been alive — a name she associated, when she associated it with anything at all, with the shape of a story Belial liked to have retold at court on nights he wanted to be reminded of his own cleverness. And yet here it sat, two books and however many years later, still drawing rations against a dead commander's seal, as though the war's paperwork had simply forgotten to notice he'd stopped being able to sign anything.

"This," she said, holding the manifest out without looking up, "is either a very old mistake, or a very old habit nobody's had a reason to break."

Gusion took it from her, unhurried, the way a man behaves when he already half-suspects he'll enjoy the answer. He read it in the time it took her to pour herself a glass she had no real intention of drinking, *Veritas Inversa* tapping once against the parchment's edge the way it did whenever a document interested him more than its contents alone would suggest. "Neither, I'd guess," he said. "A commander dies, his legion gets folded into whoever inherits the loyalty rolls, or it gets disbanded outright and the soldiers reassigned. Someone does the paperwork either way — it's the one part of dying that's never actually optional." He turned the manifest over, found nothing on its back, and set it down with the faint distaste of a man discovering a contract missing its final clause. "Unless the throne would rather not admit, on the record, that an entire legion simply kept existing without anyone claiming it. That's not a mistake, Duchess. That's an embarrassment nobody's found it worth the paperwork to correct."

"And an embarrassment," Naamah said, "is very rarely the same thing as a loss." She had already begun turning the thought toward its far more interesting shape — not who had failed to notice a dead man's soldiers still drawing supply, but what exactly those soldiers had been doing, unsupervised, for however many years it had taken Hell's court to stop pretending it would eventually sort them out. "Two captains, if the old rolls still hold. Find out where they've been keeping themselves."

Gusion's smile was the smile of a man who had already half-guessed the answer before he'd been asked to find it. "I imagine considerably closer than anyone's bothered to check."

---

The Cinder Watch had never been anything worth a name on a proper map — a border post raised, some forgotten age ago, to hold a line against a threat long since resolved one way or the other, and never formally decommissioned because no one above a certain rank had ever cared enough to sign the order. Naamah found it exactly as she'd expected to: a squat fortification of black stone gone soft-edged with age, banners faded past recognizing whose colors they'd once carried, garrisoned by soldiers who moved with the loose, unhurried discipline of men who had stopped expecting inspection sometime around the second decade of being forgotten. Weeds had found their way up through the drilling yard's cracked flagstones and nobody had thought it worth the labor of pulling them. No mirrors here worth the trouble of using, no court to reshape a room's expectations for. She let Neriah settle close and plain around her, reading, to any watch-post eye, as travel-worn finery rather than the veil it actually was. Spectacle would have meant nothing to soldiers this far past caring what impressed them, and she had learned long ago not to waste a performance on an audience that wouldn't pay for it.

A handful of them tracked her across the yard anyway — none had seen an unfamiliar face arrive by the front gate in longer than any of them would readily admit. One old soldier by the gatehouse still saluted the empty flagpole at what she guessed was the hour Aamon's musters had once begun, a habit long since worn smooth of any meaning beyond the doing of it. None challenged her. None, she noted, seemed to know whose authority might require them to.

She found Ronove in the training yard, exactly where the noise had promised he'd be — four arms and both maces moving through a drill against three soldiers at once, each of them already bleeding, none of them dead, which she judged, watching him work, to be entirely deliberate on his part rather than any restraint the fight itself demanded. He finished the drill with a final, almost affectionate blow that dropped the last soldier flat, and turned to find her watching with the open, unbothered curiosity of a large animal noticing a new smell.

"You're not one of mine," he said. Not a challenge. An observation, delivered the way another man might note the weather.

"No," she agreed. "I'm considering becoming your someone, if you'll have me. Word reached me that this whole watch has been drawing rations under a dead man's name for rather longer than a dead man should be allowed to command anything."

Ronove laughed, a short, genuine sound that startled a bird off the nearest wall. "Aamon's been dead since before half these soldiers were born. Nobody told us to stop, so we didn't. Kept fighting whatever came close enough to fight." He hefted one mace, resettled it against his shoulder with the easy familiarity of a man handling a limb rather than a weapon. "You want to be the one who tells us different, you'll want to be worth listening to. I don't follow titles. I follow whoever points me at something worth breaking."

"Then I won't waste either of our time pretending I have anything more persuasive to offer you than that." She let the plain shape of the trade sit in the open, the same way she'd have offered it to anyone whose loyalty had never run on flattery. "There's a war being fought considerably closer to the actual fighting than this post has seen in years. I can point you at it. In return, I'd like to be the one you check with first, when there's nobody left standing worth breaking."

He weighed the offer the same blunt way he'd have weighed a meal rather than an oath, no more sentiment in one than the other, and she watched the calculation resolve into something closer to relief than loyalty — a soldier glad, after years of nothing worth the name of a fight, to be handed a direction again. "Point me at something worth the trip," he said, "and I'll worry about who I'm checking with after." He turned back toward the yard without waiting for anything more from her, the matter already settled as far as he was concerned. It was, she thought, watching him go, the easiest agreement she had struck in longer than she cared to count — and she found she trusted it exactly as far as the next fight he was offered, no further, no less.

Behind him, the yard's other soldiers had gone back to their own business without a second look, and she understood, watching them, precisely how long it had been since anyone here had been given an order worth passing along to the rest. A legion could go quiet for years and never once break formation, she thought. It simply stopped expecting anyone to call it back into one.

---

Dantalion found her before she found him, which told her something useful about him before he'd said a single word.

He stood in the doorway of what had once been an officer's quarters, *Mnemosyn* held loose in one hand the way another man might hold a book he'd already read too many times to need the cover anymore. "You spoke to Ronove for the better part of an hour," he said, "and told him almost nothing true. I watched the shape of the conversation from the wall. He didn't notice. I did."

"I told him nothing false, either." She met his stare without offering him the comfort of a flinch. "There's a considerable distance between the two, and I've built a long career inside exactly that distance."

"So I gathered." He stepped into the room's dim light fully, and she took the measure of him the way she took the measure of anyone worth the patience — not the sphere itself, though it caught what little light the room offered and held it a moment too long to be entirely natural, but the stillness around him, a man who had spent years with nothing new to think about and had, in the absence of anything better, apparently perfected the art of watching. "I could simply take what you actually want from me. It would save us both considerable time, and I'd enjoy it rather more than this conversation."

"You could." She let the silence after that sit exactly as long as it needed to. "And you'd learn what I want, which is very little compared to what I could actually give you if you asked instead of took. Stolen things spoil, Captain. I imagine you know that better than most — a memory ripped loose keeps only the shape of what it was, not the weight. I don't deal in shapes. I deal in weight."

Something in his stillness shifted, fractionally, the first real crack she'd seen in it. "Go on."

"I've spent longer than this war has lasted collecting what people actually feel in the moment before they hand me the version they'd rather I believed instead — court lords caught between ambition and terror, angels bargaining with grief they haven't admitted to themselves yet, mortals remembering things no one else alive still does. Whole, unspoiled, exactly as it happened, and freely surrendered rather than torn out — I imagine that difference reads clearly enough even through Mnemosyn's own touch. I don't imagine this watch post has offered you anything worth the name of a real memory in longer than either of us would guess." She watched him weigh that the way she imagined he weighed everything — not for whether it flattered him, but for whether it was true. "I'm not offering you loyalty as a virtue, Captain. I'm offering you the first genuinely interesting thing to happen to you in years, in exchange for exactly the kind of attention you're already inclined to give anything worth watching closely."

He studied her a long moment, *Mnemosyn* turning once in his grip, catching the light again. "You understand I'll be watching you as closely as I watch anyone I've agreed to serve. Possibly closer. You're the first person to arrive at this post in years who requires understanding rather than simple obedience."

"I'd be disappointed in you if you did anything less." She let herself smile, and meant enough of it to make the rest land clean. "I've never once asked anyone to trust me, Captain. Only to find me useful enough that trust stops being the relevant question."

"An answer I find considerably more useful than I expected to." He inclined his head, the closest thing to a bow she suspected he had left in him after this many years of no one worth bowing to. "Very well. Point the legion where you like. I'll be watching to see whether the weight you promised is real."

---

She left the Cinder Watch before dusk, the requisition already amended in her own hand and countersigned with a captain's authority technically still valid, since no order had ever formally stripped either Ronove or Dantalion of the rank Aamon had given them — only the throne's own neglect, which was, she reflected, not a thing any court could later call theft without first admitting how long it had simply forgotten to look. Gusion would find the loophole elegant. She found it merely honest, in the narrow way her own honesty ever ran: she had claimed nothing that had not already been sitting unclaimed, waiting for whoever finally thought to walk out and collect it.

Dantalion's parting look stayed with her longer than she'd expected it to — not suspicion exactly, she judged on the long walk back toward the crossing that would carry her home, but the attention of a man who intended to go on weighing her, indefinitely, against whatever weight she next brought him. She had built her whole long career on being believed rather than understood, and she found, walking away from the one captain in her growing collection who had declined outright to make that distinction for her, that she did not yet know which of the two he'd eventually decide he'd rather have from her.

She already knew, walking, which memory she'd bring him first, and which she'd hold back a season longer than she'd promised him she would — a small, deliberate shortfall, the kind he might not catch for months, if he caught it at all. It would cost her something the day he did. She found the thought troubled her considerably less than perhaps it should have. Two captains, a legion nobody else had thought worth the paperwork to claim, and an afternoon's honest work — she counted it, walking on, a fair trade for whatever the weight of Dantalion's attention eventually asked of her in return.
