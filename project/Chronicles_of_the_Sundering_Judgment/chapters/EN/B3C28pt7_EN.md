# B3C28.7 – Two Ways to Ruin a Man

Days after Aamon's orphaned legion had settled into her ledgers, the report reached Naamah the way most of her useful information did — secondhand, unsigned, filtered through an agent who had learned long ago that she paid better for accuracy than for flattery. Verrow's Hold, a garrison town three provinces clear of the Valley Inverted's edge, had spent the season quietly outperforming every levy quota Heaven's recruiters had set for it, and the reason, once she'd had it run down properly, turned out to be almost embarrassingly simple: one garrison commander, a woman named Thessaly Vane, who had somehow convinced an entire farming town that sending its sons and daughters to the front was not a sacrifice but a privilege. She had apparently done it the plain way — walking her own recruits to the front personally rather than sending them off from a podium, a gesture cheap enough to cost her nothing and rare enough, in a war this long, to be worth more to a farming town than any speech. Morale, Naamah had learned across a very long career, was rarely an accident. Someone always built it, the same way someone always eventually had to be the one who tore it down.

She turned the report over once, weighing it against everything else currently drawing on her attention, and set it aside with the small satisfaction of a woman who had just found exactly the right problem for two people she'd been meaning to put to better use. Phenex had spent the better part of a season petitioning her for something worthy of his name, in the aggrieved tone of an artist convinced his talents were being wasted on commissions beneath him. Leraje had asked for nothing at all, which Naamah had long ago learned to read, from her, as its own kind of request — a captain who preferred to be handed real work rather than be caught wanting it aloud. A single town, ruined two different ways in careful sequence, would satisfy them both without either having to admit the other's method had anything to teach them.

---

Phenex arrived first, as he generally did, sweeping into her hall in the manner she'd come to recognize as a man arriving at an entrance he'd rehearsed. Degeneris rode across his shoulder the way another man might carry a favorite hound, and he paused just inside the doorway to take in the room's current shape — a long gallery, tonight, high-windowed and pale-floored, because she judged him exactly the sort of guest who'd have taken a plain chamber as an insult. "You've dressed the room for me," he said, delighted. "I'm touched, Duchess. Genuinely."

"I've dressed the room for whoever's useful to me at the moment I need them," Naamah said, "which tonight happens to be you." She let Neriah hold the gallery's shape a moment longer, watching him settle into it the way a cat settled into a patch of remembered sun. "There's a town called Verrow's Hold I'd like you to ruin. Beautifully, if you can manage it. Quietly would be a novelty, but I won't insist."

"Beautifully is the only register I have." He turned a slow circle, already, she judged, composing something in his head that had nothing to do with the actual town yet. "Tell me what's holding it together. I do my best work on something that's still standing when I arrive."

Leraje entered without any entrance at all, which was, Naamah had come to understand, itself a kind of statement — a shape simply present in the doorway that had not been there a moment before, Mors slung loose across one shoulder the way a soldier who trusted her aim never bothered checking a weapon twice. She hadn't dressed for the gallery, or for anything else Naamah might have chosen to build around her; the room's borrowed light slid off her the way it slid off stone, unclaimed and entirely beside the point. "You're going to tell him it's a garrison town," she said, before Naamah had said anything of the sort, "and he's going to want a month and an audience. I can have it quiet and finished before he's chosen a color."

"You say that like efficiency is the whole of the art," Phenex said, not turning to look at her, which Naamah judged was its own small insult, calibrated precisely. "Efficiency is what you settle for when you've never once managed to make anyone remember your work after you've left the room."

"I don't need them to remember it. I need them dead, and the ones who aren't dead too frightened to ask why." Leraje's voice carried the flat, unbothered precision of a woman reciting a fact rather than defending a position. "You want a legacy, Phenex. I want a result. One of those actually wins wars."

---

"The town is holding together," Naamah said, before the two of them could turn a professional disagreement into an evening's entertainment at her expense, "because of one woman. A garrison commander, Thessaly Vane, who has apparently convinced an entire province that this war is worth their children. Take her away cleanly enough, and the morale she built goes with her — grief doesn't recruit the way conviction does." She let the fact settle, watching each of them receive it through the particular lens of their own appetite. "How you take her away, and what you do with what's left afterward, is where I'm prepared to let you argue."

Phenex's whole posture sharpened, and Naamah watched the delight of a moment ago narrow into something closer to real interest. "A beloved commander, cut down in a way the whole province will still be telling their grandchildren about. That's not ruin, Duchess. That's a monument. I'd want the town to watch — not the killing itself, something after it, something that turns her memory into the very doubt that unmakes them. Faith curdling in front of an audience is worth considerably more to me than faith simply ended in the dark."

"And considerably more likely to leave a trail some angelic captain follows straight back to a name he can report," Leraje said. "I can put one of Mors's arrows through her at range, from ground no one will think to search. The toxin works slowly enough that the wound itself closes clean before anyone thinks to look for it, and what's left behind reads as her own body finally failing her — not a killing at all. No monument. No audience. Just a commander gone, and a town that spends the next month arguing about whether it was ever really her doing all along, or just a run of luck that happened to end." She glanced at Naamah for the first time since arriving. "Spectacle draws attention, Duchess. I'd have thought that mattered rather more to you than it apparently does to him."

It mattered, Naamah thought, considerably more than either of them currently understood — the Valley Inverted arrangement was still too new, too easily bruised by anything that looked like Hell overreaching where it had promised, for the moment, to hold its reach in check. A dead commander three provinces clear of that line was safe. A spectacle loud enough to draw a celestial investigator wandering in the wrong direction was not.

"Why this town, though," Leraje asked, before Naamah had decided how much of that she intended to say aloud. "There are garrisons closer. Softer ones, if morale's really the target." She wasn't challenging the assignment, Naamah judged, watching her — only cataloguing it, the way she cataloged everything before she trusted it.

"Because Verrow's Hold is exactly far enough from anywhere I currently need Hell's hand to look steady," Naamah said, offering as much of the true answer as cost her nothing to give. "A closer target buys the same morale collapse and a considerably worse set of questions about who authorized it, and from where. I'd rather you both did excellent work somewhere no one's currently watching closely enough to misread it." She let that settle, watching Leraje weigh it and, apparently, find it sufficient. "You're welcome to keep asking me why. I simply won't always have finished deciding how much of the answer is yours to hear."

Leraje considered this, and let it go with a plain, unbothered shrug — the shrug, Naamah judged, of someone who'd gotten more of an answer than she'd expected and had no further use for the rest. "Fair enough," she said. "You're the one who pays for the arrows."

---

"Leraje removes her," Naamah said, "cleanly, exactly as she's described — no trail, no spectacle, a death the town can spend its grief arguing about instead of avenging. That happens first, quietly, and it happens alone." She watched Leraje's mouth curve, the small, satisfied confirmation of a professional told her method was the one being trusted. "Once the commander who was holding that province together is gone and the grief has had a season to curdle on its own, the town is yours, Phenex. Not the killing — the after. Whatever the doubt leaves standing, once faith has already started doing your work for you before you've lifted a brush."

Phenex weighed this a moment, head tilted, the look of a man deciding whether he'd just been insulted or complimented and finding he couldn't yet tell the difference. "You're giving me the ruins instead of the building."

"I'm giving you a canvas already primed," Naamah said. "You've told me yourself, more than once, that you do your best work on something that's still standing when you arrive. A town half-broken by grief it doesn't understand the source of is still standing, Captain. It's simply already listening for whoever tells it what its own doubt means." She let the offer sit exactly as certain as it needed to sound. "I'd have thought you'd prefer an audience that's already half-persuaded before you've said a word."

Something shifted behind his theatrical composure, Naamah judged — real calculation replacing performance for the length of a breath. "That's not efficiency," he said slowly, to Leraje rather than to Naamah, "that's dramaturgy. I take back what I said about audiences."

"I didn't take back anything," Leraje said. "I just get to go first."

They left within the hour, Leraje already gone before Phenex had finished a second circuit of the gallery admiring its light, and Naamah let the room settle back into whatever shape it preferred when no one was left to want anything from it. She had told them both the truth, in the same narrow, exact way she told anyone anything — a commander's morale, a province's grief, a town's doubt, all of it real, none of it a lie either of them could later hold against her. What she hadn't told them, because neither had thought to ask the question that would have required it, was that Verrow's Hold itself mattered to her considerably less than the fact that two captains with appetites this incompatible had just agreed, in her hall, to work in sequence rather than in competition, because she had asked them to and given each of them a reason that cost the other nothing to grant.

---

Leraje's word reached her eleven days later, unadorned as everything she sent ever was — a single line, delivered by the same quiet channel as the report that had started this: *Done. Clean. No one's asking yet.* Naamah read it twice anyway, out of habit rather than doubt, and found herself, for a moment, almost sorry the woman took so little evident pleasure in work done this well. Thessaly Vane's death would already be settling into whatever shape a grieving province gave it — an illness, most likely, or the plain cruelty of a war that took even the ones who seemed too vital to lose. Either story would serve. Neither would point anywhere near a bow no one had seen.

She sent word back to Phenex within the hour: *The canvas is primed. Go make it art.*

---

That was worth more to her, in the long accounting she kept of what actually built power in a court like Lucifer's, than any single garrison commander's death. Word would travel — it always did, eventually, through exactly the channels she had spent a long career cultivating for precisely this purpose — that a Duchess who answered to no throne but her own had set two of Hell's most temperamental captains working in harmony on a single afternoon's asking, without needing to raise her voice or invoke a rank Leviathan had granted her only on paper. Balam would hear of it. Foras, in his own scattered corner of Belial's crumbling court, might hear of it too, eventually, filtered through whatever version reached him last. She found she did not mind which version that turned out to be, so long as every version agreed on the one fact she actually needed remembered: that when Naamah asked for something, competent people found a way to give it to her without being told twice.

It would cost Verrow's Hold everything it currently understood itself to be, in the end, and she found the thought troubled her exactly as little as it ever did. She had built nothing, in her whole long career, on pretending otherwise to herself in the privacy of an empty room — not that night, watching two captains leave her hall each believing he or she had been given the better half of the work, and not now, eleven days further into the cost neither of them had thought to ask what the other was paying.
