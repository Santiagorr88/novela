# B3C06 – The Storm of Sorrows

"You can't just walk to this one," Ereloth said, standing at the edge of a cliff overlooking a stretch of ordinary grey ocean, the kind of coastline Mikel might have driven past a hundred times without a second thought, gulls circling somewhere below them over water that gave no hint of what it actually led to. "Turein doesn't hold still long enough for a map to find it twice. You have to be let in."

"Let in by what?"

"By the place itself, mostly. It listens for the right rhythm before it opens." Ereloth crouched, picked up a smooth stone, and began humming something low and unfinished, a melody that kept almost resolving and then didn't, over and over, each near-completion sliding away into a different near-completion instead. "I know the pattern. Most people don't have the patience for it. You, on the other hand, have something better than patience." He nodded toward Mikel's chest, though Mikel had no weapon there to point at, nothing at all beyond an ordinary shirt and the ordinary body underneath it. "You've got her asking to come home. That helps more than you'd think." He glanced sideways at Mikel, something briefly serious flickering behind his usual amusement. "It won't get you all the way there on its own, mind. You'll still have to do some of the actual work yourself. But it opens the door that would otherwise stay shut no matter how well you hum."

He kept humming. Mikel closed his eyes without being told to, and felt something in his own chest answer the unfinished melody, a low answering hum he hadn't known he was capable of producing, an interior note he had never once consciously reached for in nine years of ordinary living. It came easily anyway, as though some part of him had been holding the shape of it ready for a long time, waiting only to be asked. He kept his eyes closed a moment longer than he needed to, uncertain whether opening them too soon might interrupt whatever was happening, and when he finally opened them again, the grey ocean had become a different grey ocean entirely, the light overhead gone strange and slanted in a way no honest sun produced.

---

They had arrived, apparently, though nothing about the transition had felt like arriving — no flash, no falling sensation, simply one shoreline replaced by another between one blink and the next. Mikel stood on a shore of black volcanic sand, and above the water, filling the whole visible sky, a storm turned with the slow, deliberate patience of something that had been turning for longer than the concept of patience usually applied to. It had been turning, he understood without needing to be told, since the day Michael fell — every age of that long absence spent building itself larger, fed by nothing but its own solitary waiting. It was not a storm shaped like weather. It was shaped like grief given enough time and enough isolation to build itself an entire climate, a hurricane the size of the island itself, howling with a sound that carried no wind in it at all, only sorrow, pure and enormous and entirely without a target.

Mikel felt it settle over him before he'd taken a second step onto the sand, not physical weight exactly, though it pressed on him the way physical weight might have. It did not confine itself to his own life. He felt losses that belonged to entire centuries he'd never lived through pressing against him alongside his own — soldiers he'd never met, wars he had no memory of standing in, grief old enough that whoever had first felt it had died long before he was ever born into either of his two lives. His mother's funeral surfaced somewhere inside that larger flood, a service he'd sat through dry-eyed at the time and had never once forgiven himself for since. Every student who'd stopped answering his emails after graduation surfaced too, small severances he'd told himself didn't matter and had apparently been wrong about. He staggered, one hand finding a jagged rock for balance, and understood, distantly, that this was only the storm's outer edge.

Ereloth walked beside him apparently unbothered, hands in his pockets, his bare feet leaving no visible print in the black sand at all, his usual unhurried gait entirely unaffected by whatever weight was currently trying to drive Mikel into the ground. "She doesn't do this on purpose," he said, raising his voice slightly over the wind, though his tone stayed conversational, almost gentle. "She's not trying to hurt you. She's just been sad for so long that sadness is all the air here actually has left in it."

---

His first instinct, old and reflexive, was to reach for the part of himself that had once commanded armies, some buried authority he could throw against the wind like a wall. He felt it rise in him, a cold and certain strength gathering behind his ribs, and the moment he let it surface, the storm answered with a fresh and violent surge, the howling climbing an octave, the pressure against his chest doubling in an instant.

"Wrong tool, Miki!" Ereloth's voice reached him from somewhere close by, though the wind made it hard to place exactly where. "You can't fight sadness with strength! You'll just give it more to weep about!" A pause, filled with wind. "You have to give it a reason to stop crying. Find a light inside you that has nothing to do with power. That's the only door this storm actually opens for!"

Mikel let the gathered strength go, forcing his hands to unclench at his sides, and the moment he did, the pressure against his chest returned to its previous, merely-unbearable level, still crushing, still enormous, but no longer actively escalating in response to him.

---

He searched himself for something else to offer it, and found, at first, only more of the same. He reached for the memory of a battle won, some flicker of Michael's old certainty, and the wind screamed louder for the offering, the storm recognizing pride in whatever shape it arrived wearing. He reached for a lecture that had gone particularly well, some flicker of Mikel Ardon's professional pride, and found that pride, too, feeding the storm rather than answering it, some hidden edge of superiority buried even in that small, ordinary triumph.

He let both go, and stood in the howling dark with nothing left to offer, no old certainty and no professional pride, just a man on a beach with a grief too large to argue with pressing down on him from every direction at once. His knees had begun to buckle under it, the pressure finding new ground now that he'd stopped resisting it with anything at all. He thought, in the middle of that emptiness, of Michael. He let that name go too, deliberately, feeling it slip out of his hands the way a rope slips when you finally stop pulling against something heavier than you are.

For a moment there was only the wind, and the black sand under his knees, and the specific terror of having run out of things to try.

And then, without reaching for it at all, a different memory simply arrived.

---

A Tuesday afternoon, three years into teaching, a student named Priya sitting across from him during office hours, staring at a primary source translation she'd been stuck on for two weeks. He'd explained the grammatical construction four different ways already, each one landing somewhere just short of understanding. He remembered trying a fifth way, almost out of habit rather than hope, walking her through the sentence one clause at a time. He remembered the exact moment it landed — her eyes widening, not dramatically, just a small, real shift, and the words coming out of her fast and pleased: *oh, oh, I see it now.*

He had felt, in that unremarkable office on an unremarkable Tuesday, nothing that resembled command or judgment or victory. He had felt only the plain, warm satisfaction of watching someone else's confusion resolve into their own understanding, a gift he'd had no stake in beyond wanting to give it. Nothing to prove. Nothing to win. Just a student, finally seeing what she'd been reaching for, and a professor glad, simply and completely, to have been there when it happened.

He remembered, too, the small, ordinary details that had surrounded that moment without belonging to it directly — the smell of the coffee going cold on the corner of his desk, the muffled sound of a class changing over in the hallway outside, Priya's pen tapping an absent rhythm against her notebook the whole time she worked through the sentence. None of it had mattered at the time. He found, holding the memory now, that none of it had actually been forgettable either. It had simply been waiting, filed away somewhere he hadn't thought to look until a storm made of every unresolved grief he owned forced him to go looking properly.

He held that memory now, on a black beach at the edge of a grieving storm, and felt something small and gold begin to glow behind his sternum, faint at first, then steadier, spreading slowly through his chest the way warmth spreads through cold hands held close to a fire, entirely unlike the cold, gathered strength he'd tried offering before.

---

The light spread outward from his chest, warm rather than blinding, and the wind immediately around him began to still, the howling nearby dropping to something closer to an exhausted, ragged breath. He got to his feet slowly, testing the ground beneath him, half expecting the calm to vanish the instant he moved. It didn't. He walked forward into that small, expanding stillness, one careful step at a time, watching the raging wall of grief part just enough, just ahead of him, to let him keep walking without being torn apart by it. He did not try to make the calm larger than it needed to be. He simply walked inside it, the way you walk inside any small, hard-won shelter, grateful for exactly as much as it actually gave you.

Somewhere behind him, faint over the receding wind, he heard Ereloth's voice, pitched low with something that might have been pride. He didn't turn to check. He kept his eyes on the narrow path ahead, unwilling to risk losing whatever thin margin of calm was currently keeping him upright and moving.

The path led him, eventually, to the storm's dead center — a small clearing of unnatural stillness at the heart of all that raging sorrow, the eye around which the entire grief-shaped hurricane turned. And there, waiting, exactly as Ereloth had promised, stood a sword.

---

It rose from a crystal formed, Mikel understood without being told, from the storm's own substance — countless frozen tears, layered and compressed across ages of solitary waiting, until they'd hardened into something that held its shape the way stone holds its shape. The crystal itself stood taller than he did, faintly translucent, catching what little light reached this deep into the storm and scattering it into a hundred small, cold points across its surface. Solmire's blade, visible at its center, caught that same scattered light and returned it doubled, a patient shine that seemed less like reflection and more like the blade remembering, on its own, what light was actually supposed to look like.

Mikel stood before it a long moment, saying nothing, the storm's outer howl reduced now to a faint sound at his back. He thought of every age it had spent alone here, waiting with nothing but its own grief for company, and of the seven marks on a map he'd made weeks ago, the patient, ordinary research that had somehow, against every reasonable expectation, actually led him here. He thought of Ereloth's insistence on stones and rooftops and a ruined tray of canapés, lessons that had felt, at the time, like avoidance dressed up as teaching, and understood now, standing here, that every one of them had been necessary preparation for exactly this moment.

He understood, looking at the crystal, that arriving here hadn't been the end of anything. It had only been the door. Whatever came next — whatever it would actually cost him to reach out and claim what was waiting inside that ice — was still ahead of him, close enough now to touch, and entirely unclaimed.
