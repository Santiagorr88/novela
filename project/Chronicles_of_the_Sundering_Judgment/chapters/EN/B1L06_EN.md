# B1L06 — What Winning Costs

*(Lament Arc, Chapter 6 of 6 — POV Belial, with cuts to a mortal witness and the angelic scout's report)*

The plain released him with none of the ceremony it had used to pull him in. One step he was standing at the tomb's threshold, Lament heavy and aware against his palm; the next the crystal horizon simply ended, the grief-light gave way to the ordinary dark of the portal, and he was through, back in the world he had left three days and a lifetime ago, alone with a weapon that had not stopped quietly, patiently disagreeing with him since the moment he'd closed his hand around it.

It did not fight him. That would have been simpler. It simply persisted — a low, constant awareness at the edge of every thought, the sensation of being watched by something that had decided, with infinite and infuriating patience, that watching was punishment enough. He had wanted a weapon. He had acquired, instead, a permanent witness to exactly how much he had refused to feel, and it did not seem inclined to ever stop bearing that witness quietly, in the corner of every room he would ever again stand in.

He told himself this was victory. He told himself that with the particular force he reserved for things he needed to be true rather than things that were, and did not examine too closely why the telling had, for the first time in his existence, required more than one attempt.

---

The resonance reached the mortal world within the hour, and unlike the low tremor his resistance had leaked days earlier, this one did not arrive as a question with no answer. It arrived as a second event, unmistakably paired with the first — the same stretch of coastline that had weathered a hundred ordinary squalls woke that night to a storm that had not existed six hours before it broke, cloudless sky curdling into black in the space of a single held breath, wind clawing in off a sea that had gone the wrong color of grey.

Tomas Herrera had fished that coast for thirty-one years, long enough to read weather the way other people read faces, and this one told him nothing he recognized. He banged on his neighbor's door before he'd finished pulling on his coat.

"Get the boats up. Now. All of them."

His neighbor, half-asleep, squinted past him at a sky that had been clear an hour ago. "There's no warning out. Nothing on the radio."

"There will be," Tomas said, already moving toward the dock. "I don't need the radio to tell me what I can see with my own eyes."

It raged for eleven minutes with a fury that stripped roofs and toppled trees planted before anyone's grandparents were born, and stopped as suddenly as it had started, leaving behind a silence that felt, to everyone who lived through it, less like the aftermath of weather and more like the aftermath of grief finally exhaling. Tomas stood on the dock afterward, coat still only half on, and said nothing else that night. There was nothing in his vocabulary for what he'd just watched arrive out of nowhere and leave the same way.

No one connected the storm to the earthquake three hundred miles inland. No one had the information to. Only two beings anywhere in creation understood that the world had just flinched twice in the same week for the same reason, and neither of them was in any position, yet, to explain it to the other.

Iofiel's scout reached the coordinate four days after Belial had already left it behind him.

He had traveled carefully, exactly as instructed — low profile, no escort, nothing that might draw attention to a mission the Council had, in the end, been unwilling to properly fund. He found the plain of weeping crystal precisely where the fragment had promised it would be, walked its indifferent expanse with the particular dread of a soldier who suspects he is already too late and has no way yet to prove it, and found, at the heart of it, a small stone chamber with nothing in it but the lingering, unmistakable shape of an absence.

He did not need Iofiel's scholarship to read what the room was telling him. He made the return journey already composing the words he dreaded saying, and when he finally stood before the Council, he said them plainly, because there was no gentler order to put them in.

"The tomb is empty. Someone reached it before us."

"Before us by how long?" Gabriel asked.

"Days, my lord. Not hours." The scout hesitated. "Whatever was kept there — it's already loose in the world."

Gabriel said nothing for a long moment. Iofiel, watching him, understood that his silence was not shock. It was the specific, private arithmetic of a commander recalculating every decision that had led to a scout arriving four days too late instead of an army arriving four days too early.

"We debated funding a full expedition," she said quietly, not as an accusation — there was no one left to accuse but themselves. "We chose caution instead."

"We chose caution," Gabriel agreed, "and caution chose to cost us exactly this much." He did not raise his voice. He did not need to; the weight of the sentence did that on its own. "Find out what it was. I want a name before I want an apology."

Belial returned to his court expecting triumph and found, instead, a room that had already rehearsed how to receive him.

Foras was there. Of course he was there — Belial understood, walking in with Lament's quiet, patient weight against his palm, that Foras had likely known the moment of his return before Belial himself had crossed the final threshold. Foras did not look surprised. He did not look, Belial noted with the particular fury of a man denied even the satisfaction of an enemy's shock, especially impressed either.

"My lord returns," Foras said, and bowed with exactly the correct depth, no more, no less. His many golden eyes took in the spear without hurry. "Triumphant, I see. The court will be relieved to learn your absence was productive rather than — " the pause was surgical " — merely prolonged."

The captains behind him said nothing, which was its own kind of report. Belial read the room the way he had once read battlefields, cataloguing which faces met his eyes without flinching and which found something urgent to study on the floor.

"While you were chasing ghosts," Foras said, serene as ever, "someone had to hold this together."

"And you held it." Belial let the words land flat, no gratitude dressed up as a compliment. "Now let go of it, Foras, before we mistake gratitude for permission."

"I only ever act in your interest, my lord."

"You've been busy in my absence." Belial said it the way he had once said very little else that mattered — not as a threat, not yet, but as a marker, a line drawn precisely where he intended, eventually, to collect.

Foras's smile did not reach any of his eyes. "Someone had to."

Belial did not answer that. Lament's weight shifted against his palm, patient and aware, and he understood — with the same unwelcome clarity the Sepulcher had spent three days forcing into him — that he had won a weapon capable of matching Heaven's champion, and had returned to find that the war closest to his own throne was one Lament would be no use to him in at all.

He had crossed the Sepulcher and come out the other side with the truth still in his hand.

He was beginning to understand that the truth, here, would cost him just as much as the grief had.
