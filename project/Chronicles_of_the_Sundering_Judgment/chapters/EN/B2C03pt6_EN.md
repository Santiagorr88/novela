# B2C03.6 – What the Hands Are For

Laila had learned, long before this war gave her a title worth carrying, that a glove taught its wearer nothing on the first day it was worn, or the tenth, or the hundredth. Only on the day the wearer finally stopped thinking about the glove at all, and started thinking instead about whatever it was they were reaching for. She watched for that day the way other captains watched for a breach. This morning it hadn't come yet, not for the soldier standing across from her in the training hall's cool, even light, and she let her patience show nothing of the count she kept privately of how many mornings this made.

"Again," she said, and lifted her own hands, Tenura's pale glow gathering soft along her fingers — not a weapon readying, never that, only a promise stretching thin enough to reach across the space between two people. "You are not fighting the relic, Renn. You have never once had to fight it. You are only listening for the moment it stops being a stranger to your hand."

Renn exhaled, squared his stance, and reached again for the training vessel resting on its stand — an old, decommissioned shard of something that had once mattered and now existed only to be reached for, over and over, by soldiers learning what their hands were actually for. His fingers closed around it. Nothing answered him. His shoulders dropped, and Laila read in the slump the small, specific weight of a young recruit who had let himself hope, this time, for a different result.

"You tightened your grip the instant before contact," she said, gentle in the way she was gentle with all of them, which was not softness so much as a refusal to let disappointment calcify into shame before it had to. "The relic feels that. It reads a closed hand as a demand, and demands are the one thing nothing sacred has ever answered honestly." She crossed the space between them and adjusted his wrist herself, two fingers, no more force than settling a child's collar. "Open. Let it decide whether you're worth answering before you decide you already know."

He tried once more, and this time something in the shard's old, spent light stirred, faint and brief, gone again before either of them could name it fully. It was enough. Laila watched the surprise cross his face and felt, in the small private place she kept for exactly this, the warmth of watching someone become slightly more than they had been an hour earlier. She had built her whole long service on collecting mornings like this one, hundreds of them, thousands, and told herself, most days, that they added up to something worth the collecting even when no council would ever ask to see the count.

---

The runner found her before the session's proper end, breathless in a way that meant the order behind him outranked whatever courtesy might have had him wait. "Captain. Commander Orifiel requests you at the chart-room. Today, not tomorrow."

She dismissed Renn with a nod that promised the lesson would continue and followed the runner through corridors she knew well enough to walk without watching her own feet, already turning over what kind of request pulled Orifiel away from his wall on a day like this one. She found him exactly where she expected to, the Amaranth ledger still open on the table though its ink had long since dried, his shield banked low along his fused arm in the way Laila had learned to read as a man who had already decided something and simply hadn't finished saying it aloud.

"I released half the Amaranth garrison this morning," he said, without preamble, because preamble had never been his habit with her. "To the search. Ezequiel is already carrying that order." He studied her a moment, and something in his stone-set face softened by the small degree she had only ever seen it soften around her. "I need something similar from you, Laila. I like asking it of you less than I liked asking the wall to thin itself."

"My people aren't a wall, Orifiel." She said it without heat, only fact, the way she corrected him perhaps once a decade and no more, because he had earned, across their shared centuries, the right to be corrected gently rather than often. "You can measure how much a wall can spare. You cannot measure how much synergy training a bearer's hands can spare. The answer changes with every hand."

"I know it." He closed the ledger, the small sound of it final in the quiet room. "Raphael needs trackers who can feel the sword's resonance if the field search stumbles across so much as an echo of it. No one else in this Bastion has hands trained to feel that particular silence." He paused, and Laila felt, for a moment, whatever paternal weight he carried for every captain under his command settle more heavily on her specifically than she had felt it settle in years. "Half your active roster. I would not ask if I thought there were slack anywhere else to find first."

She held the number a moment before answering, turning it over fully, unhurried, the way she turned over every request that touched her people. "Half," she said finally, "and I choose which half."

"You always do."

"Because you already know I'll choose the ones closest to being ready, not the ones easiest to spare." She let that sit a moment. "Send mine into the field before their hands are steady, and you won't get discipline back from it. You'll get soldiers who reach for a relic that isn't theirs, and come away from the search having learned the wrong lesson about what their own hands are for." She had said some version of this to him before, decades apart, and he had never once argued the point, only listened the way he listened to almost no one else in this Bastion. "Give me until morning to choose properly. I won't waste the extra night."

"Take it." He did not soften the request further. They both understood by now that softening it would only insult the seriousness with which she was already carrying it.

---

She chose Karin to lead the field detail before she chose anyone else, because Karin's gift for reading the bond between wielder and relic ran deeper than any soldier's currently under her command, and because she trusted her to come back and say honestly when a lead had died rather than let hope outlive its own evidence out of kindness. Karin would draw the rest of her small team from among the synergy-trained soldiers still finishing their own apprenticeships — Yaron among them, young enough that this would be his first assignment carried further than the training hall. Laila found Karin in the armory's quiet end, braided light still catching in her hair from whatever glyph-work she'd been practicing, and gave her the assignment the same plain way Orifiel had given his to her.

Karin took it without flinching, though something crossed her face Laila recognized immediately, having worn it herself more times than she cared to count. "You're sending us to look for something the whole Bastion has been failing to find for years, Captain."

"I am."

"And if we find nothing. Which is what everyone before us has found."

Laila set down the gauntlet she'd been mending and gave the question the honesty it deserved rather than the reassurance it might have been easier to offer. "Then you will have spent weeks confirming an absence instead of a presence, and that will feel, most days, like the same thing as failure." She held Karin's gaze, wanting her to hear the rest without softening. "It isn't the same thing. A bearer who hasn't been found yet is not a bearer who doesn't exist. I have spent longer than either of us can easily count guarding people for relics they hadn't claimed yet, and I have never once regretted the years that came before the claiming, however many of them there were."

"That's not the same as an answer, Captain."

"No," Laila agreed. "It's the only honest one I have."

---

The lead came four days later, carried up through Karin's own chain of report by the soldier who'd first flagged it — Yaron, breathless with an eagerness Laila recognized immediately, having worn something close to it herself more times than she cared to count. A scout working the old eastern battlefields had dug a shard from a collapsed barrow that answered faintly, or seemed to, hand shaking slightly around the wrapped bundle as he swore to it, to the same resonance pattern Tenura's own field-tests had been calibrated against for years. Karin had held it herself first and found nothing conclusive either way, and had sent it up rather than bury a possibility she wasn't certain enough to close on her own authority. Laila understood, watching Yaron's face while she unwrapped the bundle, that he had let himself believe this was the one already, somewhere on the walk over, in whatever quiet way hope does its damage before the evidence arrives to correct it.

She held it herself before anyone else touched it, Tenura's glow reaching toward the wrapped shape with the same careful attention she gave every candidate, however unlikely, because she had promised herself early in this assignment that no lead would ever be dismissed before it had been properly held. The glow found nothing worth the name. Not silence exactly — an old blade always answered something, however faint, the ghost of whatever violence had once been done with it — but nothing that carried the unmistakable warmth she had spent centuries learning to recognize and had never once mistaken for anything else. She held it a full minute longer than the reading required, because Yaron was watching her hands the entire time as though her verdict might still change if she simply held it long enough.

"It's a soldier's blade," she said finally, gentle with the words in the specific way she reserved for verdicts nobody in the room wanted. "Old, and it died meaning something to whoever carried it. But it isn't hers, Yaron. It was never going to be hers."

---

Yaron didn't argue. That was, somehow, worse than if he had — she watched him absorb the verdict with a careful stillness, determined not to let his disappointment become anyone else's burden, and found that stillness harder to witness than any protest would have been. He'd flagged the barrow three weeks ago on his own time, cross-referencing old maps against Karin's resonance charts before he'd ever brought it to anyone with the rank to authorize a proper dig. He had wanted this one specifically, she understood, watching him now, not for the credit of finding it but for the plain relief of an ending.

She set the shard down and crossed to him rather than letting the distance between captain and soldier do what distance always did to grief this size. "You did the work well," she said. "The barrow was worth checking. The chart pattern was worth trusting. You did not fail today, Yaron. You simply finished confirming one more place she isn't, and that is not nothing, whatever it feels like standing this close to it." She rested one gloved hand briefly against his shoulder, Tenura's light banked so low it barely showed, offered as comfort rather than power, the one use of the relic no training manual had ever needed to teach her. "I have confirmed more absences than I can count across more centuries than I like admitting to. I am still standing here. So are you."

He nodded, not fully steadied, but steadied enough, and she let him have the rest of the walk back to the barracks in silence, guessing that he needed the quiet more than he needed any further words from her.

---

She returned to the training hall that evening rather than to her own quarters, because an empty barrow and a soldier's borrowed grief were not, in the end, reasons to let the actual work wait until morning. She lifted Tenura from its stand and settled the gloves back over her hands, warm and familiar after all these centuries of exactly this motion, and reached once more for the old training shard Renn had failed to wake that morning.

It answered her instantly, a soft and steady light that asked nothing and promised nothing, only confirmed, the way it had confirmed ten thousand times before, that some hands were made for holding what the finding hadn't yet produced. She did not know, standing alone in the emptying hall with the shard's light settling gold across her palms, whether tomorrow would bring Karin word of another lead, or another absence, or nothing at all. She only knew, closing her fingers gently around the light rather than gripping it, that she would still be here regardless, hands open, for however long the search asked it of her.
