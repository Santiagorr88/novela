# B1L05 — The True Tomb

*(Lament Arc, Chapter 5 of 6 — POV Belial)*

The door was not a door. He understood that the moment he crossed it — there was no frame, no threshold, only a place where the plain's grief thickened past bearing and then, all at once, resolved into a room.

It was small. That was the first insult, and it landed before any other feeling had the chance to arrive first. He had imagined, without ever admitting he'd imagined it, something to match the scale of what this place had cost him — a cathedral of sorrow, a throne of black glass, some architecture worthy of the price he'd already paid to reach it. Instead he found a chamber no larger than a modest hall, bare walls of the same weeping crystal, and at its center, resting on nothing, unadorned by pedestal or ward or a single ornamental flourish, a spear.

It did not shine. It did not hum with the low, proprietary pride he remembered from Solmire's report — the sword that had, by every account, announced itself the moment Miguel's fingers closed around it. Lament simply lay there, plain as a tool left out by a workman who trusted no one would think to steal it, and for one unguarded second Belial's first true reaction was contempt.

*This* was what had cost him his knees, his pride, a tremor felt three hundred miles inland by a fisherman who would spend the rest of his life not understanding what he'd sensed? A spear with no more presence than a length of iron in a smith's forgotten corner?

The contempt did not survive contact. He felt it the moment he let himself actually look — not at the spear's surface, but at what moved beneath it, the way a man senses weight under still water before he sees the thing that displaces it. It was not radiance. It was gravity. The room bent toward the spear the way grief bends a person's posture without their permission, and what Belial had mistaken for humility was, he understood with a hunger that arrived faster than his contempt had left, something closer to restraint. This weapon was not undressed. It had simply never needed to perform.

He took a step toward it, and the room answered the way the plain had answered his second step in the beginning — not with violence, but with a warning, delivered directly into whatever part of him still remembered how to be afraid.

*Solmire seduces,* something in the grief-thick air seemed to say, though there was no voice, only the certainty a room can carry when it has held one truth long enough to stop needing to speak it aloud. *I do not.*

He did not stop walking. He had never once, in the whole of his existence, let a warning decide anything for him, and he was not going to grant this room the singular honor of being the first.

---

The vision arrived the instant his shadow fell across the spear's length, and it did not announce itself as a vision at all — it felt, for one disorienting heartbeat, like memory, like something that had always belonged to him and had simply been waiting in a drawer he'd forgotten he owned.

He saw hands. Not his own — larger, paler, marked along the knuckles with the particular calluses of someone who worked with grief the way a smith worked with metal, patiently, over and over, refusing to let the material win. He saw a forge that was not a forge, no fire in it, no anvil ringing with triumph — only silence, and water, and a figure bent over the spear's half-finished length with the specific, exhausted devotion of someone doing a duty he had long since stopped believing he deserved to carry.

There was no laughter in this making. Belial understood, with a clarity that arrived whether he wanted it or not, that whoever had forged this weapon had done it as penance rather than joy — had shaped it not to prove anything to the universe, but because someone, somewhere, needed a truth heavy enough to be believed, and this maker had judged himself the only one left willing to carry the cost of forging it.

He recognized it. That was the part that frightened him, though he would never once use that word for it even alone in his own thoughts. He recognized the particular exhaustion of doing a terrible, necessary thing because no one else would, and hating yourself a little more with every stroke for being the kind of creature who could. It was not pity he felt, looking at those pale, careful hands. It was something closer to kinship, and kinship was so much worse than pity that for a moment he could not tell which one he was drowning in.

A voice reached him from somewhere inside the vision itself — not the maker's, not quite, but something the spear had kept of him, the way a room keeps the shape of a conversation long after the people who had it are gone.

*Why do you want to carry this?*

"Because no one else dares to," Belial answered, before he had decided to answer at all, and the honesty of it startled him more than the question had.

The voice did not correct him. It did not comfort him either. It only asked again, once, in exactly the same tone, as though the answer had not landed anywhere it could be used — and the silence that followed was, he understood slowly, the spear's actual reply.

He had spent centuries despising creatures who sacrificed anything for a cause larger than their own ambition. He had built an entire self out of the conviction that such sacrifice was, at bottom, a failure of nerve dressed up as virtue. And here, in a vision he had not asked for and could not refuse, he understood — for exactly as long as the vision lasted and not one second longer — precisely what it had cost the maker of this spear to keep making it anyway.

The vision released him. He staggered, though there was nothing physical to stagger from, and found himself on one knee before he had decided to kneel, his hand not yet touching the spear, close enough now that he could feel its gravity pulling at something in his chest that had no proper name.

---

"No," he said aloud, to the room, to the vision, to whatever remained of the maker's grief still soaked into the crystal beneath his palm. His voice did not shake. He made certain of that the way he made certain of everything — by force, applied precisely, at exactly the point where it was needed most.

He would not be forged by this the way its maker had been forged by his own devotion. He would not surrender the way Miguel, by every report reaching Hell, had surrendered to his sword — arriving hollow and letting a weapon fill the hollow with someone else's certainty. Belial had no hollow. He had spent an age making sure of that, and he was not going to let one small, humble, aching piece of iron convince him otherwise now, in the one room in all of creation built specifically to make convincing him feel like mercy.

"I am Belial." He said it the way he had said very little else in his existence — not as a boast, which was how it would have sounded to anyone listening, but as an anchor, a thing to hold onto while the room tried, patiently and without malice, to take something from him that he had no intention of giving. "My will is my own."

It was not victory. He understood that even as he said it, even as he closed his hand at last around Lament's shaft and felt the grief in it recognize him — not welcome him, *recognize* him, the way one old wound recognizes another — and did not let it change what he had come here already certain of. He was not making peace with the spear. He was refusing it a peace it clearly wanted to make with him, gripping it the way a man grips a blade by the edge rather than the hilt, because the edge, at least, was a pain he had chosen.

The spear did not resist his grip. It simply accepted it, the way the room had accepted his contempt, patient in a manner that unsettled him more than any struggle would have. He had expected a battle. He had been given, instead, something that felt uncomfortably like being humored.

He stood. Lament's weight settled against his palm, wrong in a way he could not yet name — not heavy, not light, but *aware*, in a fashion Ruach had never once managed to be in all their centuries together. The room's grief did not lift. It simply turned, all at once, toward acknowledging that it had a new custodian, whether or not that custodian had agreed to be changed by the custody.

He had come here for a weapon to match Solmire. He left holding something that had, in the space of a single vision, shown him exactly how much of himself he'd spent a lifetime refusing to look at directly — and he had won, if this was winning, by refusing to look at it even now, with the truth of it literally in his hand.

Belial turned from the tomb without looking back at it, Lament held before him like a verdict he had not yet decided how to read, and did not let himself wonder, not once, why victory had never before required this much of him to keep insisting that it was one.
