# B3C38 – The Unchained Soul

Margaret Hollis died the way she had lived, quietly, without fuss, somewhere in the small, unremarkable hours of the night, between one breath and the one that never came after it. She had fallen asleep in the same reading chair she'd fallen asleep in most nights of the last decade, a worn paperback open face-down across her chest, the lamp beside her still burning warm and yellow against the dark.

She felt herself come loose from her body with a gentleness that surprised her, a familiar lightness she recognized, distantly, from every dream she'd ever had of flying as a girl. She looked back once at the chair, at the small, still woman sitting in it with her reading glasses slipped down her nose, and found she felt no grief at the sight of her, only a quiet, matter-of-fact recognition, the way one might notice a coat finally left behind on a chair after a long, comfortable evening. The paperback had slipped a little further down her chest since she'd drifted off reading it, its cover catching the lamplight at an angle that made the title briefly unreadable. She found, studying that small, ordinary detail, that she remembered exactly which page she'd left off on, though she suspected the knowing of it no longer mattered quite the way it once had.

She had spent sixty-one years running the little branch library on Aldergate Street, had shelved more books than she could count and remembered the names of more patrons than she'd ever let on. She remembered, drifting there above her own quiet body, the exact weight of the front doors' brass handle, worn smooth by decades of hands passing through it before hers and after. She remembered the particular hush that settled over the reading room every afternoon around three, when the schoolchildren came pouring in and, within minutes, somehow always fell silent without her ever needing to ask them to.

She had lived a simple life, by most measures, unremarkable enough that she'd sometimes wondered, in her quieter moments, whether it had actually amounted to anything worth the living of it. No husband, no children of her own, nothing that most obituaries would have thought worth more than a passing line, a life spent quietly among due dates and hushed reminders rather than anything the rest of the world might have thought grander. She found, drifting free of her body now, that the question no longer weighed on her the way it once had.

---

She waited for the pull she'd always assumed would come, the tunnel of light every account she'd ever half-believed described so consistently, some vast and judging presence waiting to weigh whatever she'd made of her sixty-one years against whatever standard actually mattered. She had attended church dutifully for most of her life without ever quite settling her own private doubts about what actually waited on the other side of it, had privately suspected, more than once, that death would simply prove to be the answer to a test she'd spent her whole life studying incorrectly for, never quite certain which chapters actually mattered until it was too late to go back and check.

It didn't come. She simply floated, weightless and unhurried, in a stillness so calm it took her a long moment to recognize the absence of fear in herself as anything worth noticing at all.

No tunnel opened before her. No presence loomed, judging or otherwise. She found herself instead suspended in a quiet, silvered space that held no walls she could name, her thoughts clearer than they had felt in years, free of the small aches and forgetfulnesses that had settled into her these last few years of living. She could remember, with a sudden, startling clarity, the exact color of her mother's kitchen wallpaper from a childhood home she hadn't thought about in decades. Ahead of her, where she had expected a single path to finally reveal itself, she found instead a great many doors, each one standing slightly ajar, soft light spilling from behind each threshold in a color she had no name for.

She studied them a long moment, more curious than afraid, and found herself thinking, absurdly, of the library's own reading room on a quiet Sunday morning, a dozen different books left open on a dozen different tables, each one waiting for whichever reader happened to wander close enough to actually pick it up. She had always loved that particular quiet, the sense of a hundred different stories all patiently waiting their turn. She found she loved this considerably larger version of the same feeling just as much.

She counted the doors as best she could, though the counting itself seemed to shift the moment she attempted it, more doors revealing themselves the longer she looked, as though the space itself had no particular interest in being measured by anything so ordinary as a number. She gave up counting after a while and simply let herself take in the whole of it instead, the way she'd once learned to take in the whole of a crowded reading room without needing to catalogue every single face inside it.

---

A voice reached her then, soft and unhurried, carrying none of the weight she'd braced herself for, only a settled patience that reminded her, oddly, of the way certain regulars used to lower their voices automatically the moment they stepped through the library's front doors, out of some quiet respect for the hush she'd spent decades carefully tending.

*The scales have been broken,* the voice said, arriving less like sound and more like a thought that had simply always belonged to her, only now finally spoken aloud. *The judgment is your own. The path is your choice.*

The words settled into her slowly, plain and unadorned, carrying none of the grandeur she might once have expected from anything claiming to speak with this much quiet authority behind it. She found the plainness of it more convincing than any grander pronouncement could have managed. The regulars who'd trusted her most, back on Aldergate Street, had always been the ones who spoke to her exactly like this.

Margaret listened for whatever came next, and found nothing further offered, only the same patient stillness holding steady around her. She had no name to put to the voice, and yet found herself trusting it entirely, the way she'd trusted certain quiet strangers over the years without ever quite being able to say why. She wondered, briefly, whose voice it had actually been, some vast and gentle presence she suspected few living souls had ever been given cause to properly meet. She let the wondering go without needing an answer. Whatever had spoken to her plainly meant her no harm, and that, for now, felt like enough to trust.

She thought, sitting quietly with the voice's words in the stillness that followed, of every sermon she'd sat through across her long life describing judgment as something handed down from above, weighed and measured by a scale she'd never once been permitted to actually see for herself. This felt like something else entirely — a door simply left unlocked, patient enough to wait for her to decide, in her own time, what she actually wanted to walk through.

---

The terror she hadn't quite realized she'd been carrying finally loosened its grip entirely, giving way, slowly, to something closer to wonder. She turned her attention back to the waiting doors, studying each one in turn now that fear no longer crowded out her actual curiosity about them.

One door, nearest to her left, carried behind it a deep and settled quiet, the particular hush of a long, well-earned rest, a place built for nothing more demanding than simply being still after a lifetime spent in motion. She lingered there a moment, tempted by the plain comfort of it, thinking of every long, tiring evening she'd spent closing up the library alone, shelving the last stray returns before finally locking the front doors behind her. Rest had always sounded like exactly what she'd have chosen, on any one of those tired evenings, if someone had actually offered it to her outright. She let her attention drift onward regardless, curious what the other doors might hold before she settled on anything.

A second door opened onto something she could only half comprehend, glimpses of a world entirely unlike her own, unfamiliar colors moving across an unfamiliar sky, the promise of an entire new life waiting to be lived from its very first breath. She studied it a long moment too, curious in a way that surprised her, some part of her that had never quite finished wondering, across every quiet evening she'd spent alone with a book instead of a family of her own, what a different life might have actually looked like. She had read a thousand such lives across her years behind that library counter, had lived vicariously through every one of them without ever quite envying the characters who lived them. She found herself, studying this door, genuinely tempted for the first time by the idea of living one instead of simply reading about it.

A third door, smaller and plainer than the other two, carried behind it something she recognized instantly and completely — the familiar hush of the reading room on Aldergate Street, dust motes drifting gold through afternoon light, the particular quiet of a place she had loved every single day of the sixty-one years she'd spent tending it. She could almost hear it, the soft creak of the front door's hinge that she'd meant to oil for years and never quite gotten around to, the particular rustle of pages turning in the reading nook by the window where the schoolchildren always gathered. She understood, studying that door, exactly what waited on the other side of it: not another life, not simple rest, but the chance to remain, gently, as whatever quiet presence a place like that sometimes seemed to carry, watching over the readers who would keep wandering through its doors long after her name had faded from anyone's memory but the building's own.

She thought of every patron she'd ever helped find exactly the right book on exactly the right afternoon, of every quiet child she'd watched fall in love with reading in that same worn armchair by the window. She thought of the young boy who'd come in every Tuesday for two full years asking for anything with dragons in it, of the elderly widower who'd started visiting just to sit somewhere that still felt occupied. She found she wasn't ready, not yet, to let go of that particular joy entirely, not when there was still so much of it left to quietly keep watch over.

Margaret smiled, the expression settling over her with an ease she hadn't felt in years, and drifted, unhurried, toward the third door. She thought, crossing that last small distance, of every reader she had never actually met yet, every quiet child who would one day wander through those front doors looking for a story worth losing an afternoon to, every tired soul who might simply need somewhere warm and quiet to sit for an hour before facing whatever waited for them outside. She found she was rather looking forward to meeting all of them, in whatever quiet, unseen way she'd now be able to.

The other two doors remained exactly where she'd left them, patient and unhurried, neither one troubled in the slightest by her choosing something else instead, their soft light still glowing steady behind her, waiting, she suspected, for whatever other soul might eventually wander close enough to actually need them. She understood, reaching for the third door's worn handle, that the choice had never actually needed to be a permanent one at all — only the right one, for now, for whatever came next in whatever new and unhurried shape her own small, quiet story was about to take.
