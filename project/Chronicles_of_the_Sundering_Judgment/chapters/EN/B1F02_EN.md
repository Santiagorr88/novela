# B1F02 – What the Tide Smells

Stolas found Vepar exactly where he expected to find him, standing at the edge of a still black pool deep within his own domain, water that answered to him the way ordinary water answered to gravity. A single dark thread curled through the pool as Stolas approached, unmistakably blood, though Stolas could see no wound anywhere near the water's edge to account for it — some trace Vepar's own dominion had drawn here from some distant battlefield, carried through whatever channel only a Commander of his own gift could actually perceive. Vepar did not look up. "Foras is weak," he said, without preamble, his voice carrying that same resonant quality of something spoken from very deep water even here, standing on dry stone. "And weakness is only another fluid, waiting to find its level." He gestured once at the thread of blood still curling through the pool. "This one's his. Thinner than it should be, this long after a battle that ended days ago."

Stolas inclined his head, unsurprised by either the observation or the lack of any greeting preceding it. Vepar rarely wasted words on courtesy when a direct order would do the same work more efficiently, a habit Stolas had come, across years of service under him, to genuinely appreciate rather than resent. Other commanders wrapped their instructions in enough ceremony to waste half a morning before the actual substance ever arrived. Vepar simply told a subordinate what he wanted, offering nothing further by way of instruction on how to actually manage it, which suited Stolas's own temperament considerably better than anything more elaborate would have.

"Go and learn exactly how far the tide has gone out," Vepar continued, still watching the water. Stolas considered the instruction a moment before responding, choosing his own words with the same care he brought to anything that might later be quoted back to him. "Am I looking for anything specific," he asked, "or just whatever happens to be floating?" Vepar's mouth curved, faint and unhurried. "Everything floats, eventually. You only need to collect it before anyone else does."

Stolas withdrew to his own working chambers within the hour, Umbra Manus already open across his desk, its pages the particular deep black that seemed to swallow rather than reflect whatever light happened to fall across the room around it. He had never especially enjoyed fieldwork, the crude business of moving bodies through physical space to gather information that could, with sufficient patience, simply be extracted from the fabric of the world itself. Umbra Manus rewarded exactly that patience, each spell within it purchased at the cost of some portion of the caster's own remaining years, a price Stolas had long since made his peace with in exchange for precision no cruder method could match.

He began with the oldest bindings first, incantations that reached backward through recorded time rather than forward through space, unearthing the layered history of Foras's holdings one careful excavation at a time. The chamber around him settled into the hush he reserved for deep excavation, every ordinary distraction set aside in favor of the slow, methodical unspooling of a household's accumulated history — pacts sworn and broken, allegiances shifted, small compromises made decades or centuries ago that no one currently active in Foras's court likely even remembered existed.

Stolas refused himself the comfort of not knowing. Working through Foras's buried history, he found exactly the inconvenient detail his method had always promised. Most of his colleagues under Vepar's banner preferred results delivered quickly, results that looked impressive without necessarily being especially reliable. Stolas had built his own considerable reputation on the opposite principle: better a slow, thorough path than a fast, comfortable one, and he had yet to regret the choice.

The pages of Umbra Manus turned themselves as he worked, each incantation drawing him deeper into records long since buried under centuries of more recent, more urgent business. He passed over dozens of minor findings without pausing — small debts, forgotten grudges, the ordinary accumulated debris of any household this old — searching instead for the specific kind of thread that would actually justify the cost of pulling it. Vepar's order had called for leverage, not gossip, and leverage required precision most casual excavation never bothered to achieve.

The excavation finally yielded something Stolas trusted: not a vague rumor, but a name. He found it sealed beneath a binding-ward he recognized immediately as Malthus's own work, a ritual signature unmistakable to anyone who had studied Malthus's craft as closely as Stolas had — a false genealogy layered over the true record, designed to make a careless reader believe the pact had lapsed decades ago when in fact it had not. Unpicking it cost him three further incantations and a correspondingly steeper toll, though the name beneath it, once fully exposed, more than justified the expense: Threll, a minor Squad Leader within Foras's household, bound to him by pact rather than by any deeper loyalty, who had apparently begun quietly maintaining contact with an emissary from a rival household entirely unconnected to Vepar. The pact itself, sworn to secure a grain shipment through contested territory two decades past, had never formally been dissolved — a loose thread Foras's court, so far as the record suggested, believed Malthus's ward had closed for good.

He traced the thread further, reconstructing the shape of the original binding with the same patient care he brought to any excavation. The pact had been sworn decades ago, in circumstances the record suggested had felt, at the time, entirely routine — a minor accommodation made to secure a temporary alliance that had long since outlived its original purpose. The absence of any later annotation on the binding suggested to Stolas that whoever had negotiated it on Foras's behalf had not expected it to matter again, and had simply left it buried under decades of business that mattered more urgently, until Stolas's own careful digging brought it back into the light.

What struck him most, turning the finding over, was not the pact itself but the silence surrounding it. No record anywhere in Foras's own household suggested anyone had reviewed this arrangement in years, possibly decades. A household this large, this old, inevitably accumulated forgotten obligations of this precise shape, small debts owed to parties no longer relevant enough to warrant active attention. The question, as always in Stolas's line of work, was not whether such debts existed. It was whether anyone still knew where to find them, and whether that knowledge could be turned into something useful before the opposing side thought to look first.

Stolas held the finding a long moment, weighing its usefulness against the effort it had cost to extract, turning the name over in his mind the way he turned over any piece of evidence before committing to what it actually meant. A single wavering loyalty, on its own, meant relatively little in the grand architecture of a household this large. As confirmation of what Vepar's own order had already led him to suspect, though, it meant a great deal more — that Foras's foundations, however solid they still appeared from the outside, had begun quietly rotting in places no one currently watching had thought to check.

He allowed himself, closing the grimoire on this thread, a small measure of the dry satisfaction that came from confirming precisely what everyone else had merely guessed at. The craftsmanship of the false genealogy suggested Malthus had considered it sufficient to bury the pact for good — the sort of confidence that came from underestimating exactly how much information a sufficiently patient scholar could extract without ever needing to ask a single living soul a single question.

"Foras's people," Stolas murmured to the empty chamber, more for his own amusement than any actual audience, "still think secrets stay buried simply because no one's dug recently." He found the thought considerably funnier than it perhaps deserved, though he had long since stopped apologizing to himself for the genuine pleasure this particular brand of quiet, patient work gave him. Other Captains under Vepar's banner preferred the loud satisfaction of a battlefield kill. Stolas envied them nothing about the noise.

He considered, allowing himself a further moment of dry amusement, how many of Hell's own Captains treated ignorance as some kind of shield rather than the vulnerability it actually was. A commander who did not know his own household's buried debts could not defend against them being called due at the worst possible moment. Foras, whatever his considerable strengths as a battlefield commander, had — so far as Stolas could tell — never valued this particular kind of vigilance the way Vepar's own court did. That gap, more than any single secret Stolas had actually managed to extract today, struck him as the more useful finding by far.

He had met Malthus only twice across the years, each time at some careful diplomatic remove where neither side said anything of real substance. He had come away from both encounters with the same settled impression — a mystic thoroughly devoted to ritual and loyalty, a good deal less devoted to the tedious, unglamorous business of actually auditing what that loyalty was worth in practice. Stolas did not consider that a flaw exactly. He considered it, rather, precisely the kind of opening a patient scholar like himself existed to exploit.

He felt the toll the working had taken only once he finally set the grimoire aside, a familiar, hollow weariness settling into him the way it always settled after this particular kind of excavation — not physical exhaustion exactly, but something closer to a small, permanent withdrawal from whatever span of years he still had left to spend. Umbra Manus never asked for more than the working actually required. It also never once offered a discount for results Stolas himself judged worth the price. He had made this particular trade often enough across his long service to no longer flinch from the sensation, though he had also never quite managed to stop noticing it either.

He allowed himself a moment to simply sit with the weariness before moving on to anything else, an old habit he had never bothered breaking. Some of his colleagues under Vepar's banner treated the cost of their own gifts as an inconvenience to be pushed past as quickly as possible. Stolas had always found more value in acknowledging it plainly instead, a small private accounting of exactly what tonight's finding had actually cost him to obtain. It made the eventual results feel earned rather than simply handed to him, a distinction he suspected mattered more to his own satisfaction than it would have to anyone watching from outside.

He rested only long enough to steady himself before beginning the report he would carry back to Vepar, unwilling to let a night pass between a finding this useful and the moment his commander actually learned of it. He had learned, early in his service under Vepar, that information delayed even briefly tended to lose a measurable portion of its value, opportunities closing quietly while a report sat unread on someone's desk. He did not intend to let tonight's finding suffer that fate.

He drafted the report carefully, choosing his words with the same precision he brought to any excavation, cross-checking each claim against the record twice before committing it to the page, unwilling to overstate a single Squad Leader's wavering loyalty as something larger than it currently was. In Stolas's experience, Vepar valued precision over enthusiasm every time, and Stolas had built his own standing in this court specifically by giving his commander no cause, ever, to doubt the accuracy of anything he reported. Exaggeration was, in its own way, another form of the ignorance he scorned — a comfortable inflation of a finding's importance rather than an honest account of exactly what it was worth.

Vepar received the finding the same way he received everything — without visible surprise, though something in the stillness of the water beside him shifted subtly at the detail Stolas offered, a ripple with no obvious cause that Stolas noted carefully but chose not to remark on aloud. "A wavering loyalty," Vepar said finally, turning the information over with the same unhurried patience he brought to any calculation. "Small. But small cracks widen, given the right pressure in the right place."

"There will be more," Stolas said. "Foras's household has not been audited this thoroughly in longer than most of his current Captains have served him. I doubt this is the only loyalty that's drifted since." Vepar's translucent features shifted into something that might, on a less unsettling face, have passed for satisfaction. "Then keep pulling the thread," he said. "Foras's tide has gone out further than even he knows. I intend to see exactly how far before the water comes back in."

"And if Malthus notices someone's been digging?" Stolas asked, more out of professional thoroughness than any real concern, already fairly confident of what answer his commander would give him. Vepar's expression did not change. "Then he'll know what every commander eventually learns — that the tide doesn't announce itself before it turns. It simply turns, and by the time anyone notices, the shore already looks different." He turned back toward the still black water, and Stolas took the gesture as the conversation's end. "Bring me the next thread when you have it."

Stolas inclined his head and withdrew, already turning over in his own mind which buried record he intended to excavate next. He had not expected this particular assignment to yield anything beyond a single minor confirmation of what Vepar's order had suggested he already suspected. Finding an actual name, an actual thread worth pulling, had exceeded even his own considerable confidence in his method. He intended to make the most of that unexpected fortune, one careful excavation at a time, for exactly as long as Foras's household continued providing this much material to work with.
