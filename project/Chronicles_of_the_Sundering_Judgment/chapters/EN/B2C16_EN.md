# B2C16 – The Riddle of the Laughing Smith

The light kept sinking regardless of what Mikel decided to do about it, and he made his peace with that the way he'd made his peace with every other impossible constraint this island had handed him since the fog first swallowed the mainland behind him. He returned to the anvil with the last honest hour of daylight he could count on, and laid both hands flat against its worn, ancient face this time instead of one, some instinct insisting that the earlier, tentative touch hadn't been enough to ask the stone whatever question it was still refusing to answer.

The warmth came first, faint and impossible in metal this old, this thoroughly abandoned — a ghost of heat with no visible source, the residue of a forge fire that should have gone cold centuries before anyone alive today had been born. He held his palms against it a long moment, feeling it pulse very faintly beneath his skin, patient in a way that felt less like coincidence and more like something old finally noticing it had company.

He thought of every ruin he'd ever studied properly, every excavation where stone this age had long since surrendered whatever warmth its makers had once poured into it. None of them had prepared him for this. This forge did not feel abandoned the way ruins felt abandoned. It felt, beneath his palms, more like something merely resting between one use and the next. He glanced once toward the tree line, gauging how much of the fading light he still had left to spend here, and pressed his hands more firmly against the stone.

He was aware, distantly, of how this would read in any paper he might one day write about it — a historian abandoning every principle of careful, skeptical inquiry to press both palms flat against a rock and wait for a vision to arrive. He had stopped caring, somewhere in the last several months, whether the discipline that had built his entire career still applied to whatever this search actually was. The old rules had been built for evidence that behaved the way evidence was supposed to behave. Nothing about this island had behaved that way since the moment the fog first swallowed the boat.

---

The vision arrived without warning, breaking over him the way a wave breaks over a swimmer who has misjudged the tide. He had expected, if he'd expected anything at all, something closer to the somber weight of his own recurring dreams — grief, judgment, the crushing scale of divine consequence he'd spent months learning to associate with anything touching this search. What found him instead was joy.

Raw, chaotic, undirected joy, energy that moved without purpose or apology, sparks that laughed as they scattered rather than burning with the grim purposefulness he'd expected from any weapon born of war. He felt, more than saw, a presence at the center of it all — not a figure exactly, nothing his mind could resolve into features or form, only a raw, delighted laughter that rang through the vision like a bell struck in genuine, unguarded pleasure. It bore no resemblance to the reverent, terrible creation myths he'd spent a career studying. This felt like something made for the sheer, uncomplicated pleasure of making it.

He saw fragments rather than a coherent scene — metal folding under a hammer that struck not out of anger but out of something closer to delight, sparks arcing away in patterns too playful to belong to any serious act of forging he'd ever read described. There was no solemnity in any of it, no sense of a sacred duty being carefully discharged. Whoever had stood at this anvil once had worked the way a child works at something purely for the pleasure of watching it take shape, careless of consequence, untroubled by weight. It was, Mikel understood even as the vision continued to unspool around him, the single strangest creation myth he had ever encountered in a lifetime spent cataloguing them.

---

The laughter did not feel entirely foreign to him, and that unsettled Mikel more than the vision's strangeness ever could have. He thought he knew this sound. Not consciously, not in any way he could have explained to another scholar without sounding like a man losing his grip on his own credibility, but somewhere beneath conscious memory, in the same buried country his recurring dreams had always come from, something in him strained toward this laughter as though it might belong to the same figure who had forged the spear that haunted his nightmares — the dark, judging blade that answered grief with cruelty rather than relief. He could not have said, if pressed, whether that was recognition or simply a mind too tired and too far from home inventing a connection it wanted to find.

He sat with that half-formed suspicion a long moment after the vision released him, turning it over with the careful discipline of a man who had built his whole career on not mistaking coincidence for pattern. He tried, methodically, the way he'd once have approached a disputed source, to trace the sound back to some ordinary origin — a half-remembered lecture, a book he'd skimmed years ago and forgotten reading. Nothing fit cleanly. He could not prove the laughter belonged to the same buried country his dreams came from. He also could not shake the feeling that it did.

For the first time since this search had begun, the question that mattered wasn't *what is this*. It was *why did I already know this*, and he had no honest answer for it yet, only the growing, uncomfortable certainty that an answer existed somewhere just beyond what his own memory was willing to surrender.

---

He searched the anvil's surface properly this time, working outward from the exact spot where his palms had rested, rather than the base and surrounding stone he'd already combed bare hours earlier. The inscription revealed itself only when he cleared away a fine layer of ash that had settled, undisturbed, into a shallow groove worn into the metal — letters in a script his eyes recognized from the old ethnography's footnote, faint but legible once the ash lifted clear.

*I am a verdict given not with anger, but with joy. I cannot be claimed by wrath, only by laughter.*

He read it three times, his fingers tracing the worn grooves of each character as though touch might confirm what his eyes were already certain of. It matched nothing in his memory of the spear's dark, wordless cruelty. It matched everything in the vision's raw, undirected delight. He pulled out the small notebook he'd carried since the archive, hands not quite steady, and copied the phrase down twice, once in the original script as best his untrained hand could render it, once in his own translation, unwilling to trust a discovery this fragile to memory alone.

---

He sat back on his heels and let the full weight of the realization settle into him properly. He had been thinking of this search — of the sword, of judgment itself — entirely wrong from the start. He had approached it the way a soldier approaches a weapon: something built for war, something that carried grief because grief was simply the tax war always levied on everyone who touched it. The inscription suggested something else entirely. A verdict that could not be claimed by wrath. A judgment that answered cruelty not with more cruelty but with something closer to relief.

He thought of the spear from his dreams, of the specific, patient malice in every judgment it delivered, and found himself circling a conclusion he had no way yet to confirm: that the two weapons might not be simply different tools built for the same grim purpose, but something closer to opposites — two answers to the same question, arriving at conclusions that could hardly have been further apart. He held the thought loosely, unwilling to trust it as settled fact on the strength of one inscription and one vision, however certain it felt sitting here in the dark.

It raised a question he had no comfortable place to set down. If one weapon answered cruelty with more cruelty, and the other answered it with something closer to mercy, what did it mean that his own dreams had only ever shown him the first, and never once, not in all these months, offered him a glimpse of the second? He had assumed, without ever quite examining the assumption, that whatever haunted his sleep was the whole of the story. Sitting here with the inscription's plain, contradictory claim in front of him, he understood for the first time that he might have only ever been shown half of it.

---

The boat waited exactly where the fisherman had promised it would, though the sun had fully set by the time Mikel finally stumbled back down to the shore, breathless from a scramble through undergrowth he could no longer properly see. He had half expected to find the shallows empty, the promise from that morning quietly revoked the way promises so often were once the cost of keeping them became real. The fisherman only glanced once at his face, taking in whatever the last few hours had left written there, and said nothing about the lateness at all — only pushed off from the shallows the moment Mikel climbed aboard, his oars finding their rhythm in the near-total dark with the ease of someone who had made this crossing blind more than once before.

"Last time I brought someone here," the fisherman said, several long strokes into the silence, "they didn't come back the same either."

Mikel found, to his own surprise, that he had courage enough tonight to push the question further than he had on the way in. "Did they come back better, or worse?"

The fisherman took a full stroke of the oars before he answered, the boat gliding forward into darkness that gave no hint of the mainland still somewhere ahead of them. "Came back with a new question instead of an answer," he said finally. "Same as you, I'd guess."

"What was theirs?" Mikel asked, though some part of him already suspected he wasn't going to get an answer worth the asking.

"Not mine to tell." The fisherman's voice carried no unkindness in the refusal, only the flat, settled finality of a man who sounded, to Mikel's ear, like someone who had made his peace with a boundary a long time ago and had no intention of revisiting it tonight. "Same as yours won't be mine to tell, once you've had time to sit with it properly." He said nothing further, and Mikel understood, watching the set of the man's shoulders in the dark, that nothing further was coming tonight regardless of how he asked for it.

He let the silence stand, and let his hand find the notebook still tucked against his ribs, the inscription's careful copy safe inside it, and sat with his own new question the rest of the long crossing home. The fog thinned as they went, the mainland's scattered lights finally resolving somewhere ahead of the bow, and Mikel found himself, for the first time in longer than he could easily place, less afraid of what he didn't yet understand than curious about it — a small, unfamiliar shift, and one he suspected, turning the inscription's words over once more in his mind, he owed to whatever had laughed at him from inside that vision, whoever or whatever it actually turned out to be.
