Chapter 24. The Path of Laughter.

The instant his fingers closed around the last word of the inscription, the space of Turein collapsed all at once, unevenly. The silver forge, the warm coastal fog, and the shadowless light of the spiral trees dissolved into a pale dust that withdrew from his mind in a single held breath.

Mikel woke with a physical jolt, on his knees on the cold flagstones of his own office in Navarion's humanities building. The afternoon's golden light came in flat through the tall corner window, cutting across the ordinary clutter of his black pine desk. His coat was rumpled, his forehead damp with sweat, and his right hand closed tight — with the rehearsed reflex of an old habit — around the blackened piece of metal that always sat as a paperweight beside his cold cup of coffee.

Beneath his skin, the old scar on his right palm gave off a single pulse of real heat and went dark before he could doubt his own waking. Ignoring the ache in his ribs and the dull throb in his chest, he crawled to the desk, opened his notebook with trembling fingers, and wrote down the anvil's inscription before the ordinary forgetfulness of the afternoon could scrape the ink from his mind. There had been no boats, no roads, no distance on any map. The secret of the laughter had just woken directly beneath the stone of his own ordinary lectern.

He stayed seated at his black pine desk for the better part of the afternoon, staring at the four lines of the inscription with the dispassionate fixity of a surgeon absorbing an unexpected diagnosis. The taste of ozone and ash from the projection lingered at the back of his throat, reminding him that the border between his dreams and his chalk-dust routine was no longer as thin as the humanities dean's office would have considered safe. He hadn't traveled a single real mile — his middle-aged body hadn't left that office at all — but the map of his reality had rearranged itself in one disorienting heartbeat.

He set aside his siege slides and his military campaigns at once. He began instead a systematic deconstruction in his notebook, so feverish that any colleague glancing over his shoulder would have diagnosed the final collapse of his career. He hunted for myths of holy fools, trickster gods, and mercurial smiths who forged "with a hammer that never once struck out of anger," chasing the forge's mystical laughter through centuries of scattered manuscripts.

...

The archive work consumed an entire week of afternoons in a kind of siege-quiet, and Mikel caught himself skipping meals, surfacing from his research only when the library's fluorescent lights shut off on the hallway timer.

The special collections archivist found him there on Tuesday evening, the returns cart balanced on one hip, watching him from the doorway with the same meticulous attention she'd give a mis-shelved call number.

"This isn't military history anymore, Ardon," she said, her office voice dry and without ornament. "I've seen you from the front desk. You've been chasing the same idea through this catalogue for months. Are you ever going to tell me what you're looking for down here?"

"I still don't know how to explain it to myself," Mikel admitted, with a small, tired, genuine smile. "Give me a little more time before I file for early retirement with the dean."

She laughed at that, with the patience of someone who decides not to force open a door that's already opening slowly on its own. She set an uncatalogued volume down on the corner of the pine desk and tapped its cracked spine with one finger before turning back toward the hallway.

"There's a regional folklore collection in the basement, filed under 'miscellaneous,' in paper folders that never made it into the digital system," she said, already walking away. "If whatever you're chasing has any roots near this river, you're more likely to find it down there than in anything properly indexed by the department."

...

The final trail turned up exactly where the archivist had told him to look. Mikel spent two full days among the basement's forgotten shelves, coated in the same damp lime as the foundation, before he found the uncatalogued volume tucked in a neglected corner, titled in tired lettering:

*"Folktales of the Navarion Valley."*

He sat at the archive's one pine table, under the steady hum of the fluorescent tube, and opened the brittle pages with fingers that trembled around his flashlight. His pulse quickened in his chest as the valley's local accounts of the wandering bard matched, line for line, the mystical chord of Turein's forge.

The manuscript described the bard healing the torn tendons of the wounded not with salves or the discipline of war, but by mending the weary with nothing more than the chord of his own laughter. Mikel ran a hand over his injured shoulder, and the certainty settled into his sternum with the fixity of a well-cut stone. The bard wasn't a myth confined to some impossibly distant island. He had been waiting the whole time beneath the foundations of the very library Mikel crossed every morning in his ordinary chalk-dusted life.

...

Mikel left the humanities building well past midnight, the volume of folktales pressed under his arm like an unsheathed blade. The night had turned cold enough that his breath showed white under the walkway's lamps, and the campus, at that hour, belonged to him entirely — a wasteland of empty stone benches and silent sycamores.

He was about to open his car door when he made out the figure at the park's western edge, under the one lamp whose light flickered steadily with the damp. It was a barefoot street musician, sitting motionless on the low stone wall, his guitar case closed at his feet and his sun-bleached hair falling loose over the collar of his coat. He wasn't playing; he simply sat watching the library's old stone facade with a stillness that stopped Mikel cold on the wet gravel.

Mikel didn't raise a hand in greeting, and the musician didn't move in the shadows either, but through the freezing drizzle he felt a low, steady pulse move through his chest — the echo of a second fragment his unveiled memory had only just begun to recover. He got into his car in silence and drove home through the drizzle, certain, at last, that the first chord of the real path had become unmistakable beneath the stone of Navarion.
