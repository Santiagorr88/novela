Chapter 8. An Echo in the Archives.

The proposal Mikel had submitted to the department chair three weeks ago read, on paper, like a natural continuation of his existing work — Mythological Echoes in Pre-Modern Military History, a survey of the folk narratives that grew up around real campaigns, the way legend accreted onto fact until the two became inseparable. It was the kind of title that raised no eyebrows and required no explanation, the sort of research a man could disappear into for months without anyone thinking to ask what he was actually looking for. He had chosen the wording carefully, in the specific, guilty way a man chooses a lie he intends to keep telling himself as well as everyone else.

He was not, if he was honest with the version of himself that only surfaced at two in the morning, researching echoes of war anymore. He didn't yet have a proper word for what he was researching — a persistent, contradictory shape that kept surfacing whenever he let his mind wander too far from the material he was supposed to be teaching, a sense that the judgment he dreamed of night after night was not the whole of the story, only the part that had happened to land on him. More mornings than not, he sat in his office with a stack of folklore anthologies that had nothing to do with his actual field, chasing a single half-formed idea through every culture that had ever bothered to write anything down about the men and gods who dispensed verdicts: that somewhere, in some telling he hadn't found yet, judgment and mercy might not have been opposites at all. He didn't know yet what he was looking for well enough to give it a proper name. He only knew that when he found the right thread, something in his chest — that same old, familiar absence — would recognize it before his mind caught up.

...

He found the footnote on a Tuesday, deep in the university's special collections, in a section of the stacks so rarely visited that the air itself smelled of old paper and undisturbed dust, and the librarian had to consult a paper ledger to locate the shelf at all. The book itself was unremarkable — a privately printed ethnography of an island culture, the kind of academic vanity project that got printed in editions of two hundred and then forgotten by everyone except the estate that had funded it. Mikel had pulled it almost at random, following a citation trail three sources removed from anything relevant, and had nearly set it back down twice before his eye caught on a single line buried in a footnote near the end of the third chapter.

The islanders speak of a sky-smith who forged a sword not from divine rage, but from a star's laughter.

He read it twice, and then a third time, the hair on his arms rising for reasons that had nothing to do with the archive's poor climate control. The author had dismissed the line as "fanciful nonsense, likely a corruption of a more conventional creation myth," and moved on without a second thought. Mikel could not move on. He sat with the book open on his knees for what felt like minutes and turned out, when he finally checked his watch, to have been the better part of an hour, the light through the high archive windows shifting from morning to early afternoon without his noticing it pass.

...

He had been sitting there long enough that the archivist noticed. She was someone he knew mostly by sight and occasional nod in the corridor — competent, a little sharp-tongued, the kind of person who ran the deep stacks with the quiet proprietary care of someone who considered the entire collection at least partly her own responsibility. She stopped at the end of his table with a cart of returns balanced on one hip and looked at him for a long moment before she spoke.

"You've been sitting with that same book since before my lunch break," she said. "Either it's the most gripping thing this collection owns, or you've fallen asleep with your eyes open. I've seen professors do both."

"Comparative folklore," Mikel said, which was true in the way a half-truth is true, technically accurate and entirely misleading. "I'm chasing down a citation trail for a new project. Turns out it goes deeper than I expected."

"Comparative folklore." She raised an eyebrow, not quite believing him and not quite willing to say so outright. "You've published exactly nothing on folklore in nine years, Ardon. You do sieges. You do doomed garrisons. Are you secretly writing a novel and hiding it behind a grant proposal?"

"Would you think less of me if I were?"

"I'd think you were finally doing something with your evenings besides marking." She glanced at the book properly for the first time, at the cracked spine and the faded, water-stained cover, and her expression shifted toward genuine curiosity. "That whole section, honestly — nobody comes back here anymore. I had to check the paper ledger to find you a call number, because it's not even in the digital catalogue properly. The last time anyone requested anything from that shelf was over twenty years ago, according to the log, and I don't even recognize the name who signed it out. Some visiting researcher, I think. Long before my time here."

"Do you remember what they were researching?"

"No idea. The log just has a name and a date. Nobody writes down why anymore, not back then." She shrugged, already turning back toward her cart. "Let me know if you need help finding anything else down here. It's nice having a reason to dust off a section nobody remembers exists."

She left him with the ledger detail sitting oddly in his mind, a small stray fact with no obvious use yet. He didn't know why it mattered that someone else had once stood exactly where he was standing, chasing something in this same forgotten corner of the archive. He kept it anyway, the same way he'd learned to keep the ache in his chest and the dreams he couldn't place — not understood, simply held, on the quiet theory that understanding would eventually catch up if he refused to let go of any of it first.

...

The footnote, once he chased it properly, opened onto a longer passage he'd nearly missed on his first pass — a description, secondhand and clearly embellished by generations of retelling, of a weapon that "judged with irreverent joy," forged by a smith the islanders called — in a phrase that didn't translate cleanly — the one who laughs at what the world takes seriously. The sword's judgments, the text insisted, were absolute — no criminal it turned against ever escaped its verdict — and yet never, in any version of the story the author had collected, had it left behind grief. Only, strangely, relief. A weight lifted rather than a wound delivered.

It was the exact opposite of everything Mikel remembered from the only judgment he'd ever felt land against his own body in a dream — a spear of pure, wrong-colored dark, driving into him with no laughter in it at all, only an old and patient cruelty. He read the passage a fourth time, trying to reconcile the two, and could not. He had no evidence the two things were even related. He only knew, with a certainty he couldn't yet justify to anyone, least of all himself, that he wanted them to be.

...

The text named a place before it exhausted itself into further speculation the author clearly hadn't trusted enough to commit to with confidence: an island, shrouded in enough contradictory legend and navigational myth that the author had spent an entire paragraph simply cataloguing the ways different informants disagreed about where, precisely, it could be found. One elder swore it lay three days' sail past a reef that appeared on no chart. Another insisted it could not be found by sailing toward it at all, only by sailing away from something else and letting the island find you instead. The author had thrown up his hands, in prose that carried the particular exasperation of a scholar who'd expected simple geography and been handed a riddle. Turein. Mikel stared at the name until it stopped looking like a collection of letters and started looking, instead, like the first solid ground he'd stood on in weeks. Not a myth. Not a feeling he couldn't name. A place, on a map, however unreliable that map might prove to be — and somewhere he might finally be able to walk toward instead of sitting still and waiting for the next dream to tell him more than the last one had.

He closed the book with hands that weren't entirely steady, the dry, papery smell of it still caught in the back of his throat, and did not return it to the shelf.

...

He called the dean's office from his own that same afternoon, before the day's ordinary discipline had time to talk him out of it. He rehearsed the request twice at the grey window overlooking the quad, turning the wording over with the fluency guilt lends to careful men, and delivered it on the second ring with a steadiness that surprised his own throat.

"Turein." The dean repeated the name with the flat skepticism of an administrator who has to account for a department's ledgers before winter freezes them shut. "I'll be honest with you, Ardon — I had to check the faculty atlas twice just now to confirm the rock even exists on paper. Are you asking me for a field-research line to go chase down an uncatalogued myth off the northern coast?"

"I think," Mikel said, and the words came out stripped of his usual academic hedging, low and carrying a sincerity that asked no one's permission to be true, "that this could be the most important research I've done in ten years in this department."

There was a long pause on the line, long enough that Mikel wondered whether the old copper connection had simply failed somewhere under the rain. When the dean spoke again, the dryness had gone out of his voice, replaced by one of those small kindnesses men allow themselves only when they suspect a colleague standing watch beside them is close to breaking under the cold. "You know something, Ardon — in ten years, you have never once asked this department for a single day off. Not sick leave, not family leave, nothing. Ten years of perfectly punctual attendance, and I've never once had to worry, on any given morning, whether your lecture hall would be sitting empty at eight o'clock. But the faculty committee just froze the extraordinary research line. There's no budget for field travel outside the indexed programs — and certainly not for a rock the navy's own charts have written off since the age of the quill. The department can't approve this trip, Ardon. I'm sorry, on paper and off it."

Mikel felt the familiar absence in his chest widen, cold, but this time carrying a strange, quiet clarity he hadn't expected. "I understand," he said, quietly.

"Are you actually all right, Ardon?" the dean added, and the personal question carried none of the office's formality, only honest concern. "I don't mean the budget. I saw you in the hallway this morning. You walk like a man three weeks behind on something nobody in this faculty ever assigned him. Are you genuinely all right?"

"I'm fine," Mikel said. He heard the falseness of the sentence land on the line like a copper coin dropped wrong on stone, giving back the flat, hollow sound of something counterfeit. The dean didn't push; he was courteous enough to know when a closed door preferred staying that way, and he inclined his voice into something like a nod before he hung up with a brief wish of good luck.

Mikel sat with the phone still warm against his ear, looking at the old ethnography still open on the desk in front of him. The physical journey to Turein was dead in the department's ledgers now; the island stayed exactly where it had always been — unreachable, wrapped in the fishermen's contradictions, confined strictly to the page of the archive. For the first time in months of mute grief, he felt something that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite hope, but sat close enough to both that he could no longer tell, alone in the darkening office, which of the two was winning the vigil. Turein would remain a legend he couldn't reach. But under his own feet, on the silent, uncatalogued shelves of Navarion, the echo of Solmire's laughter was still waiting for his gloved hand to work up the nerve to shake the dust from it.
