Chapter 22. The Riddle of the Laughing Smith.

The light over the island of Turein kept sinking into an impossible, frigid dusk, and Mikel made his peace with the strangeness the same way his mind had already made peace with the thick fog that had swallowed the coast at his back the instant the retreat began. In this mystical border there was no physical sea to measure against any ordinary ledger, only a thick, dry cold rising off the stone of the ancient, abandoned forge. Mikel approached the anvil of the vision with his hands bare, pulled forward by an ache beating in his chest with the fixity of an inheritance no amount of sleeplessness could silence.

When he pressed both palms flat against the cold, worn surface of the star-metal, the blunt edges seemed to give along some invisible seam. The clean-edged vacancy in his sternum answered this time not with the rust-fisted grip of his ordinary nightmares, but with a tempered, vibrant chord that spread through his temples, letting him weigh a debt older than the Schism itself.

...

The laughter that suddenly filled the vision's silent clearing was not strange to him at all, and that very familiarity unsettled him more than any real enemy's hostility ever could have. Through the layers of his mystical trance, Mikel witnessed no orderly workshop of white serge, no solemn military ceremonies from Heaven's War Council. He saw instead an immense, chaotic vortex of stars collapsing beneath the hammer of a craftsman forging purely for the joy of it, with no ledger to balance and no plans to render to any scribe.

He thought he recognized this raw delight — not from his ordinary nine-o'clock lectures, but from the same buried country his dreams of the dark sword had always come from, the one that judged with mourning and cruelty. He felt that amoral laughter as the exact opposite of the mace in his nightmares. Standing at the center of the silver clearing, Mikel understood that Solmire had never been conceived as an instrument of punishment. It had been shaped as a promise of restoration and relief, forged out of pure joy.

...

Mikel knelt on the floor of the mystical forge, brushing away with his fingers a thin layer of ash that had settled undisturbed in a worn groove of the stone. The ash still carried a faint smell of ozone and freshly doused smoke, and beneath it he could make out letters carved in the same ancient script that had been haunting his humanities notes for weeks. He traced each character with his fingertips, and the translation of the riddle settled into his sternum with the weight of a well-cut stone:

*"I am a verdict given not with anger, but with joy. I cannot be claimed by wrath, only by laughter."*

He read it three times in the silence of the mystical afternoon, and understood the immense tragedy of his own repetition. Until that moment, his sleeplessness had only ever let him read half the book — the mourning of the one who suffers under the stone. The other half, the one that answered the world's rust with an irreverent joy, was still waiting to be claimed beneath the flagstones.
