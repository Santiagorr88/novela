Chapter 14. The Herb of Stillness.

The feeling had followed him for three blocks before Mikel let himself admit he'd noticed it at all. It sat somewhere behind his sternum, a low, steady pressure that had nothing to do with the late hour or the empty street or the quality of silence a university town took on once the last lecture hall had emptied for the night. He told himself, as he'd told himself every night for the better part of a week now, that it was nothing — a mind that had spent too long lately on judgment and mercy and impossible swords, tired enough to start manufacturing threats to match the mood it had been living in. He had built an entire career on the discipline of not mistaking pattern for coincidence. He was, he reminded himself, breaking his own rule tonight for no better reason than exhaustion.

He did not turn around. Turning around would have meant admitting there was something behind him worth turning for, and admitting that felt like the first step toward believing every strange thing his own mind had been feeding him since the dreams began. He kept walking instead, hands in his coat pockets, his pace neither faster nor slower than it had been outside the archive twenty minutes earlier, and told the pressure behind his sternum, silently and without much conviction, that it was simply going to have to wait until he was somewhere with better lighting to be taken seriously.

The campus thinned out behind him block by block, lecture halls giving way to the quieter residential streets he'd walked home along for nine years without once giving the route a second thought. He knew every crack in this pavement, every house whose porch light never quite worked, and the familiarity of it had always been a comfort before tonight. Tonight it felt thinner than usual, worn down to something he couldn't quite name, and he watched the dark gaps between streetlights a moment longer than the route had ever required of him before.

...

The attack came in the narrow stretch between two shuttered storefronts, where the streetlight had burned out weeks ago and the university's maintenance requests apparently moved slower than anything else in Mikel's life. He heard nothing before the impact — no footstep, no rush of displaced air, nothing his ordinary senses could have flagged in time to matter. One instant he was walking. The next he was on the ground, the book bag torn from his shoulder and skidding somewhere out of reach, a weight pinning him that felt, in the single confused heartbeat before real fear arrived, entirely too large and too wrong-shaped to belong to anything human.

He looked up into a face his mind kept trying and failing to resolve into anything it recognized. And then, before conscious thought could form a single tactic, it happened.

It was not a reasoned decision; it was an ancient reflex, a spring wound tight across eons that suddenly released itself into the dark with absolute readiness. His muscles remembered. In one instant his shoulder and right arm tensed to execute a perfect deflection-and-counter guard — an impeccable, age-old torque built to turn the creature's claw aside and drive the heel of his palm into the center of its chest in a single clean, geometric arc. The reflex was total, lightning-fast, tracing through the alley's gloom the clean line of the Host's General.

But Mikel Ardon's mortal frame had never been conditioned to carry the violence of that kind of speed. Mid-motion, in the middle of the guard's perfect execution, the muscle fibers and the tendon of his right shoulder — weak and worn thin by years of chalk dust and routine — gave way under the unbearable torque of the reflex uncoiling through them. He felt a horrible, dry crack under his collarbone, followed at once by a searing, tearing pain that ran down his arm like cold lightning. The air caught in his throat in a strangled sound. The tendon had torn under the strain of holding a weight that no longer belonged to it. The perfect guard collapsed before it could land, and Mikel found himself flat on the cold asphalt again, the monster's hand already closing back around his throat, gravely hurt by the very demand of his own reflex, his right arm hanging useless and aching in the gutter.

...

The creature's hand closed around his throat a second time and, in that very instant, simply stopped — not loosened, not eased, only stopped, every ounce of intent behind it draining out of the grip as if the creature had forgotten, mid-motion, what it had reached for him to do. Mikel felt the pressure hang there, arrested, neither tightening further nor releasing, for one long, disorienting heartbeat.

Then he watched whatever crouched above him blink, its half-resolved face losing whatever fixed, hungry purpose had driven it a moment before, its golden eyes fastened suddenly on a stillness it didn't seem to know how to measure. Something had passed through the alley in that instant that Mikel had no name for and, turning it over afterward, could not confidently place. It might have come from somewhere very far off. Or it might, just as easily, have come from nowhere outside the alley at all. He could not rule out that it had reached through him on its way to the creature — some small, unfamiliar thing in his own chest, already spent on a guard that had failed him, answering a threat his hands and his training had failed to answer a second time. He had no way to tell the two possibilities apart, and no one he could ask who wouldn't think him mad for asking.

The creature's grip loosened on its own. It staggered back a step, then another, then turned and fled into the dark at the alley's far end without a single backward glance.

...

Mikel lay on the cold pavement a while before he trusted his own legs enough to sit up, catching his breath every time a movement pulled at his torn right shoulder, his pulse loud enough in his ears to drown out the ordinary night sounds slowly reasserting themselves around him. It was only once he'd gotten his breathing back under some approximation of control that he noticed the leaf.

It lay exactly where the creature had crouched a moment before, pale and faintly luminous even in the alley's near-total dark, its shape unlike anything that grew in any garden or forest Mikel had ever walked through in his life — veins that ran the wrong direction, an edge that seemed to shift faintly when he wasn't looking at it directly. He picked it up with hands that were still not quite steady, half expecting it to dissolve at his touch the way things in dreams so often did. It didn't. It sat solid and cool against his palm, and as his fingers closed around it, something in his chest — that same old, familiar absence he'd carried for as long as he could remember — went quieter than it had in years. Not answered. Simply, briefly, at rest.

He sat there in the mouth of the alley for a long time, turning the leaf over in trembling fingers, and found he could not have said, if anyone had asked him in that moment, whether the calm settling into him belonged to the leaf itself or to something in himself finally recognizing it.

...

He called her before he'd fully decided to, his thumb finding the archivist's number in his contacts with the unthinking speed of a man who needed to hear another human voice more than he needed a good reason to interrupt her evening. She picked up on the third ring, and he heard, even before either of them said anything useful, the small sounds of a life continuing somewhere else entirely — a television murmuring low in the background, the domestic ordinariness of it almost unbearable against what he'd just survived.

"Ardon." Her voice carried a flatness that sounded, to him, like someone deciding in real time whether to be annoyed at the hour. "It's nearly midnight. Please tell me this is about the shelf and not something that could have waited for office hours."

"It's about the shelf," he said, and heard how unconvincing it sounded even as it left his mouth. "I had a — I wanted to ask whether the special collections catalogue has a cross-reference system for the older ethnographies. The uncatalogued ones."

There was a pause on the other end, longer than the question warranted. "You sound strange. Are you all right?"

He opened his mouth to tell her. The words sat right there, close enough to taste — I was attacked in an alley by something that wasn't a person, and my arm tried to move like it knew how to fight and tore itself apart doing it, and then it simply stopped, and I don't know why, and I am sitting on the pavement with a leaf in my hand that doesn't belong to this world — and for one full second he meant to say some version of it, because the alternative was carrying the whole of it alone for one more night in a string of nights that had already gone on far too long.

"I'm just tired," he said instead. "Long day. I'll ask about the catalogue properly tomorrow."

She didn't push, though he could hear, in the small silence that followed, that she didn't entirely believe him either. "Sleep something off, Ardon. We'll pick up the section tomorrow." The line went quiet, and he sat there afterward with the phone still warm against his ear and the leaf still cool in his other hand, and let both silences sit with him a while longer before he finally stood, dusted off his coat with a stifled wince at the pull in his ruined shoulder, and started the walk home under Navarion's leaden sky.

...

He examined the leaf properly once he was back in his own apartment, under a lamp bright enough to banish whatever the alley's darkness might have exaggerated, and found it looked no less impossible in good light than it had in bad. His right shoulder throbbed with a dull, steady insistence that forced him to keep the arm pinned against his chest. He turned the leaf slowly between the fingers of his left hand, watching the faint luminescence shift with the angle, and let a thought surface that he had circled for years without ever letting it land in words this exact.

He had felt borrowed for as long as he could remember. Not unhappy, precisely — his life fit him well enough, on the surface, in the way a life assembled from reasonable choices generally did. But underneath the surface there had always been this: the persistent, low sense of a coat cut for someone else's shoulders, worn every day regardless. He had never let himself say it plainly, not even to the version of himself that surfaced only at two in the morning. He said it now, quietly, to an empty apartment and a leaf that had no business existing.

"I don't think this is my life," he said aloud, and heard how strange the words sounded leaving his mouth, and did not take them back.

He reached no conclusion from saying it. The leaf offered no answer, and the apartment stayed exactly as empty and as quiet as it had been before he'd spoken. But something in him had shifted all the same — not resolved, not explained, only, for the first time, honestly acknowledged. He set the leaf down carefully on the desk beside the old ethnography he still hadn't returned, pressed a cold compress against his injured shoulder, and sat with the borrowed shape of his own life a while longer before he let himself sleep.
