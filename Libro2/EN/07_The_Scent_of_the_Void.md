# Chapter 7 — The Scent of the Void

He stopped taking contracts in the days after the Balkans, without ever quite deciding to. There was no ceremony to it — no call made to sever ties, no debt collected on his own behalf for once — only the slow accumulation of nights spent in cheap internet cafés instead of in transit toward the next target, the fluorescent lights above him humming at a frequency that made his teeth ache after the third hour, cross-referencing reports that had nothing to do with any ledger he'd ever kept. He told himself, in the early days, that it was simple curiosity, the kind any professional might indulge between jobs. By the second week of it he'd stopped bothering to tell himself anything at all, and simply let the search consume the hours a contract would otherwise have filled, one screen and one coffee at a time, in a string of cities he was already beginning to lose track of. Three weeks had passed that way by the time he finally had something worth calling a lead.

The old work hadn't stopped calling. Two brokers he'd worked with for years had reached out with jobs he would once have taken without a second thought — good money, clean targets, nothing that would have troubled his conscience even slightly. He'd turned both down with excuses vague enough to be insulting, and neither broker had pushed, which told him something about how he must have sounded when he said no. He didn't examine that too closely either.

The pull behind his sternum had never gone quiet since the granary. If anything it had sharpened, honed itself over three weeks into something closer to a compass than an ache, and it did not point toward wealth or toward the kind of men his old ledger had once been built to track. It pointed toward absence. Toward places where something had been taken cleanly, without struggle, without the ordinary mess a soul makes leaving a body against its will — no grief left hanging in a room, no unfinished sentence, only a silence with a very particular shape to it, one his body seemed to recognize a half-second before his mind caught up and gave it a name. He had learned, without meaning to learn it, to feel the difference between an ordinary death, which left the world around it disordered in ways he'd spent a lifetime reading, and this other thing — a theft dressed carefully enough to pass, most nights, for nothing worth investigating at all. He was, he suspected, becoming fluent in a language no one had ever taught him, one word of absence at a time.

He found the pattern in a stack of unrelated news items that no single desk editor would ever have thought to connect, because no single desk editor had ever been asked to read all of them at once, in sequence, with three weeks of growing certainty pressing against the back of his skull while he did it. A street artist in one city, gone missing after neighbors reported he'd started painting in colors that didn't exist — not colors that were unusual, witnesses insisted, colors that simply weren't part of the visible world, and yet were somehow undeniably there on the canvas when they looked. A musician two continents over, hospitalized for exhaustion after weeks of playing songs no audience remembered afterward, though every one of them swore, independently, that some old and specific pain in their chest had lifted for exactly as long as the music lasted. A gambler who hadn't lost a single hand in four months, his luck growing so absolute that the casinos had quietly stopped letting him play before he ever had the chance to lose on his own — right up until the week he stopped showing up anywhere at all, his apartment found empty, rent paid three months in advance by a man who had apparently known, on some level, that he wouldn't be needing the space much longer.

Each story, alone, was a curiosity, the kind of thing a local paper ran once and never followed up on. Stacked against each other, cross-referenced by date and by the particular flavor of strangeness each one carried, they read like entries in the same ledger — fledgling gifts, barely awakened, small sparks of something that shouldn't have existed in an ordinary human life at all, each one severed at almost exactly the moment it might have started to matter to the person carrying it.

Something was harvesting them. He didn't yet have a better word for it than that, and some part of him — the part that still ached, some nights, around an absence shaped like a weapon he'd never held — suspected the lack of a better word wasn't an accident of his own ignorance. It was possible no one had ever needed the word before. It was possible he was one of the first people alive to need it at all.

---

A name had started surfacing in the corners of the search by the third week — never stated outright, only glimpsed sideways, in a forum post deleted an hour after it went up, in a financial disclosure that mentioned it once and never again. Charon Corp. Nothing about it yet felt confirmed. It felt, if anything, like a word people used only once, right before they stopped using words about the subject at all.

He made one call he'd been putting off before he let himself chase it any further — to a man he hadn't spoken to in over a year, a broker of information whose loyalty extended exactly as far as whichever payment had arrived most recently, and no further, which was, in its own dependable way, more trustworthy than most loyalty Arin had encountered. They met in a bar with no name on the door, in a city Arin had chosen specifically because neither of them belonged to it.

"Charon Corp." The broker said the name like he was testing whether it would bite him for saying it aloud, and didn't relax when it didn't. "I don't know what that company actually is. I've tried to find out twice, for money both times, and both times the trail just — stopped. Not covered up. Stopped, like someone reached in and took the next page out of the book before I could turn to it."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got that's honest." He turned his glass a full rotation on the bar before he spoke again, not drinking from it, just needing something to do with his hands. "What I do know is this: people who ask too many questions about them don't get threatened. They just stop asking questions. All of them. Every single time. I don't know if that's fear or something worse, and I've decided I'd rather not find out which." He looked at Arin directly then, something almost like concern breaking through the professional flatness he usually wore like armor. "Whatever you're chasing, go chase it. Just don't go chasing it because of me. I like breathing too much to be the reason you end up wherever the last people went."

Arin thanked him, paid him, and didn't tell him that the warning had only sharpened something that had already been decided the moment he'd first read the word on a deleted forum post.

It took him four more days of patient tracing, after that, to pin the name to something solid — shell companies, transport manifests, a logistics and private security firm that existed, on paper, with the particular blandness of something built to be forgotten as soon as it was read. Its public materials were sterile enough to be suspicious in their own right: no ambition in them, no personality, the kind of corporate blankness that only ever meant either genuine nothing or something working very hard to look like nothing. Arin had learned to distrust that particular flavor of nothing a long time ago.

---

The headquarters, when he finally stood before it, sat at the center of a place the maps called the Grey City, though no map Arin had found bothered to explain why the name had stuck, and no local he passed on the long walk in from the outskirts seemed to remember the city ever having been called anything else. He understood within an hour of walking its streets why the name had needed no further explanation. This was not a city under siege. Sieges left marks — burned buildings, empty storefronts, the particular tension of people bracing against a threat they could still name and still, in some corner of themselves, still hoped to survive. This city had no tension left in it at all. Its brutalist towers stood beneath a sky that seemed, even at midday, to be perpetually deciding against sunlight, and the people moving beneath them walked with a joyless, mechanical rhythm, eyes forward, shoulders drawn, none of them looking at each other longer than the width of a single distracted glance. He watched a woman drop her groceries on a corner and watched three people step around the spilled fruit without breaking stride, without so much as glancing down, as if the obstacle had always been part of the pavement and any reaction to it would have cost more effort than the moment was worth.

He stood at a crosswalk for the length of three full light cycles, just watching, before he let himself approach the building at the district's center. It cost him nothing, the watching, and told him more than any file the broker could have pulled. This wasn't a city being harvested. Harvesting implied a process still underway, prey still capable of noticing the predator closing in. This city had already been drained of whatever it once had, so long ago and so thoroughly that its own people no longer remembered there had been anything to lose in the first place. The despair here wasn't an event, a single wound the city was still bleeding from. It was ambient — woven so completely into the texture of daily life that no one inside it could any longer see it as anything other than weather. It settled into him with a certainty that felt almost physical, cold water finding the lowest point in a room: this was exactly the kind of camouflage a place like Charon Corp would have chosen on purpose. Not a hunting ground disguised as an ordinary city, but a city that had already stopped being ordinary so long ago that no one left in it would think to ask why, or notice a predator moving quietly through streets that had already given up expecting anything to happen in them at all.

He thought, briefly and without warning, of the veteran in that first bar, weeks ago now — *you ever get the feeling you've done this before* — and wondered whether the city itself carried some version of that same forgetting, an entire population quietly renting space for a grief that no longer had a name attached to it. He didn't let the thought finish. There would be time for it later, if there was a later at all. Right now there was only the tower ahead of him, grey against a greyer sky, and the compass in his chest pulling steadily toward it, patient and certain, the way it always did whenever he was finally close to the thing it had been built to find.

He rolled his shoulders once, settling the old, familiar weight of readiness into himself, and started walking toward the tower at the center of everything, unhurried, patient, the way he approached every hunt that had ever actually mattered.
