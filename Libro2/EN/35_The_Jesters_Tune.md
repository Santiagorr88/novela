# Chapter 35 — The Jester's Tune

Milo Ray had found a new bench since the last time, closer to the old fountain at the park's western edge, though he suspected the bench itself hardly mattered. He'd have felt the same pull sitting on the grass, or standing in the middle of the gravel path, or lying flat on his back watching clouds do whatever clouds did on an afternoon like this one. The city simply reached him wherever he happened to be in it, and he had long since stopped bothering to find the ideal seat for listening. He played instead, unhurried, letting his fingers wander the guitar's neck without any particular destination in mind, and let the day arrange itself around the music rather than the other way around.

He felt Arin first, the way he usually did — not as a sight or a sound but as a note resolving, something sharp and decisive off in the tunnels beneath the campus finally settling into stillness. Milo let a chord ring out to match it, low and quiet, matching the shape of whatever had just finished without knowing what it was. He rarely knew the particulars of these things. He only ever caught the shape they left behind.

Mikel arrived differently, softer, more like a held breath than a resolved note, drawing steadily closer to a place Milo had been quietly watching for weeks now without ever once naming it aloud, even to himself. He let that note hang unresolved too, an open chord he had no intention of closing yet, and kept playing through both of them at once, the two men's separate afternoons folding into the same song.

He thought of the two of them, sometimes, as instruments in an orchestra too large for either of them to hear properly — each convinced he played alone, neither yet able to make out the other section's part from across the hall. Milo could hear both parts. That was, as far as he could tell, most of what made him different from either of them, and he had never once found a tidy way to explain the difference that didn't sound, out loud, considerably stranger than it felt from the inside.

He had tried, once or twice over the years, to explain some version of it to someone who seemed like they might actually listen. It never went especially well. People wanted the explanation to resolve into something they could hold onto afterward, a fact they could repeat to a friend later, and Milo had never once managed to hand them one. He'd learned, eventually, to stop trying. Some things were easier carried quietly than explained badly.

---

The discord arrived a few minutes later, and this one he recognized immediately for what it was — not the ordinary friction of two lives moving toward each other, but something deliberately built into the ground beneath the university's oldest library. He felt it the way he might have felt a held breath gone suddenly tight: patient, low, and entirely intentional. Someone had planted it there to catch a mind before it found what it was looking for, to turn a searcher's certainty into doubt exactly at the moment doubt would cost the most.

He didn't need to see it to understand roughly what it was for. He had felt work like it before, more times than he cared to count, always built for the same narrow purpose — not to stop a determined seeker outright, which took considerably more effort than most of Hell's low functionaries ever cared to spend, but to slow one down, to seed just enough confusion that the seeker doubted a true path when he finally stood on it. Whoever had built this particular trap had done competent work. It was not, Milo noted with the faint, professional appreciation of a craftsman assessing another craftsman's joinery, especially inspired work. But it would do exactly what it was meant to do, and that was often worse than inspiration.

He found himself, not for the first time, faintly irritated on Mikel's behalf, an old and familiar protectiveness he'd never entirely managed to talk himself out of feeling for people who had no idea he was watching them at all. It wasn't fair, precisely, to call it unfair — the man had walked into this searching entirely on his own, following threads no one had forced him to pull. But there was a difference between a hard road and a road someone had deliberately booby-trapped, and Milo had never much cared for pretending the difference didn't matter.

He let his fingers still against the strings, listening more carefully now, searching for whatever single point held the rest of the trap together. He found it soon enough — a single anchoring rune, tucked into the ward's furthest corner, doing the quiet, unglamorous work of keeping the rest of it from coming apart on its own. Everything else about the trap was decoration. That rune was the only part that actually mattered.

---

A squirrel had settled near the base of his bench sometime during all this, drawn by whatever it was that always drew the local wildlife toward him without his ever offering anything they could actually eat. It was the same one, he was fairly sure, that had claimed this particular patch of grass on his last two visits, bold enough now to sit within arm's reach without flinching at his movement. "You again," Milo said to it, not looking away from the middle distance he'd been staring into. "I don't have anything for you today. Just the noise." The squirrel didn't seem especially bothered by the distinction, settling in regardless, tail curled neatly around its own feet, and Milo let it stay, the way he let most uninvited company stay.

The boy appeared a few minutes after that, small and unsupervised in what looked, to Milo, like the particular way of children whose parents had wandered a few benches too far in conversation to notice exactly where their attention had drifted. He wore a backpack far too large for him, one strap slipping off a shoulder he didn't bother fixing, and he stood at a careful, curious distance, watching Milo with an unguarded scrutiny Milo read as belonging to a child too young yet to have learned tact. He glanced twice toward his parents' bench, as though weighing whether the question was worth the risk of wandering this far, before he finally worked up whatever courage it required.

"Why do you talk to yourself?" the boy finally asked, planting his feet with the serious, deliberate stance of someone asking a question he considered extremely important.

Milo didn't stop playing, and didn't look up right away either, letting the boy's question sit a moment inside the melody before he answered it. "I don't talk to myself," he said, at last, with the mild, unbothered patience of a man who'd fielded this exact accusation more times than he could easily count. "I talk to everything that hasn't learned yet not to listen to me."

The boy frowned at that, grave and deliberate in a way Milo found oddly endearing. "That doesn't make sense," he said, though something in the way he said it made it sound more like an invitation than a complaint.

"Most true things don't, the first time you hear them," Milo said, and finally looked up, offering the boy the same easy, unguarded smile he gave most strangers who lingered long enough to earn one. "Give it a few years. It'll make more sense than you'd like it to."

The boy laughed at that, bright and sudden, though nothing in his face suggested he'd actually followed a word of it. He ran back toward his still-distracted parents a moment later, and Milo watched him go, more or less satisfied. He hadn't been trying to be understood. Understanding could come later, on its own time, if it came at all. The listening was the part that mattered today.

---

He turned his attention back to the anchoring rune once the boy had gone, and let his fingers find, without quite deciding to look for it, the single impossible chord the moment actually called for — not a shape any teacher could have taught him, not a fingering that belonged to any scale this particular guitar was built to hold. The chord rang out low and strange across the empty stretch of grass around him, a sound that seemed, for the half-second it lasted, to have more depth to it than six ordinary strings had any business producing.

Beneath the library, a mile off and out of sight, the rune cracked and went dark, its low patient malice guttering out like a snuffed candle. He felt it happen rather than saw it. The ward didn't vanish. It simply stopped being dangerous, a trap that would still be sitting there tomorrow but would no longer catch anything that walked across it. Somewhere close to that library, Milo understood, whatever mind had been about to wander into it would find, instead, only ordinary stone and ordinary confusion, the harmless kind every seeker eventually learns to work through on his own.

---

The chord's last note faded into the evening air, and Milo sat very still afterward, his hand resting flat against the strings to quiet them. It wasn't tiredness exactly, though something in his shoulders had gone loose the way shoulders go loose after real effort. It was closer to a kind of homesickness, the wistful, unplaceable ache of having done a small version of something else, somewhere he couldn't quite name, though he tried, briefly, the way a man tries to remember a face from a dream already half-gone by morning.

He didn't reach for it directly. Reaching for it directly rarely brought back anything worth having, only the vague sense of a shape where a memory should have been. He let it sit at the edge of him instead, unexamined, the way he let most things sit that were more comfortable felt than looked at closely. Whatever it was, it didn't frighten him. It only made him feel, for the length of a breath or two, oddly small in a way the rest of his ordinary afternoons rarely managed.

He couldn't have said, if pressed, why a single cracked rune should have stirred anything in him at all. He let the feeling pass through him the same unhurried way he let most feelings pass through him, without chasing it further than it wanted to be chased, and by the time his hand finally left the strings, it had gone quiet again, tucked back wherever it usually lived when he wasn't paying it any attention.

---

It was on the park's far side, past the fountain and the last of the wandering students, that something caught his eye without his having gone looking for it — a figure moving between the trees, there and then not there, gone almost before Milo's attention had properly settled on him. White hair, unhurried gait, the general shape of a man who looked deep enough in his own thoughts to have stopped noticing his surroundings entirely. He carried something under one arm, a bag or a satchel Milo couldn't make out clearly at this distance, and walked with what looked, from a distance, like the unbothered ease of someone who had somewhere to be but no urgency about getting there. Too far to be certain. Too brief to chase.

Milo came half up off the bench before he'd consciously decided to move, the guitar sliding forgotten off his lap, some old instinct in him straining hard toward the space where the figure had already vanished. He held himself there a long moment, caught between the pull and everything patient in him that had learned, across more years than he let himself count, exactly what it cost to chase a thing before it was ready to be caught. He sat back down slowly, the decision costing him considerably more than his usual unhurried calm let on. Soon, perhaps. Not yet.

He gathered the guitar case without his usual care, latching it in three quick motions rather than the familiar unhurried ritual, and stood a moment longer than he meant to, eyes still fixed on the trees where the white-haired man had disappeared. Arin's resolved note and Mikel's still-open one both sat forgotten now at the very edge of his attention, crowded out entirely by the one pull he hadn't let himself follow. He walked out of the park a different way than he'd come, unwilling, tonight, to pass anywhere near the spot where the fountain had briefly shown him someone he still wasn't ready to meet.
