# Chapter 15 — Infiltration

The mundane security gave him no trouble at all — a keycard system he cloned in the time it took a night guard to finish his coffee, cameras looped on a fifteen-second delay that Arin had bypassed with nothing more sophisticated than patience and a laptop borrowed from a pawnshop two blocks over. He had expected that much. Charon Corp's public face was exactly as bland as its paperwork, a logistics firm too unremarkable to justify anything more than the ordinary precautions any company kept against ordinary thieves, and ordinary thieves were the only kind its architects seemed to have planned for.

The other kind of security took longer, and told him considerably more. He felt the first ward two floors down, a subtle pressure against the compass in his chest that had nothing to do with sound or sight, a held-breath quality to the air itself that no untrained watchman would ever have noticed passing through it. He moved around it rather than through it, the way he'd learned to move around every wrongness he'd tracked across the past month, patient and unhurried, letting the pull behind his sternum guide his feet down a stairwell that shouldn't have existed on any blueprint the company had filed with the city.

Three more wards followed the first, each one subtler than the last, and Arin studied the shape of each before he stepped past it, the way he'd once studied a mark's routine before deciding exactly when to move. The second ward sat woven into a fire door nobody in the building's official paperwork had any record of installing. The third hung invisible across an entire stairwell landing, thin as a held breath, and Arin felt it brush past him and pass on without catching, for reasons he didn't stop to examine. Each ward, subtler than the last, told him more clearly than any confession could have exactly how much this building had to hide and exactly how little its owners expected anyone mortal to come looking for it.

---

The halls below the official basement were not built for people. Arin understood that within the first few steps down the final stairwell: ceilings a fraction too low, doorways a fraction too narrow, every proportion off by some small and deliberate margin he couldn't have named but felt in his shoulders all the same. The cells lined both sides in long, silent rows, each one holding a shape that had once, unmistakably, been a person.

He counted eleven in the first corridor alone before he stopped counting, because counting past a certain number stopped being useful and started being its own kind of cruelty. They sat or lay in postures too still to be sleep, their eyes open and unseeing, their chests rising and falling in the shallow, mechanical rhythm of bodies that had been left running long after whoever operated them had stopped paying attention to why. Thin threads of pale light rose from each of them, drawn steadily upward into a lattice of arcane machinery bolted into the ceiling above — one more thing, among many, that he had learned to see over the past month without ever once being taught how.

He moved along the row slowly, cataloguing details out of old professional habit even as the rest of him recoiled from every one of them. A man in a torn business suit, his tie still knotted with the particular care of someone who had dressed for an ordinary morning and never made it home. A girl who couldn't have been more than sixteen, a school bag still looped over one slack shoulder. None of them reacted to his passing. None of them had reacted, he understood, to anything at all in longer than any clock down here still bothered to track. He had seen death in every ugly shape his old work had ever shown him. He had never seen anything harvested quite this patiently, this efficiently, this much like a factory floor humming along on schedule.

---

He was three cells past the last one he'd meant to look at when the whisper reached him, so faint he nearly mistook it for the machinery's own hum. "How long." The voice belonged to a woman in the fourth cell, her eyes tracking him now with an effort that seemed to cost her everything she had left to spend. "I don't know how long I've been here. I don't know if anyone even knows I'm still — " She didn't finish it. She didn't need to.

Arin stopped. He knew, with the flat professional certainty of a man who had built an entire career on discipline, that stopping was the single worst decision available to him in this hallway — that every second spent standing still here was a second closer to discovery, that his whole plan depended on moving through this building like something that had never been present in it at all. He crouched by the bars anyway, close enough that his voice wouldn't carry past the two of them.

"I don't know either," he said. "But I know now. That's more than you had a minute ago." He held her gaze, something tightening in his chest that had nothing to do with the pull he'd followed here at all, something closer to plain, simple anger with somewhere to land at last. "I'm going to stop this. I don't know yet how. But I promise you that."

She didn't answer. He wasn't certain she had the strength left to. But something in her eyes changed, faint and small and entirely real, and Arin filed the shape of that change away somewhere he intended to carry for the rest of whatever this hunt turned out to cost him.

---

The harvest itself happened four cells further down, and Arin arrived in time to see the whole of it — a demon in a technician's coat directing the process with a flat, bored efficiency that read, to Arin, like a man who had done this so many times it had stopped registering as anything worth his attention. The captive strapped into the extraction frame was younger than the woman had been, barely more than a boy, and Arin watched the pale thread of his essence pulled loose in one long, unbroken draw, watched it crystallize mid-air into something small and bright and utterly obscene, watched the technician pocket it with the same casual motion a man might use to pocket loose change. The boy's body went slack in the frame the instant the draw finished, not dead, Arin understood with a fresh and specific horror, but simply emptied — hollowed out and left running on whatever scraps remained, exactly like the woman four cells back, exactly like every other silent shape lining this endless hallway.

He forced himself to stay long enough to learn the process rather than simply flee the sight of it — the restraints, the rhythm of the draw, the exact moment the technician's attention wandered to a handheld device instead of the boy still slack in the harness. Knowledge like that had a use later, if there was a later, and he filed it away with the same cold discipline he'd once reserved for a mark's schedule, disliking, distantly, how easily the old habit still fit him.

He had killed men for less than what he'd just watched. He had killed men, if he was honest with the version of himself he'd stopped hiding from a month ago, for considerably less conviction than the cold, absolute certainty settling into him now.

---

The alarm found him two corridors later, a low klaxon rising from speakers he hadn't noticed were part of the ceiling's architecture at all, and Arin understood, in the half-second before the first guards rounded the corner ahead of him, that some ward further back had finally caught what his patience had let him slip past three others. He didn't run. Running would have wasted the only advantage he had left, which was knowing this building's layout better, in the last twenty minutes, than the men now hunting through it did after years of working here.

The demon captain who came for him personally moved differently than the guards flanking him — unhurried, almost amused, a confidence Arin read as belonging to something that had never once needed to rush toward a kill in its own territory. Something about it sat wrong to Arin's eye in a way he couldn't quite pin down, a wrongness in the set of its shoulders under the well-tailored suit that he filed away without stopping to name. "A ghost," it said, its voice carrying the dry, professional interest of a man appraising an unexpected delivery. "We don't get many of those down here. Most of what wanders in gets caught by something a great deal less subtle than wards." It studied him a moment longer, head tilting with an interest that had nothing kind in it. "You've seen more than you were meant to. That tends to be fatal, in my experience."

"I've been told that before," Arin said, "by men considerably better at delivering on it than you look."

The captain's amusement didn't waver, but something behind its eyes sharpened, recalculating him. "Confidence, from something that's about to be walked out of here in a jar." It raised one hand, unhurried — a signal, not yet given, that would close the last open stretch of corridor behind Arin the moment it fell.

Arin saw the gap before it closed. He saw, in the same glance, the two cells nearest him, close enough to reach in the handful of seconds that gap would stay open. Taking the corridor meant walking out of this building tonight with everything he'd learned still intact. Reaching the cells instead meant giving that corridor up for good, trading the one clean path left to him for two people who couldn't yet stand on their own.

"Then it's a good thing," Arin said, already moving, "that I've stopped caring much about what tends to happen."

---

He didn't wait for the captain's hand to fall. He crossed to the two cells nearest him instead, tearing the crude locks free with a strength that had nothing elegant in it and didn't need to, and felt the gap behind him close for good in the same instant the second lock gave way. He hadn't freed these two because it was tactically sound. He'd freed them because a woman in the fourth cell of the first corridor had asked him how long, and he had promised her an answer he still didn't fully have, and some promises, he was learning tonight, cost exactly as much as they were worth and not one coin less.

"Move," he told the two captives, already turning to meet the guards closing from both directions. "Whatever happens next, just move."

The younger of the two, a man barely steady enough to stand on his own legs, hesitated at the cell door as if he'd forgotten, in however long he'd spent hollowed out in that frame, what a door was even for. Arin caught his arm and steered him bodily into the corridor behind him, buying him half a step of distance he didn't have to spare. "I know," he said, low and fast, not looking away from the guards. "I know. Walk anyway."

He did not know, in the seconds before the first guard reached him, whether he would walk out of this building tonight at all. He knew only that the man who had slipped into this place a careful hour ago, tracking one more absence from a safe distance, was not the man standing in this corridor now, and whatever came through that door next was going to have to deal with the second one instead.
