# Chapter 6 — Whispers of the Summit

The mountain had no name that any mortal tongue had ever settled on for long, and no mortal foot had ever completed the climb to find out whether it deserved one. The air at this height thinned past the point where lungs built for lower places could do useful work with it; the cold did not so much bite as erase, patiently, whatever it touched long enough. Nothing grew here. Nothing nested here. The peak held itself apart from the ordinary business of the world below it — not empty, exactly, but reserved, the way a single held breath is reserved from the noise of ordinary speech.

The figure who sat at its summit had not moved, in any sense a moving thing would recognize as movement, in longer than anyone still living could have measured. Wind came and went below him in seasons he no longer counted, each one arriving indistinguishable from the last by the time it reached this altitude, scoured of whatever character it might have carried lower down. Snow gathered on his shoulders and slid away again and gathered once more, in a rhythm as patient as breathing. His robes, stitched from some fabric that caught the thin light strangely, showed no wear that the years should have left on them. He did not need shelter. He simply did not move, and had not, for longer than the question of why would have meant anything to ask.

He did not see, not in the way sight is usually meant. What reached him instead was a kind of weave — the world laid out beneath him not as shapes and distances but as threads of light and shadow, cause tugging gently at consequence somewhere miles and years away, the whole vast fabric of things trembling faintly, always, with its own slow work of becoming. Some threads burned bright and briefly and were gone, a single mortal life measured start to finish in the time it took him to register the pattern of it. Others ran long and low beneath everything else, currents so old and so wide he had long since stopped being able to tell where any one of them had begun. Watching the weave no longer felt like an act to him. It felt, simply, like the shape his stillness had learned to take.

Two threads, tonight, pulled at him differently than the rest.

They came from far apart — one from a city gone quiet with concrete and forgetting, one from a room lined with old books and the particular hush of a mind that has learned to distrust its own memories — and they should have meant nothing more to him than any other two threads in a weave that held uncountable millions. And yet each one arrived carrying a note he recognized the way a struck bell recognizes the specific pitch of another bell struck somewhere far across a valley: not a sound he was hearing for the first time, but one his own stillness answered before he had decided to let it.

One thread was muted, sorrowful, a light held prisoner behind a cage of doubt it had built for itself out of very human material — grief it didn't understand, dreams it couldn't place, a chest that ached around an absence it had no name for. The other was sharper, a single piercing note whetted fine by something that called itself justice and might, in time, need to learn the difference between justice and the hunger that so often wears its clothes. He held both notes a long while without moving, without reaching toward either, only listening — recognizing something in each of them that he could not have named if asked, and did not try to.

He did not let himself finish the thought that wanted, quietly, to follow. Some doors, once opened even in the privacy of his own stillness, did not close cleanly again.

A third thread threaded itself into the weave then, and this one was not familiar at all — a greasy, hungry note, small and mean and entirely without the patience the two brighter threads carried without knowing they carried it. It moved the way vermin move, low and quick and certain of its own cleverness, leaving behind it a residue that clung faintly to every thread it brushed against, a kind of smallness that spread wherever it passed. He followed it back along its own tangled path and found, at its source, one of Hell's lesser scouts, clumsy in every way that mattered to the weave even as it moved with real stealth through the mortal world it was hunting in — competent enough to go unnoticed by the mortals whose streets it crossed, and entirely unaware that anything above them was watching it at all. It had found the muted thread. It was closing on it now, slow and greedy, certain of an easy prize, already tasting a victory it had not yet earned and would, if left uninterrupted, claim within the hour.

He weighed the cost before he weighed anything else, the way he had learned, over an age long enough to make the lesson permanent, that every stitch he pulled in another thread's favor unraveled some small length of his own. It would have been easy, once, not to notice the cost at all. It had never once, in all that time, become easy to ignore it on purpose.

He measured the scout against that cost and found the scale, for a long moment, genuinely uncertain — such a small thing, to spend himself on. Such a clumsy, replaceable little hunger, one of thousands Hell would not miss and would not remember losing, its extinguishing or its success equally beneath the notice of anything that mattered on the scale he usually measured things by. He had let smaller injustices than this one pass unremarked for centuries at a stretch, telling himself, each time, that stillness preserved for the moments that truly required it was not the same failure as stillness spent on cowardice. Most of those times, he still believed that had been true.

He could feel himself deciding not to intervene taking shape, reasonable and easy and entirely within his right to choose, argued out in the same patient, exhausted logic he had used a thousand times before to justify exactly this kind of restraint. And then, for reasons he did not examine too closely even in the privacy of his own unbroken quiet, he set that shape aside. Not because the two brighter threads demanded it of him — they did not know he existed, would not have asked him for anything even if they had, would likely have recoiled from him as readily as from the thing hunting them, had either of them been given the chance to see him clearly. He set it aside because the choice to look away, when looking away cost him nothing and looking on cost him everything, was itself a shape he had sworn, somewhere so far back he could no longer date the vow, never to let himself wear comfortably again. He had broken that vow before. He tried, each time it came due again, not to break it twice in a row.

He shaped a single word instead — not spoken, not in any tongue with a mouth built to hold it, but let loose into the wind the way a held breath is finally let go. It traveled the whole shivering distance between the summit and the muted thread's mortal city faster than wind should have been able to carry anything, and where it arrived, it did nothing so crude as strike. It simply settled, the way frost settles, over a hunger that had never once in its clumsy existence felt anything like contentment — and the scout, mid-stalk, lost the thread of its own purpose entirely, turned aside as if remembering, quite suddenly, some other errand infinitely more pressing, and did not come back.

The cost arrived a half-breath later, the way it always did — not pain, precisely, but its architecture: a fine new crack tracing itself through the stone beneath him, no wider than a hair, and audible, if anything at this altitude could still be called audible, only as the faintest whisper of settling rock, a sound so small that even the thin wind nearly carried it away before it finished. He had stopped counting the cracks some centuries ago, once the counting itself had started to feel like a second, quieter kind of vanity. He had never stopped feeling each new one arrive. That much, at least, he had not allowed himself the mercy of losing.

He let his attention settle back over the weave once more, out of habit more than need, and found the two brighter threads unchanged by any of it — still burning their separate, muted, unremarkable burns, still entirely unaware that anything watching them existed at all, let alone that it had just spent a fragment of itself to keep one of them safe a little longer from a danger neither of them would ever know had come close. That, too, was part of the shape of the vow. Not to be thanked. Not even, most of the time, to be noticed. Only to have acted, and to carry what the acting cost, and to let the two of them go on believing, if they believed anything about the night at all, that the world had simply been kind to them by chance.

The crack in the stone did not close. It would not close. It would simply remain, one more line in a map only he could read, a quiet accounting of exactly what it cost to see clearly and choose, each time, to act anyway rather than let the seeing be its own excuse for stillness.

The wind quieted further, and then further still, until even the memory of sound seemed to withdraw from the summit altogether. He settled back into his stillness, and the mountain settled with him, and for what would seem to the world below like the length of several patient weeks, neither the figure nor the peak that bore him moved again — though far beneath him, in a city he had already half-forgotten the name of and a room lined with old and doubting books, two threads went on quietly burning, closer now to each other than either of them yet had any way of knowing.
