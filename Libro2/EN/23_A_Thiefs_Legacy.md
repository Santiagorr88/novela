# Chapter 23 — A Thief's Legacy

The trail Sariel followed deeper into the Tower had nothing to do with sight or sound. It was ambition he tracked now — the particular psychic residue a mind as hungry as Belial's left behind wherever it lingered too long over a text, a mark on the archive's silence distinct from the ambient screaming. He had learned to read that kind of residue over centuries of hunting things that didn't want to be found, and Belial's had settled into this place deeply enough that months later it was still plain to a mind trained to look for it.

The trail ended at a hidden study three levels below the one where the vision had found him, a chamber concealed behind a shelf that swung inward at a touch, its hinge worn smooth by use its builders had never intended anyone else to discover. Sariel stepped through into a room untouched by the archive's chaos, shelves arranged with the obsessive order of a mind that wanted nothing left to chance.

The screaming hush that filled the rest of the Tower faded almost entirely once the shelf sealed shut behind him. What replaced it was a silence too deliberate to be accidental, evidence that someone had gone to considerable trouble to keep it that way. He moved through it carefully, still favoring the side where the vision's aftermath had left a dull ache behind his ribs, cataloguing the room's contents with the same patient thoroughness he brought to any space he needed to search without leaving evidence he'd searched it.

---

The fragments of the Codex Mortalis sat displayed across a reading table exactly where Belial had left them, undisturbed since the night he'd last studied them here. Sariel approached slowly, reading what remained of the ancient text with the hungry attention of a man who understood, better than most, how much a single surviving fragment could cost to obtain. The pages spoke only in fragments and riddles of weapons neither Heaven nor Hell had ever fully claimed as their own creation — nothing so plain as an origin, only the unsettling suggestion that one existed somewhere beneath the text's evasions.

Belial's annotations crowded the margins in a hand grown increasingly urgent as the pages progressed, question after question scrawled beside passages the original text answered only in riddles. Sariel had expected arrogance. He found instead desperation — a mind working frantically to understand a power it had already decided to seize, regardless of whether understanding arrived in time to matter.

He traced the progression across a dozen fragments, watching the handwriting change as the study wore on — confident at first, almost dismissive in its brevity, then gradually cramped and urgent, entire pages given over to the same question rephrased a dozen ways. Belial had not found what he sought cleanly. He had found enough to act on, and had acted, and Sariel understood, reading the last and most frantic of the annotations, that acting without fully understanding was likely the reason Belial now carried a permanent, unwanted cost he had never anticipated when he first set out to claim his prize.

---

It was the different hand that caught his attention — older, more cautious, its ink faded to a shade Belial's fresher annotations hadn't yet begun to match. It sat crowded into a corner Belial's notes had left untouched, as though whoever wrote it had known enough to leave a warning where a careless reader might miss it, and a careful one wouldn't.

*Whoever claims this without being its rightful owner will find no glory. They will find a debt.*

Sariel copied the line into memory, precise to the letter, and became aware, in the same instant, that he was no longer alone in the chamber.

"You've found the old margin note." The voice belonged to a minor archivist, elderly even by the standards of a realm that measured age differently than mortals did, his robes marking him as one of the Tower's permanent staff rather than a visitor passing through. He'd entered without Sariel noticing, and stood now examining the reading table with the mild, proprietary interest of a man checking on furniture, not one guarding forbidden knowledge. "Most who come through here miss it entirely. Your lord's attention rarely wandered that far from the text itself."

Sariel kept his borrowed indifference in place. "Who else has consulted this collection, before my lord?"

"Before Lord Belial?" The archivist gave a short, dry laugh, unguarded — the laugh of a man too bored with his own work to suspect the question carried any real weight behind it. "No one, in centuries. This isn't the kind of material anyone reads twice for pleasure. It gets read twice because the first reading never actually made sense."

"And the older hand," Sariel asked, letting the question trail after the first as though it barely mattered. "Do the Tower's records say whose it was?"

"No name attached to it that I've ever found, and I've looked, if only out of my own idle curiosity over the years." The archivist considered the question with more genuine interest than he'd shown the rest of the exchange. "Older than the collection itself, if the ink's any judge. Whoever wrote it wasn't cataloguing anything. They were warning off whoever came after them, and didn't much care whether anyone ever read it." He shrugged, already losing interest again, and drifted back toward the doorway without waiting for Sariel to respond further. "Take your time. No one's come looking for this dust in longer than either of us has probably been alive."

Sariel said nothing more, and let the archivist go, filing the answer away as carefully as he'd filed the margin note itself.

---

The records of the heist sat further back in the study, in a locked drawer Belial's annotations had indicated the location of without bothering to conceal it — arrogance winning out over caution, even here. Sariel read through them with the same flat, professional attention he'd give any hunt's final piece of evidence, and felt something cold settle into his chest as the full shape of it assembled in front of him.

The plan had been built in careful stages, each recorded with the dispassionate precision of a man documenting a campaign, not confessing to a theft — a false treaty to buy passage past the Sepulcher's outer wards, a manufactured rivalry to draw its lesser guardians from their posts at the exact hour Belial needed them absent, and finally the sacrifice, timed to the precise moment the deception could no longer hold on its own. Nothing in the notes read like improvisation. Every step had been mapped in advance, contingencies included, the whole operation assembled with the same meticulous care Sariel recognized from centuries of his own hunts.

Belial had not found Lamentum through diligence or divine favor. He had orchestrated its theft, deliberately and at real cost — the sacrifice of Aamon, Marshal of the Infernal Legions, offered up to the guardian that had once protected the Weeping Sepulcher's approach. The notes named the Sepulcher's location, along with the exact sequence of deceptions Belial used to breach its final ward — none of it recorded with pride in craftsmanship, only the flat, transactional satisfaction of a thief cataloguing a job well executed.

Sariel read the account of Aamon's sacrifice twice, the second time more slowly than the first. Belial's notes gave a commander of Aamon's standing exactly three lines — no mention of the legions he'd marshaled, no acknowledgment of the rank he'd spent centuries building, only the bare fact of the sacrifice and a brief, clinical note on how effectively it had opened the Sepulcher's final approach. Sariel had killed a great many things in his long service, and had rarely troubled himself over what any of them had once commanded before he ended them. He found, reading Belial's cold three lines about a marshal reduced to a single tactical expenditure, that this particular death bothered him more than most of his own had.

---

Sariel sat back from the drawer's contents and let the larger truth settle into place. He had come here half expecting to find evidence of some terrible, singular genius — a mastermind who had earned his prize through understanding no one else possessed. He found instead a thief, patient and ruthless but fundamentally ordinary in his methods, who had been willing to spend what others weren't to take something that had never belonged to him.

That understanding reframed everything Gabriel had sent him here to learn. The weapons were not artifacts of either side's making, prizes any general might have legitimately claimed through conquest or divine right. They belonged to neither Heaven nor Hell. Belial had not won Lamentum. He had stolen it, the way any common thief steals a prize too valuable to walk away from honestly, and the war both realms had spent centuries fighting over its use had never involved a party who actually owned what was being fought over.

He thought of Gabriel's suspicion in the cold observatory, the unfinished shape of it — that Malthus's killer predated even Hell's knowledge of itself, that a trail waited somewhere in this Tower. Gabriel had guessed at the size of the mystery without yet guessing at its nature. Sariel now carried both, and understood, sitting alone in a dead man's study three levels beneath Hell's oldest archive, that some truths cost considerably more to carry than they had seemed to cost simply to seek.

---

Sariel took nothing else from the study — no fragment pocketed, no page torn free, nothing that might mark him as having been here should the study be searched again. But before he stepped back through the concealed shelf, he paused a final moment over the spot where the unsigned margin note had waited, faded and easy to overlook, for however many silent centuries had passed since someone first thought to write it.

Gabriel had not asked him to remember a warning meant for thieves. Nothing in his instructions had mentioned it. But some old instinct, the same patient certainty that had made him good at tracking souls too well hidden for anyone else to find, told him the warning would matter again before this was finished — that a debt promised to whoever claimed something without rightful ownership was not a detail a hunter of his experience could afford to trust to memory alone. He drew a small scrap of blank parchment from the lining of his forged papers, where it had been folded flat and unnoticed since he'd crossed into Hell, and copied the line onto it letter for letter, before folding it away again somewhere no records clerk would think to look.

It was, he understood, the first decision he had made on this mission that Gabriel had not specifically authorized. He found he did not regret it. A hunter who only followed instructions to the letter caught nothing that hadn't already been named for him, and Sariel had built a long, quiet career on being considerably better than that.

Then he stepped back through the shelf, sealed it behind him with the same patience he'd used to open it, and turned his attention toward the long, dangerous walk back out of Hell's oldest and hungriest archive.
