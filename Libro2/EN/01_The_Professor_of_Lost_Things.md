# Chapter 1 — The Professor of Lost Things

The lecture hall at Navarion smelled of old paper and radiator dust, and on mornings like this one — grey light pressing flat against the high windows, autumn already thinking about winter — it felt smaller than its four hundred seats deserved to feel. Mikel Ardon stood at the lectern with his notes untouched in front of him and did not need them. He was an unremarkable man to look at, and had built a small, unconscious craft out of staying that way — middle-aged, hair gone white a few decades early, dressed in the kind of modest wool a department stopped noticing after the first year, thin leather gloves worn in every season regardless of weather, for reasons none of his colleagues had ever thought worth asking about. His voice matched the rest of him: low, a quiet weariness settling into every pause, pitched too quietly to reach the back rows without the room going still first. Ninety students a term looked straight through him without once registering they'd looked at anything at all, and some old, careful part of him made sure it stayed that way. He never needed them, not for this material. The words came the way they always did for the wars nobody else in the department wanted to teach — the doomed garrisons, the last stands, the sieges that ended not in relief but in silence — arriving in his mouth already shaped, as if he were reciting from memory, not reasoning it out from sources. He had never examined that fact too closely. It was easier, most days, not to.

"The garrison at Ashkelet Ridge held for eleven days," he said, "against odds no serious historian believes should have been survivable. Not for eleven hours. Eleven days." He let the number sit in the room, watching it land or fail to land across ninety-some faces arranged in their usual Tuesday-morning postures — chins on fists, laptops open to windows that weren't his slides, the particular glazed patience of second period. A few had actually stopped typing. Those were the ones he spoke to now, without meaning to, the way a man will unconsciously address the two or three people in a crowd who are actually listening. "They had no supply line. They had no relief column coming. What little food remained, they gave to the wounded first, as a matter of course, without an order needing to be given. What they had was a commander who refused, for eleven days, to accept that the position was already lost."

He heard his own voice shift the way it always did on this kind of material — drop half a register, slow down, as if he were setting each word down carefully rather than saying it. He gripped the edge of the lectern until his knuckles paled, because for one disorienting second the ridge in his mind wasn't Ashkelet at all. It was somewhere with no name he could summon, under a sky the wrong color — not blue, not grey, but the color of a bruise deciding whether to heal — and the weight settled into his own right hand wasn't a marker pen. It was heavier than that, and warmer, and it *wanted* something from him that a marker pen never had.

He blinked. The lecture hall came back into focus a half-second late, the way a room does after you've stood up too fast — dust motes hanging gold in a shaft of window light that, for the length of that same half-second, had seemed to carry a spark of impossible blue, there and gone before he could be sure he'd seen it. A trick of tired eyes. He'd been sleeping badly. He said as much to himself, silently, in the same reasonable tone he used to explain the reading to a room of undergraduates, and did not entirely believe it.

"Read chapters four and five for Thursday." He let them go twelve minutes before the hour was properly up — which he never did, not in nine years of teaching this course — and told himself, gathering his own notes with hands that were not quite steady, that it was because he'd covered the material efficiently today. Not because he needed the room emptied before anyone thought to look too closely at him.

---

He was gathering his notes when he realized one seat was still occupied. A student — one of the quieter ones, a girl who took meticulous notes in a small precise hand and never spoke up in section — hovered at the base of the steps with her bag already on her shoulder, the posture of someone who has talked herself into approaching and then out of it, twice already, and is now simply out of better options.

"Professor Ardon." She said it fast, like ripping off a bandage before she could reconsider. "Can I ask you something that isn't about the reading?"

"You can ask." He kept his voice easy, the voice he used for students working up to a confession that embarrassed them — a grade, usually, or a family emergency eating into a deadline.

"Do you ever dream about wars you didn't live through?" She flushed immediately, a blotchy color climbing her throat, as if hearing the question out loud had confirmed how strange it sounded. "Not like — not history-brain, not 'I read about it and now I'm thinking about it before bed.' Like you were *there*. Like it's a memory and not a dream at all, except it can't be, because none of it ever happened to me. I've been having them for weeks now, and I thought, you study this for a living, maybe it's normal, maybe it happens to everyone who spends enough time in it, and I didn't know who else to ask that wouldn't think I was—" She stopped herself before the sentence could finish.

Mikel felt his chest go very still, the stillness of a man standing at the edge of a step he can't see the bottom of. "It's your mind organizing what it's absorbed." He heard the answer come out smoothly, generically, exactly the shape he'd have handed any student asking any strange question in the three minutes before he needed to be somewhere else. "History has a way of feeling personal once you've spent enough hours living inside it on the page. It doesn't mean anything more than that."

She nodded, visibly relieved, her shoulders dropping half an inch, and thanked him, and gathered the rest of her things, and left him alone with the echo of her own question still standing in the room after she'd gone.

He lingered in the emptying hall longer than the moment required. It wasn't what she'd said that stayed with him, not exactly. It was that he had never once, in his entire adult life, told another living person that the honest answer to her question would have been *yes* — not "it happens," not "it's normal" — *yes, every night, and it doesn't feel like a dream to me either.*

---

The quad he crossed to reach his office was older than the country around it knew how to date properly, its paving stones worn concave by a few hundred more years of footsteps than the university's official history cared to advertise, and Mikel had walked it often enough to stop noticing the particular hush that settled over it once the students thinned out — the sense, common to old ground everywhere, that the quiet here had been earned by something the buildings themselves no longer remembered. The ache came for him crossing it, the way it always did — no warning, no buildup, just a sudden fist closing somewhere behind his sternum that had nothing to do with muscle or bone or anything a cardiologist had ever found a name for. He stopped mid-stride, one hand flat against the cold stone of the arts building, and breathed through it in the careful, counted way he'd taught himself years ago, in through four, hold for four, out for four, until the moment passed and left him faintly nauseated and alone with the sensation that had never, in all the years he'd carried it, gotten any easier to describe: that something enormous had once occupied that exact space in his chest, filled it completely, been the whole shape of him — and was gone now. Not pain, exactly. A vacancy with edges.

Three different doctors, across three different years, had found nothing. Scans that came back clean. Blood work that came back clean. The third had used the word *idiopathic*, which Mikel had gone home and looked up, and which had turned out to mean, essentially, *we don't know, and neither will you.* He'd stopped mentioning it to the fourth doctor before that appointment even started, because there was only so many times a man could watch a stranger's face rearrange itself into practiced, professional uncertainty before the telling became its own kind of exhausting.

He walked the rest of the way to his office more carefully than the pain strictly warranted, one hand still faintly pressed to his own chest through his coat.

---

That night the dream came again, the way it always did when the ache had been bad during the day. He stood on a battlefield that existed nowhere on any map he'd ever taught from, under a sky the color of a healing bruise. Golden-armored soldiers fell around him in every direction, and each loss felt like a debt only he was left holding. The air tasted of ozone and of something beneath it he never had a waking word for. His own hand held a weight that hummed — not with sound, but with a certainty that felt, disorientingly, more like his real hand than the one he woke up with every single morning of his adult life.

Then a shadow crossed the sky where no shadow should have reached, wrong enough that every golden soldier around him flinched at once, and a black, ancient thing drove into him with a sound like a verdict read aloud. The humming weight was torn from his grip mid-motion, and the loss registered not as disarming but as amputation.

He woke with a scream caught silent behind his clenched teeth and his hand already closing — fast, practiced, decades of practice compressed into a single sleeping reflex — on nothing at all, on empty air above the blanket, on the exact remembered shape of a weight that had never, in this life, been there. Beneath the sheet, where the glove wasn't there to hide it, the old scar across his right palm gave one faint pulse of heat with no source he had ever managed to trace, and was gone again before he was fully certain he hadn't imagined it.

---

His hand found the fragment on the nightstand instead — it always did, some nights, without his permission — a piece of blackened, unidentifiable metal he'd bought at a flea market years ago for reasons he'd never been able to articulate even to himself, not to the vendor haggling him up two units of currency he could barely spare that month, not to the friend who'd asked, half-joking, if he was starting some kind of collection. It was cool under his palm at first, then warmed slowly to his skin. One broken edge was jagged enough that it should have hurt to hold and never quite did. In bad light, the lines etched across its face seemed to shift when he wasn't looking at them directly, and went still again the instant his eyes returned. He didn't know what it had once been part of. He'd stopped letting himself wonder, most nights. He knew only that closing his hand around it, on nights like this, made the rest of the dream recede a little faster than it otherwise would.

He was still holding it, turning it slowly against his palm, when his phone lit up and buzzed against the nightstand — a colleague from the department, one of the few who still bothered to call instead of text, as if the effort of it were its own small act of care.

Mikel picked up on the second ring. The voice on the other end skipped hello entirely, the way it always did. "You're up. Good. Come get a drink with me tomorrow. I'm not asking you to plan it — I'm asking you to show up."

"Can't." Mikel was already reaching for the excuse that came easiest. "I've got marking to catch up on."

A pause, just long enough to make clear it wasn't believed. "You've been saying that for a month, Ardon. You sleep like garbage — don't tell me you don't, I've seen you in the corridor at eight in the morning looking like you lost a fight with the night. You cancel everything. And you've been walking around all month like a man three weeks behind on something nobody assigned him." A breath, softer. "I'm not going to make you talk about it. I just want you to know I'm here, if you ever decide you want to."

Mikel sat with the phone against his ear a moment longer than the silence strictly needed. He thought, with a clarity that startled him, about the girl in the lecture hall and the honest answer he hadn't given her, and about how easy it would be, right now, to say three real sentences instead of one more excuse — to be, for the first time in longer than he could measure, not alone with any of it.

"Thanks," he said instead. "I mean that. Just — not tonight."

"Next week, then. I'm holding you to it."

He said something that might have been agreement, and hung up, and set the phone face-down on the nightstand, and turned off the light, and lay in the dark a while longer with the cold fragment still closed in his fist, listening to his own heartbeat and to nothing else the night was willing to tell him.
