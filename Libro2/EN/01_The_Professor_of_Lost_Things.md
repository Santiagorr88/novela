# Chapter 1 — The Professor of Lost Things

The lecture hall at Navarion smelled of old paper and radiator dust, and on mornings like this one — grey light pressing flat against the high windows, autumn already thinking about winter — it felt smaller than its four hundred seats deserved to feel. Mikel Ardon stood at the lectern with his notes untouched in front of him and did not need them. He never needed them, not for this material. The words came the way they always did for the wars nobody else in the department wanted to teach — the doomed garrisons, the last stands, the sieges that ended not in relief but in silence — arriving in his mouth already shaped, as if he were reciting something memorized rather than reasoned out from sources. He had never examined that fact too closely. It was easier, most days, not to.

"The garrison at Ashkelet Ridge held for eleven days," he said, "against odds no serious historian believes should have been survivable. Not for eleven hours. Eleven days." He let the number sit in the room, watching it land or fail to land across ninety-some faces arranged in their usual Tuesday-morning postures — chins on fists, laptops open to windows that weren't his slides, the particular glazed patience of second period. A few had actually stopped typing. Those were the ones he spoke to now, without meaning to, the way a man will unconsciously address the two or three people in a crowd who are actually listening. "They had no supply line. They had no relief column coming. What little food remained, they gave to the wounded first, as a matter of course, without an order needing to be given. What they had was a commander who refused, for eleven days, to accept that the position was already lost."

He heard his own voice do something it always did on this kind of material — drop half a register, slow down, as if the sentence itself weighed more than the others around it, as if he were setting each word down carefully rather than saying it. He gripped the edge of the lectern until his knuckles paled, because for one disorienting second the ridge in his mind wasn't Ashkelet at all. It was somewhere with no name he could summon, under a sky the wrong color — not blue, not grey, something closer to a bruise deciding whether to heal — and the weight settled into his own right hand wasn't a marker pen. It was heavier than that, and warmer, and it *wanted* something from him that a marker pen never had.

He blinked. The lecture hall came back into focus a half-second late, the way a room does after you've stood up too fast — dust motes hanging gold in a shaft of window light that, for the length of that same half-second, had seemed to carry something like a spark of impossible blue, there and gone before his eyes could commit to having seen it. A trick of tired eyes. He'd been sleeping badly. He said as much to himself, silently, in the same reasonable tone he used to explain the reading to a room of undergraduates, and did not entirely believe it any more than half of them believed the reading mattered.

"Read chapters four and five for Thursday," he said, twelve minutes before the hour was properly up, and let them go — which he never did, not in nine years of teaching this course — and told himself, gathering his own notes with hands that were not quite steady, that it was because he'd covered the material efficiently today. Not because he needed the room emptied before anyone thought to look too closely at him.

---

He was gathering his notes when he realized one seat was still occupied. A student — one of the quieter ones, a girl who took meticulous notes in a small precise hand and never once spoke up in section — hovered at the base of the steps with her bag already on her shoulder, the posture of someone who has talked herself into approaching and then out of it, twice already, and is now simply out of better options.

"Professor Ardon." She said it fast, like ripping off a bandage before she could reconsider. "Can I ask you something that isn't about the reading?"

"You can ask." He kept his voice easy, unhurried, the voice he used for students working up to something they were embarrassed by — a grade, usually, or a family emergency eating into a deadline.

"Do you ever dream about wars you didn't live through?" She flushed immediately, a blotchy color climbing her throat, as if hearing the question out loud had confirmed exactly how strange it sounded. "Not like — not history-brain, not 'I read about it and now I'm thinking about it before bed.' Like you were *there*. Like it's a memory and not a dream at all, except it can't be, because none of it ever happened to me. I've been having them for weeks now, and I thought, you study this for a living, maybe it's normal, maybe it happens to everyone who spends enough time in it, and I didn't know who else to ask that wouldn't think I was—" She stopped herself before the sentence could finish.

Mikel felt something in his chest go very still, the particular stillness of a man standing at the edge of a step he can't see the bottom of. "It's your mind organizing what it's absorbed," he said, and heard the answer come out smoothly, generically, exactly the shape he'd have handed any student asking any strange question in the three minutes before he needed to be somewhere else. "History has a way of feeling personal once you've spent enough hours living inside it on the page. It doesn't mean anything more than that."

She nodded, visibly relieved, her shoulders dropping half an inch, and thanked him, and gathered the rest of her things, and left him alone with the echo of her own question still standing in the room after she'd gone.

He stood in the emptying hall a moment longer than the moment required. It wasn't what she'd said that stayed with him, not exactly. It was that he had never once, in his entire adult life, told another living person that the honest answer to her question would have been *yes* — not "it happens," not "it's normal" — *yes, every night, and it doesn't feel like a dream to me either.*

---

The ache came for him crossing the quad, the way it always did — no warning, no buildup, just a sudden fist closing somewhere behind his sternum that had nothing to do with muscle or bone or anything a cardiologist had ever found a name for. He stopped mid-stride, one hand flat against the cold stone of the arts building, and breathed through it in the careful, counted way he'd taught himself years ago, in through four, hold for four, out for four, until the moment passed and left him faintly nauseated and entirely alone with the sensation that had never once, in all the years he'd carried it, gotten any easier to describe: that something enormous had once occupied that exact space in his chest, filled it completely, been the whole shape of him — and was gone now, leaving nothing behind but the precise, hollow architecture of its absence. Not pain, exactly. A vacancy with edges.

Three different doctors, across three different years, had found nothing. Scans that came back clean. Blood work that came back clean. The third had used the word *idiopathic*, which Mikel had gone home and looked up, and which had turned out to mean, essentially, *we don't know, and neither will you.* He'd stopped mentioning it to the fourth doctor before that appointment even started, because there was only so many times a man could watch a stranger's face rearrange itself into practiced, professional uncertainty before the telling became its own kind of exhausting.

He walked the rest of the way to his office more carefully than the pain strictly warranted, one hand still faintly pressed to his own chest through his coat, the way a man walks after he's forgotten, for one unguarded second, that a limb doesn't work the way it used to — testing his own balance against a memory of wholeness that belonged, as far as he could ever tell, to someone else's body entirely.

---

That night the dream came again, the way it always did when the ache had been bad during the day, as if the two were the same wound speaking in two different languages. He stood on a battlefield that existed nowhere on any map he'd ever taught from, under a sky bruised the exact color of a wound that hadn't decided whether it meant to heal. Golden-armored soldiers fell around him in every direction, their light going out mid-fall like candles pinched between careless fingers, and he felt each extinguishing not as a sight but as a debt, one more entry in a ledger only he seemed to be keeping. The air tasted of ozone and something else beneath it, something he never had a waking word for. His own hand held a weight that hummed — not with sound, with a kind of certainty that had a pulse of its own, an unbearable clean surety that felt, disorientingly, more like his real hand than the one he woke up with every single morning of his adult life.

Then a shadow crossed the sky in a place no shadow should have been able to reach, low and total and wrong in a way that made every golden soldier around him flinch at once, and something black and ancient drove into him with a sound like a verdict being read aloud. The humming weight was torn from his grip mid-motion, and the loss of it didn't register as disarming — it registered as amputation, as though whatever had just been ripped from his hand had been a second spine, load-bearing, the one thing keeping the rest of him upright.

He woke with a scream caught silent behind his clenched teeth and his hand already closing — fast, practiced, decades of practice compressed into a single sleeping reflex — on nothing at all, on empty air above the blanket, on the exact remembered shape of a weight that had never once, in this life, actually been there.

---

His hand found the fragment on the nightstand instead — it always did, some nights, without his permission — a piece of blackened, unidentifiable metal he'd bought at a flea market years ago for reasons he'd never been able to articulate even to himself, not to the vendor haggling him up two units of currency he could barely spare that month, not to the friend who'd asked, half-joking, if he was starting some kind of collection. It was cool under his palm at first and warmed slowly to his skin the way stone does, jagged at one broken edge in a way that should have hurt to hold and never quite did, etched along its face with lines that seemed, in bad light, to shift very slightly whenever he wasn't looking at them directly, resettling into stillness the instant his eyes returned. He didn't know what it had once been part of. He'd stopped letting himself wonder, most nights. He knew only that closing his hand around it, on nights like this, made the rest of the dream recede a little faster than it otherwise would — like a tide that could, for a few minutes, be persuaded to turn early.

He was still holding it, turning it slowly against his palm, when his phone lit up and buzzed against the nightstand — a colleague from the department, one of the few who still bothered to call instead of text, as if the effort of it were its own small act of care.

"You're up," the voice on the other end said, not quite a question, when Mikel picked up. "Good. Come get a drink with me tomorrow. I'm not asking you to plan it — I'm asking you to show up."

"Can't," Mikel said, already reaching for the excuse that came easiest. "I've got marking to catch up on."

A pause, just long enough to make clear it wasn't believed. "You've been saying that for a month, Ardon. You sleep like garbage — don't tell me you don't, I've seen you in the corridor at eight in the morning looking like you lost a fight with the night. You cancel everything. You've got that look you get when something's chewing on you and won't stop." A breath, softer. "I'm not going to make you talk about it. I just want you to know I'm here, if you ever decide you want to."

Mikel sat with the phone against his ear a moment longer than the silence strictly needed. He thought, with a clarity that startled him, about the girl in the lecture hall and the honest answer he hadn't given her, and about how easy it would be, right now, to say three real sentences instead of one more excuse — to be, for the first time in longer than he could measure, not alone with any of it.

"Thanks," he said instead. "I mean that. Just — not tonight."

"Next week, then. I'm holding you to it."

He said something that might have been agreement, and hung up, and set the phone face-down on the nightstand, and turned off the light, and lay in the dark a while longer with the cold fragment still closed in his fist, listening to his own heartbeat and to nothing else the night was willing to tell him.
