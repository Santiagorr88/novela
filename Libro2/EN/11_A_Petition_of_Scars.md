# Chapter 11 — A Petition of Scars

Camael had not slept in the frontier realms in eleven days, and it showed in ways armor could not hide no matter how carefully a man polished it before a council he had no patience for. His breastplate carried three fresh scores that hadn't been there the last time he'd stood in this chamber, each one scrubbed clean of whatever had made it but not the memory of what had, and a long scorch mark ran the length of his left pauldron where something had tried, and failed, to burn through to the shoulder beneath. He had not bothered to have it repaired before making the crossing back. Let them see it, he'd decided somewhere over the third sleepless night. Let the council look at exactly what the frontier cost before they spent another session arguing about whether it was worth defending.

He crossed the length of the rebuilt chamber with the particular economy of a man who had walked a great many halls in the last decade and stopped, somewhere along the way, seeing any real difference between them. The captains along the walls straightened as he passed, the way soldiers straightened for a commander who had clearly just come from where they had not, and Camael registered the reaction without acknowledging it. He had never wanted deference. He had only ever wanted the council to move at something closer to the speed the frontier demanded of everyone standing in it, and deference, in his long experience, had never once bought him that.

Gabriel rose to meet him halfway, and Camael read the concern in his old friend's face before either of them had spoken a single word — not surprise at the state of the armor, but the deeper, older concern of a man watching someone he cared about arrive already spent. It was a look Camael had grown almost too used to receiving from him over the last several years, and he had never fully decided whether he resented it or was quietly grateful someone still bothered to give it.

"You came straight from the front," Gabriel said. It was not a question.

"I came straight from watching six of my scouts fail to bring back a seventh," Camael said. "Straight seemed the only direction worth taking. I did not think this could wait for me to look presentable first, and I find, the longer this war runs, that I have less and less patience left for looking presentable regardless."

Gabriel's gaze moved once, briefly, over the scorch mark on his shoulder, and something in his expression tightened. "That should have been seen to before you crossed."

"It will be seen to when I have an hour to spare for it," Camael said. "I have not had an hour to spare for anything but this council in longer than I care to count."

---

He did not wait to be invited to speak. He had never been a man for the council's slower rhythms, the careful turn-taking that let every faction feel heard before anything was decided, and eleven sleepless nights had worn away whatever patience he'd once had for it besides. He gestured, and two of his own guard carried in a containment cage between them, iron-worked and warded thick enough that the thing inside it could only be glimpsed in flickers — a shape that was, in the space of a single held breath, something with an angel's bearing, then something with a demon's hunger stretched across the same bones, then something that belonged to neither category at all, formless and wrong in a way that made even Camael's hardened guard look away rather than watch it settle.

"We took this alive," Camael said, "at considerable cost, from a border realm three days past the edge of anything this council has bothered to map in the last decade. It does not know what it is. I watched it try to answer that question to itself for the better part of an hour before I gave the order to bring it here, and it never once settled on an answer that held." He let the cage's flickering light speak for itself a moment before he went on. "Zadkiel has told this council, more than once, that the old boundaries are bending. I am not qualified to argue his theory. I am qualified to tell you what happens on the ground when a boundary finishes bending and simply breaks. This is what happens. We are no longer speculating about a symptom. We captured one."

A murmur ran the length of the chamber, quiet enough to stay beneath the threshold of actual interruption, loud enough that Camael heard exactly how many of the captains along the wall had needed to hear it to believe it. He had expected that. He had brought the creature here precisely because a written report, however carefully worded, had never once moved this council the way a thing they could see with their own eyes did, and he had grown tired of watching his written reports gather dust in whatever chamber Gabriel's aides filed them in.

Gabriel studied the cage a long moment, something grave settling over features that had not fully recovered their old brightness since the retreat. "Zadkiel will want to examine it himself."

"Zadkiel can examine it as long as he needs to," Camael said. "I only ask that this council stop treating his warnings as a hypothesis while I bury soldiers on the strength of what they've already become. I have read his reports, Gabriel. Every one of them, twice, because the first reading never quite convinced me the way I needed to be convinced. This convinced me. I would rather this council reach the same conclusion by looking at what I brought than by waiting for one of my own people to become the next specimen."

---

He paused before he said anything further, and the pause itself was visible — the first break in an otherwise unbroken frontier discipline since he'd entered the room. "Gabriel. Before I make my case properly." He kept his voice low enough that it wouldn't carry past the two of them, though half the chamber was surely watching regardless. "Jeremiel's name. Is it recorded somewhere. Properly. Not just in my own memory, where I've kept it, but somewhere this council will still have it after I'm gone from this seat myself."

Gabriel's expression softened in a way it hadn't for anything else Camael had brought into the room that morning. "It's in the Hall of the Fallen, Camael. Recorded the week after the Sundered Sky, in the same hand that recorded every other name we lost that day. I saw to it myself."

Camael nodded once, a small, contained motion, and said nothing further about it. He did not need to. He had asked the only question that mattered to him on the subject, gotten the only answer he'd come for, and folded it away somewhere behind the armor and the scorch mark and the eleven sleepless nights, exactly where a man like Camael kept the things too large to carry openly through a council chamber.

---

"So," Uriel said, before the silence following that exchange could fully settle, his aura already brightening at the edges of his armor. "Now we have proof. Not theory, not Zadkiel's careful caution about a crisis he still can't fully describe to this council's satisfaction. Proof, in a cage, that the world is coming apart at exactly the pace I've been warning it would."

"It is proof of Zadkiel's crisis," Camael said, turning to face him directly, "not proof of your solution to it. I did not bring this creature here to arm your argument, Uriel. I brought it because I am tired of burying soldiers under a council that debates policy while I have already run out of patience for debate." He let that land, then pressed on, his voice gaining the particular flatness of a man laying out a case he had rehearsed too many times in the dark to soften now. "I am not asking this council to march on Hell. I am asking it to stop treating restraint as the default position simply because it is the more comfortable one. Every week we spend deliberating is a week the frontier pays for in soldiers I have to write letters about afterward. I would like, for once, to write fewer of those letters."

He looked around the chamber as he said it, meeting the eyes of captains who had never once stood a watch on the ground he was describing, and let the silence after his words carry whatever weight it needed to carry on its own. "I do not say this to shame anyone in this room. I say it because I have spent eleven nights watching soldiers under my command hold a line against something none of us fully understands, and I have come to believe that the greatest danger this council faces is not moving too quickly. It is mistaking careful language for careful action, and calling the first one enough."

"Then support the campaign I've already proposed," Uriel said, "and you'll write none at all, because we'll have ended the threat before it reaches your scouts a second time."

"I did not say I supported your campaign," Camael said. "I said I was tired of burying soldiers. Those are not the same sentence, however much you'd like to hear them as one."

---

The exchange did what exchanges in that chamber had lately taken to doing — it did not resolve so much as shift, faction by faction, toward whichever position had just been given the loudest proof. Camael watched it happen with the flat, professional attention of a man reading a battlefield. Uriel's faction, already the loudest in the room, grew visibly louder in the wake of the caged creature's presence, three captains along the far wall exchanging glances that needed no words to carry their meaning. He watched one of them lean toward another and murmur something behind a raised hand, watched the second nod, slow and certain, and understood without needing to hear the words exchanged that the cage had already done more to move that particular corner of the room than any argument Uriel himself could have made without it.

Camael had not intended to become Uriel's evidence. He understood, watching it happen anyway, that intention had very little to do with how a council chose to use what was placed in front of it, and felt something cold and familiar settle into his chest at the understanding — not regret, exactly. He had brought the creature here to be seen for what it was. He simply hadn't accounted for how many different things a single captured horror could be made to mean, depending entirely on who was doing the looking, and found himself, not for the first time this year, wishing this council had fewer factions in it and more soldiers willing to simply agree on what was standing in front of them.

It was Zadkiel who broke the momentum before it could fully harden into consensus, rising from his seat for the first time that session, his silver eyes moving from the cage to Camael and back again with the particular stillness Camael had always found more unsettling than Uriel's fire.

"Camael brings us a warning," Zadkiel said, "and everyone in this chamber hears it as a war drum. I hear it as an alarm bell. They are not the same instrument, whatever their volume might suggest to ears already listening for a reason to march." He crossed to the cage himself, studying the flickering shape within it with the focused, unhurried attention of a physician examining a wound rather than a soldier assessing a threat. "This creature is not evidence that we should strike Hell before Hell strikes us. It is evidence that the cycle itself is failing faster than I feared, in a manner no campaign of Uriel's could touch even if it succeeded completely. You could raze every stronghold Belial holds tomorrow, Uriel, and this — " he gestured at the cage without looking away from it " — would still be happening the day after, because it has nothing to do with Hell's ambitions and everything to do with a structure older than either side of this war beginning to come apart at its seams. Camael did not hand this council a reason to attack. He handed it a reason to stop pretending we have the luxury of understanding this slowly."

Camael said nothing to that, though something in his shoulders eased, fractionally, at hearing his warning returned to him in a shape closer to what he'd actually meant by bringing it. He had not come to arm a faction. He had come because eleven sleepless nights had taught him, more thoroughly than any council session ever could, exactly how little time was left for anyone in this chamber to keep mistaking urgency for someone else's argument instead of the plain, shared fact it actually was.
