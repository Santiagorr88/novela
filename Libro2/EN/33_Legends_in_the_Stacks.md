# Chapter 33 — Legends in the Stacks

The special collections room had become, over the past weeks, less a workspace than a second home, and Mikel had stopped pretending otherwise sometime around the third consecutive night he'd fallen asleep at the reading table with a book still open under his cheek. He knew the smell of it now the way he knew his own office — decaying paper and old leather binding, dust catching gold in the afternoon light through the high windows, a particular staleness that had stopped registering as unpleasant weeks ago and started registering, instead, as simply the smell of getting closer.

He kept the same corner table each visit now, out of habit more than necessity, the surface around him permanently cluttered with open volumes he'd stopped bothering to reshelve between sessions. No one had asked him to clear the space in weeks, an unspoken permission Mikel had accepted gratefully without ever once thanking anyone for it properly.

He worked through source after source with the same patient, methodical hunger, cross-referencing every fragment of the Wandering Bard motif against traditions he'd once have dismissed as unrelated on sight. The physical ritual of it steadied him in a way he hadn't expected — the particular texture of brittle pages under careful fingers, the small, satisfying scratch of his pencil against the notebook he'd carried since the archive, its pages now crowded nearly to the last blank margin with names and fragments and half-formed theories. He had never once, across a career built on the careful, patient accumulation of small certainties, found a search that rewarded the accumulating quite this steadily.

He had abandoned, weeks ago, any pretense of maintaining his ordinary teaching schedule alongside this search. Two lectures cancelled, a graduate seminar handed off to a colleague with an excuse thin enough that he suspected no one had actually believed it, and still he found himself unwilling to slow down even slightly, some old, urgent instinct insisting that the answer sat close enough now to punish him for every hour spent on anything else. He had never once, across nine years of careful, disciplined scholarship, let a research question consume him quite this completely. He found he no longer had much interest in questioning why this one had earned the exception.

---

The archivist found him there most afternoons now, and today she arrived with two cups of coffee she hadn't been asked to bring, setting one down within his reach without comment and settling into the chair across from him with her own. She watched him work a while before she finally said what he suspected she'd come to say, her eyes moving once over the cluttered table between them, taking in the sheer volume of what he'd accumulated without commenting on any of it directly.

She said it plainly, once the silence had stretched long enough that filling it herself apparently seemed the kinder option: "I don't know what you're looking for, Ardon, but whatever it is, it's been looking for you too, for months now. It shows."

Mikel wrapped both hands around the cup more for something to hold onto than for the warmth of it, and found himself, for once, not immediately reaching for a way to deflect the observation. "I'm starting to think it was looking for me long before I noticed anything was missing," he said.

It wasn't a full confession. It was the closest he'd come to one in longer than he could easily measure, and he watched something in her expression settle at hearing it — not satisfaction exactly, but what looked, to Mikel, like the quiet of someone who had been waiting a long time for even this much honesty and had finally, unexpectedly, received it.

"How long, do you think?" she asked, after a moment. "Whatever it is. How long has it been looking?"

Mikel turned the question over longer than its size seemed to warrant, his eyes drifting to the stack of open books between them without really seeing any of them. "I don't know yet," he admitted. "Longer than I've been alive to notice it, is the honest guess. I know that sounds insane."

"You've said stranger things to me this semester than that one." She said it lightly, but something careful sat underneath the lightness, the particular gentleness Mikel read as a friend deciding not to push a door that was already, however slowly, opening on its own. "I'm not going anywhere, Ardon. Whatever this turns out to be."

She didn't push for more. She only nodded once, as though the admission had confirmed something she'd already suspected, and let the silence between them sit companionably rather than forcing it toward anything neither of them was ready for yet.

---

The pattern finally resolved itself three days later, out of fragments scattered across traditions that had never once been placed in conversation with each other before Mikel had thought to try it. A wandering musician who appeared, under different names and different faces, in folklore separated by oceans and centuries — always laughing, always associated with mending rather than breaking, always described by the specific, recurring detail that his songs left behind more than they took. Mikel laid the fragments out across the reading table in careful rows, tracing the same shape through each one, and felt the particular vertigo of a scholar watching an argument he'd built alone finally cohere into something he could no longer talk himself out of believing.

He had spent a career trained to distrust exactly this kind of pattern-matching across unrelated sources, the seductive, dangerous ease of finding connections that existed only in the eye of someone determined to find them. He held the thought up against his own evidence a long while, testing it from every skeptical angle his training still offered him — checking dates against dates, tracing whether any single manuscript could have plausibly influenced another closely enough to explain the overlap through simple borrowing rather than shared origin. No such influence existed anywhere he could find it. The traditions had developed independently, separated by oceans, by centuries, by languages that shared no common root, and still the same figure appeared under a different name in each one, always carrying the same specific detail: he left more behind than he took.

Mikel found, for the first time in this entire strange investigation, that the pattern survived every test he threw at it, and felt something settle in his chest that was neither triumph nor relief, only the plain, quiet certainty of a scholar who had finally stopped needing to doubt his own eyes.

---

The oldest fragments, buried deepest in sources so obscure even the university's own catalogue barely acknowledged their existence, agreed on one further detail the more recent accounts had lost entirely: a nexus, a place where whatever power the Wandering Bard carried had once concentrated most strongly, described in language too old and too inconsistent to translate cleanly. Three separate manuscripts, written in three languages that had never once shared a scholarly tradition, described the same convergence in strikingly similar terms — a place beneath ordinary ground where the boundary between the sacred and the mundane had worn thin enough, once, for something to pass through it and settle.

Mikel spent the better part of an afternoon cross-referencing each fragment's fractured, half-mythical geography against a modern survey map, discarding one candidate location after another as the details refused to align cleanly. The first manuscript described a ridge overlooking water that no longer matched any coastline he could find on record. The second spoke of a grove standing at the meeting point of two old roads, roads that had since been paved over so thoroughly their original course survived only in a handful of property deeds older than the university itself. It was the third source, the most fragmentary of the three, that finally supplied the detail that let the other two click into place — a passing, almost incidental reference to a place of learning already old when the manuscript's own author had written it down.

He marked all three locations on the same map and sat back slowly as a single point finally emerged where all three accounts agreed closely enough to trust. He stared at the overlapping marks a long moment before he let himself believe what they were actually telling him.

Not some distant island. Not a forgotten valley three continents removed from anything familiar. The nexus sat, according to sources centuries older than the university itself, directly beneath the ground he'd walked across every single day for nine years without once suspecting what waited underneath it. He checked the overlap a second time, then a third, half expecting the coincidence to dissolve under closer scrutiny the way so many promising leads already had across this long, strange search. It didn't. The three marks held their position on the map with the same stubborn certainty every time he checked them.

---

He left the archive later than he meant to that evening, his mind still circling the map's impossible coincidence, and crossed the quad toward the main gate with barely half his attention on the path in front of him. A street musician sat near the gate again, guitar case open beside him, but the guitar itself lay untouched across his knees, silent. He wasn't playing. He simply sat, watching the library's old stone face with a stillness that struck Mikel, in the half-second his attention actually caught on it, as strange enough to notice at all.

Mikel slowed for the length of a single step, some small, unexamined part of him registering that the musician looked, sitting there in that unnatural quiet, like a man waiting for something specific to happen. He didn't stop to consider it further. His mind was already elsewhere, turning over the map and the nexus and the ground beneath his own feet, and he walked on past the gate without a second glance, the musician's stillness noted and set aside like a hundred other small oddities this whole investigation had trained him to notice without stopping to examine.

---

He reached his car and sat behind the wheel a long while before he trusted himself to drive, the whole shape of the discovery finally settling properly into place. He had crossed an ocean, risked a fisherman's cryptic warnings, spent months chasing a myth through libraries and folklore collections scattered across half a dozen unrelated traditions — and the answer, in the end, had been waiting the entire time directly beneath the halls where he'd built his entire ordinary life.

He thought of every morning he'd walked across that same ground on his way to a lecture, entirely unaware of what supposedly waited underneath his own feet, and felt a strange, dislocating vertigo at the thought — not fear exactly, though fear sat somewhere close to it, but the specific disorientation of a man discovering that the map of his own daily life had been drawn wrong the entire time, its most important landmark simply left off the page. Nine long years of ordinary quiet mornings, and not one of them had ever thought to warn him.

He had a destination now, close enough to walk to, and he understood, starting the engine at last, that whatever he found there was going to cost him considerably more to face than any of the searching that had brought him this close to it. He drove home slowly that night, the notebook full of names and fragments sitting on the passenger seat where the inscription's copy had ridden home from Turein weeks before, and let himself feel, for the length of the whole quiet drive, the particular weight of finally standing at the edge of an answer he no longer had any excuse left to avoid. He would sleep on it, he decided, pulling into his own driveway at last. Tomorrow, in the plain, ordinary light of morning, he would actually go looking.
