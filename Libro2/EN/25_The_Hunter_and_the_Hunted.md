# Chapter 25 — The Hunter and the Hunted

Arin had not slept in the same city twice since the Grey City, and he'd stopped expecting to anytime soon. He moved the way he'd once moved between contracts, city to city, cash to stolen cash, except now the men reading his movements weren't rival mercenaries or the occasional police sketch. He could feel them if he held still long enough to pay proper attention — demons circling in hungry, brutish probes that never quite closed the distance, and something else behind them, colder and more patient, watching from a remove he couldn't quite locate no matter how carefully he searched for it. He trusted neither. He had learned, across a month that felt considerably longer than a month, that trusting either side of this war was the fastest way to end up owned by it.

He kept moving because moving was the only strategy he had left that still worked reliably. A man who never slept in the same place twice was harder to corner than a man who thought he'd found somewhere safe.

He had grown fluent, over these last weeks, in a kind of exhaustion he'd never once encountered across a career built on considerably more conventional dangers. Contract work had taught him to read a room, a target, an exit. It had never once taught him to read the particular pressure of being watched by two entire realms at once, each one weighing him against a purpose he still didn't fully understand himself. He ate when food presented itself, slept in short, careful stretches that never let his guard drop all the way, and kept walking, because the alternative to walking was standing still long enough for one of his watchers to finally decide he was worth the trouble of closing the distance.

---

He shared a table without meaning to in a refugee shelter two cities south of the Grey City's ruins, one of the countless makeshift kitchens that had sprung up in the weeks since the tower's collapse to feed people who'd fled a city that had, by every official account, simply suffered an industrial accident. The shelter itself was a converted gymnasium, cots lined in rows too close together, the particular hum of too many displaced lives crowded into too little space filling every corner of it. Arin had chosen this table specifically because it sat farthest from the door, the seat that let him watch every entrance without appearing to watch anything at all. The man across from him hadn't stopped talking about it since Arin sat down, the particular compulsive retelling of someone who needed to keep saying a thing out loud until it finally stopped feeling unreal.

"You hear what really happened at that facility?" The man leaned in, voice dropped to the pitch people used for things they weren't supposed to believe. "My cousin worked security two buildings over. Says something walked in there and just — undid people. Not killed. Undid. Like they were never solid to begin with." He studied Arin a moment, searching his face for the reaction he'd apparently been fishing for from everyone at this table for days. "Some people are saying it was an angel. You hear that too?"

Arin held the man's gaze, weighing the lie against the truth and finding, as he usually did lately, that the honest answer cost him less than either extreme. "I heard it was something that shouldn't have been able to happen at all," he said. "Everything past that is just words people need so they can sleep at night."

The man studied him a moment longer, something in his expression settling rather than sharpening, as if the answer had satisfied him more thoroughly than a clean confirmation would have. He nodded slowly, and went back to his food without pressing further, and Arin sat with the strange, hollow feeling of having just watched a stranger accept a version of his own legend that no longer had anything to do with the man who'd actually lived it.

He finished his meal in the silence that followed, listening to the rest of the room's low murmur of half-formed rumor drifting around him — someone else's cousin, someone else's version of the same impossible night, each retelling drifting a little further from whatever had actually happened in that corridor. He understood, sitting there, that the story no longer belonged to him at all. It belonged to this room now, to every shelter like it scattered across however many cities the Grey City's refugees had fled to, growing stranger and larger with each new telling, and there was nothing left for him to do about that except let it happen.

---

He found the demon three days later, in a warehouse district gone half to rust on the outskirts of a city he'd already half-forgotten the name of, tracking a scent of absence that had grown familiar enough over the past month to follow almost without conscious thought. The creature had chosen its hunting ground carelessly, close enough to a residential block that Arin had spent an hour simply confirming it hadn't already taken anyone before he moved. It hadn't. He counted that a small mercy, and let it buy the creature nothing once he started closing the distance between them.

He'd nearly had the wrong read on it entirely. The demon broke for the open street the moment it caught his scent instead of the other way around, faster and more coordinated than the clumsy scavengers he'd grown used to running down across the past month, and for one disorienting stretch of alley Arin understood, with a clarity that cost him a full second of hesitation, that this particular hunt had briefly reversed itself without his permission. It doubled back through a gap in a collapsed fence he hadn't accounted for, nearly putting a full block of open ground between them before he caught the shift and cut across a loading yard to head it off instead of simply chasing it. He reached the rusted dock at the district's dead end half a step ahead of it, close enough that the demon's own momentum carried it straight into his waiting grip. It hadn't been hunting anyone tonight. It had simply, for one brief and genuinely uncomfortable stretch of alley, very nearly stopped being the one being hunted.

The interrogation that followed bore no resemblance to conversation. He held the creature's essence gathered loosely in one closed fist, the threat of unmaking it plain and unhurried, and let the demon's own fear do most of the remaining work.

"The Grey City facility," Arin said, "was not the only one."

"No." The demon's voice came out thin, flickering at the edges the way its borrowed shape kept flickering under the pressure Arin held it with. "There are others. All of them feeding the same operation. One central collection point, one portal, everything else just — tributary."

"Where."

The demon hesitated, and Arin tightened his grip fractionally, enough to make the cost of hesitation plain without needing to spend more than that. "Hidden," the demon said, the word coming apart into something closer to a gurgle. "Shielded, in a place with enough ambient psychic noise to mask the signature from anyone not already looking directly at it. An old university. Human place of knowledge, human place of power, more of both than you'd expect from somewhere that size." It gave the name like a man surrendering the last coin he had left to spend. "Navarion."

---

Arin let the name settle into him the way he'd learned to let every important piece of information settle before acting on it, and felt something sharpen in his chest that had nothing to do with the compass's usual pull toward absence. This was not simply another harvesting site to shut down. This was the center of the entire operation, the place every tributary in this monstrous network ultimately fed back into, and he understood, holding the demon's fear in one closed hand, that finding it changed the shape of everything he'd been doing since the Grey City.

He thought of every silent captive he'd passed in that first facility, of the woman from the fourth cell and the boy in the extraction frame and the countless others he'd never had the chance to reach at all. If this university truly sat at the center of the whole operation, then every one of those harvesting sites, every quiet horror scattered across however many cities this network actually spanned, all led back to a single point on a map he now finally had a name for. That was not a small thing to hold. He held it anyway, the way he'd learned to hold everything else this last month had handed him.

He was no longer running from something. He had, for the first time since this whole nightmare had started pulling him along behind it, an actual destination — a place to go toward instead of simply somewhere further to flee.

---

He should have ended it there, the interrogation complete, the information extracted and confirmed. He found himself instead studying the demon's face a moment longer, some old, unexamined curiosity surfacing before he'd fully decided to let it. "Do you recognize anything in me," he asked, "beyond what I can do to you right now?"

The demon's eyes, wide with fear a moment before, narrowed instead into something closer to genuine confusion. "Recognize?" It searched his face the way a man searches a stranger's for a resemblance he can't quite place, and came up with nothing solid enough to answer. "You're strong. Stronger than anything wearing that shape should be. I don't know what that means beyond what it plainly is." It shook its head, some of the fear returning now that the strange question had passed. "Should it mean something else?"

Arin didn't answer. He released the creature's essence and let it flee into the dark without pursuit, and stood alone a long moment in the rusting warehouse, turning the question over the way he'd once turned over a veteran's strange remark in a bar he could no longer picture clearly — *you ever get the feeling you've done this before.* He had no better answer for it now than he'd had then. He only knew that the question kept returning at exactly the moments he most needed to stay focused on something simpler, and that its persistence was, in its own quiet way, becoming a kind of answer all its own, even if he couldn't yet read what it actually said.

He turned the strangeness of it over a while longer, standing alone in the warehouse's rusted quiet, and found himself wondering, not for the first time, why a captured demon's blank confusion should matter to him at all. He had asked plenty of frightened creatures plenty of questions across the last month, and had never once cared, before tonight, whether any of them looked back at him and saw something they recognized. He made himself stop turning it over before it cost him more focus than he could afford to spend on it tonight.

He turned north, toward Navarion, and let the question stay unanswered, the same way he'd learned to let everything else about this last month stay unanswered. The road ahead would take days regardless of how fast he moved, and he found himself, walking away from the warehouse's rusted quiet, oddly grateful for the distance. It would give him time to think of nothing in particular, which was, these days, the closest thing to rest he still knew how to find.
