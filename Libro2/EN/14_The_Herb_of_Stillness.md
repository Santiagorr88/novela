# Chapter 14 — The Herb of Stillness

The feeling had followed him for three blocks before Mikel let himself admit he'd noticed it at all. It sat somewhere behind his sternum, a low, steady pressure that had nothing to do with the late hour or the empty street or the particular quality of silence a university town took on once the last lecture hall had emptied for the night. He told himself, as he'd told himself every night for the better part of a week now, that it was nothing — a mind that had spent too long lately on judgment and mercy and impossible swords, tired enough to start manufacturing threats to match the mood it had been living in. He had built an entire career on the discipline of not mistaking pattern for coincidence. He was, he reminded himself, breaking his own rule tonight for no better reason than exhaustion.

He did not turn around. Turning around would have meant admitting there was something behind him worth turning for, and admitting that felt, for reasons he couldn't fully justify even to himself, like the first step toward believing every strange thing his own mind had been feeding him since the dreams began. He kept walking instead, hands in his coat pockets, his pace neither faster nor slower than it had been outside the archive twenty minutes earlier, and told the pressure behind his sternum, silently and without much conviction, that it was simply going to have to wait until he was somewhere with better lighting to be taken seriously.

The campus thinned out behind him block by block, lecture halls giving way to the quieter residential streets he'd walked home along for nine years without once giving the route a second thought. He knew every crack in this pavement, every house whose porch light never quite worked, and the familiarity of it had always been a comfort before tonight. Tonight it felt thinner than usual, worn down to something he couldn't quite name, and he found himself watching the dark gaps between streetlights a moment longer than the route had ever required of him before.

---

The attack came in the narrow stretch between two shuttered storefronts, where the streetlight had burned out weeks ago and the university's maintenance requests apparently moved slower than anything else in Mikel's life. He heard nothing before the impact — no footstep, no rush of displaced air, nothing his ordinary senses could have flagged in time to matter. One instant he was walking. The next he was on the ground, the book bag torn from his shoulder and skidding somewhere out of reach, a weight pinning him that felt, in the single confused heartbeat before real fear arrived, entirely too large and too wrong-shaped to belong to anything human.

He looked up into something that had a face the way a storm has a face — vaguely, suggestively, in a shape his mind kept trying and failing to resolve into anything it recognized. Every instinct he possessed screamed at him to fight, and none of them told him how. There should have been something in him for this. He had felt it, hadn't he, in dreams he could never fully remember on waking — some old, buried competence that belonged to a version of himself he'd never consciously met. It did not surface now. Whatever lived in him from before stayed buried exactly where it had always stayed, and Mikel Ardon, professor of military history, found himself flat on cold pavement with a monster's hand closing around his throat and nothing in his own body willing to answer for him.

He tried, in the scattered half-second his mind could still spare for trying, to remember anything useful from a lifetime spent reading about battles fought by other men — some fragment of tactics, some instinct for where a weak point ought to be on a body built like this one. Nothing came. He had spent a career studying how soldiers survived the worst hour of their lives and found, when the worst hour of his own life finally arrived, that none of it transferred from the page to his own two hands. He was, in the end, exactly what he had always quietly suspected himself to be underneath the tenure and the publications: a man who had only ever watched war from a very great and very safe distance.

---

The hand closed around his throat and, in the very same instant, simply stopped — not loosened, not eased, only stopped, every ounce of intent behind it draining out of the grip as if the creature had forgotten, mid-motion, what it had reached for him to do. Mikel felt the pressure hang there, arrested, neither tightening further nor releasing, for one long, disorienting heartbeat.

Then he watched whatever crouched above him convulse once, its half-resolved face contorting into an expression that had nothing to do with pain and everything to do with a peace it seemed, as far as Mikel could tell, never to have expected to feel. Something passed through the alley in that instant that Mikel had no name for and, turning it over afterward, could not confidently place. It might have come from somewhere very far off — a door swinging open impossibly distant and letting a single breath of different air reach this narrow stretch of pavement before it swung shut again. Or it might, just as easily, have come from nowhere outside the alley at all. He could not rule out that whatever had reached the creature had reached through him to do it, some small and unfamiliar thing in his own chest answering a threat his hands and his training had failed to answer, before folding itself away again too quickly for him to catch hold of it. He had no way to tell the two possibilities apart, and no one he could ask who wouldn't think him mad for asking.

The creature's grip loosened on its own. It staggered back a step, then another, then turned and fled into the dark at the alley's far end without a single backward glance, moving like something that had just remembered, all at once, exactly how much larger the world was than the meal it had been about to make of him.

---

Mikel lay on the cold pavement a long moment before he trusted his own legs enough to sit up, his pulse loud enough in his ears to drown out the ordinary night sounds slowly reasserting themselves around him. It was only once he'd gotten his breathing back under some approximation of control that he noticed the leaf.

It lay exactly where the creature had crouched a moment before, pale and faintly luminous even in the alley's near-total dark, its shape unlike anything that grew in any garden or forest Mikel had ever walked through in his life — veins that ran the wrong direction, an edge that seemed to shift faintly when he wasn't looking at it directly. He picked it up with hands that were still not entirely steady, half expecting it to dissolve at his touch the way things in dreams so often did. It didn't. It sat solid and cool against his palm, and the moment his fingers closed around it, something in his chest — that same old, familiar absence he'd carried for as long as he could remember — went quiet in a way it hadn't gone quiet in years. Not answered. Simply, briefly, at rest.

He sat there in the mouth of the alley for a long time, turning the leaf over in trembling fingers, and found he could not have said, if anyone had asked him in that moment, whether the calm settling into him belonged to the leaf itself or to something in himself finally recognizing, however dimly, a shape it had been waiting to hold again.

---

He called her before he'd fully decided to, his thumb finding the archivist's number in his contacts with the particular unthinking speed of a man who needed to hear another human voice more than he needed a good reason to interrupt her evening. She picked up on the third ring, and he heard, even before either of them said anything useful, the small sounds of a life continuing somewhere else entirely — a television murmuring low in the background, the domestic ordinariness of it almost unbearable against what he'd just survived.

"Ardon." Her voice carried a flatness that sounded, to him, like someone deciding in real time whether to be annoyed at the hour. "It's nearly midnight. Please tell me this is about the shelf and not something that could have waited for office hours."

"It's about the shelf," he said, and heard how unconvincing it sounded even as it left his mouth. "I had a — I wanted to ask whether the special collections catalogue has a cross-reference system for the older ethnographies. The uncatalogued ones."

There was a pause on the other end, longer than the question warranted. "You sound strange. Are you all right?"

He opened his mouth to tell her. The words sat right there, close enough to taste — *I was attacked in an alley by something that wasn't a person, and then it simply stopped, and I don't know why, and I am sitting on the pavement with a leaf in my hand that doesn't belong to this world* — and for one full second he meant to say some version of it, because the alternative was carrying the whole of it alone for one more night in a string of nights that had already gone on far too long.

"I'm just tired," he said instead. "Long day. I'll ask about the catalogue properly tomorrow."

She didn't push, though he could hear, in the small silence that followed, that she didn't entirely believe him either. "Sleep something off, Ardon. We'll pick up the section tomorrow." The line went quiet, and he sat there afterward with the phone still warm against his ear and the leaf still cool in his other hand, and let both silences sit with him a while longer before he finally stood, dusted off his coat, and started the walk home.

---

He examined the leaf properly once he was back in his own apartment, under a lamp bright enough to banish whatever the alley's darkness might have exaggerated, and found it looked no less impossible in good light than it had in bad. He turned it slowly between two fingers, watching the faint luminescence shift with the angle, and let a thought surface that he had circled for years without ever once letting it land in words this exact.

He had felt borrowed for as long as he could remember. Not unhappy, precisely — his life fit him well enough, on the surface, in the way a life assembled from reasonable choices generally did. But underneath the surface there had always been this: the persistent, low sense of a coat cut for someone else's shoulders, worn every day regardless. He had never let himself say it plainly, not even to the version of himself that surfaced only at two in the morning. He said it now, quietly, to an empty apartment and a leaf that had no business existing.

"I don't think this is my life," he said aloud, and heard how strange the words sounded leaving his mouth, and did not take them back.

He reached no conclusion from saying it. The leaf offered no answer, and the apartment stayed exactly as empty and exactly as quiet as it had been before he'd spoken. But something in him had shifted all the same, the way a door shifts when it is finally, after years of resting nearly shut, pushed the rest of the way open — not resolved, not explained, only, for the first time, honestly acknowledged. He set the leaf down carefully on the desk beside the old ethnography he still hadn't returned, and sat with the borrowed shape of his own life a while longer before he finally let himself sleep.
