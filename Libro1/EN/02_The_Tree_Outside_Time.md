# Chapter 2 — The Tree Outside Time

He was cast from his own body into cosmic, amoral chaos. Supernovas guttered out one after another, gone before their light reached him. Somewhere, a star-sized hammer rang against an anvil made from a collapsed sun, and galaxies rose and unraveled with each strike, born and undone faster than his mind could hold either state as real. Nebulae twisted and reformed into shapes that had no names in any language Miguel knew. He had stood in the presence of the Divine Word before, had felt creation as an act of order, of law given breath. This was not that. This was making without a ledger, without a plan he could trace, without the faintest concern for what any of it would cost.

His mind reached for words like *good* or *evil* and found them laughably small against what unfolded before him. He was a mote of dust, witness to something no soul was ever meant to see, and his own comprehension was not required for any of it to be true. He tried, out of old habit, to fix a location to what he was seeing — a plane, a realm, some coordinate he could report back to the Council. There was none. Wherever this was, it existed before the maps he knew had names for their edges. He had led armies across a dozen fronts, had memorized the topography of Heaven's wars until it lived in his body more than his memory. None of that training offered him a single foothold here.

Through all of it ran laughter. Jubilant, irreverent, and utterly without concern for consequence — the most frightening sound Miguel had ever heard, because it held no cruelty in it at all, only indifference to every distinction by which he understood existence. This blade had not been forged for a cause. It was neither holy relic nor demonic weapon. It had been made, he understood with soul-deep certainty, for the sheer joy of making it — nothing grander, nothing crueler, nothing that answered to Heaven or Hell or any court that might someday judge it. One final peal of laughter ended the vision. Miguel returned to his body gasping, his heart racing with a fear he had never known: not damnation, but meaninglessness.

As the laughter receded, a second sound lingered beneath it — not an echo, but a lower voice. Miguel went still, straining after it, one hand braced against his own knee. It might have been argument. It might have been protest, one voice pressing against the joy of the other, quieter and almost pained. The harder he listened, the less distinguishable the second voice became from his own doubt, until he could no longer be certain he had heard anything at all beyond the ringing aftermath of the first.

He told himself it was only the last distortion of a fading vision. But some old, disciplined part of him — the part that had listened for ambush in a thousand silences — refused to fully believe that. Had another hand moved within the making, or was his guilt inventing a second presence beneath the laughter?

He turned the question over for as long as he dared, which was not long. The grove had gone quiet around him again, the metal leaves motionless, the light flat and waiting, and nothing in that stillness offered him an answer either way. He had commanded armies built on the certainty that an enemy's intentions could be read from posture, from breath, from the smallest tell a body could not help but give away. This vision had offered him none of that — only sound, already gone, already halfway to something he might have imagined out of his own need to not be the only one questioning what he had just watched come into being. He waited a half-second longer than pride liked to admit, listening for the second voice to return. It did not. Whatever it had been, the vision had carried it beyond his reach. Doubt was a luxury the ache in his chest would not let him keep, not this close to an answer he had crossed a wasteland to find.

He stood before the sword, the maker's laughter still echoing somewhere behind his sternum, fainter now but not gone. The questions — origin, purpose, maker, the second voice he could no longer swear he had heard — remained unanswered, but the blade itself demanded his full attention now. There was only the sword's truth, and it was enough.

He closed his hand around the hilt, no longer hesitant, and drew Solmire from the heart of the ash. The old tree groaned as the blade left it, a low, grinding sound felt as much through the soles of his boots as heard. The grove's light dimmed, and the air turned unnaturally still and cold. Even the metal leaves, which had not stirred once since he entered, seemed to hold themselves more rigidly than before.

Power moved through him — not warm, not holy, but clean and final. The ache in his chest simply stopped, replaced not by peace but by placid, terrible certainty. His weariness vanished in an instant, replaced by an unnerving perfection of posture: his spine straightened of its own accord, his breath settled into a rhythm he had not chosen. Even his grip on the hilt adjusted itself without his willing it, his fingers finding a hold that felt rehearsed a thousand times over, though he had never held this weapon before this hour.

He was whole, but part of him had gone quiet. He lifted the blade before his eyes and studied it — light given weight and edge, now awaiting whatever purpose its bearer might impose upon it. The blade imposed no demand he could perceive. It did not ask to be raised for judgment, or for mercy, or for anything at all. That, more than anything else in the grove, was what unsettled him most.

Behind him, the tree was dying. Not slowly, the way trees were meant to die, but in minutes — bark splitting along lines too straight to be natural, leaves curling and dropping in handfuls rather than one at a time, the starlit pool at its roots dulling from silver to gray. Miguel turned at the sound of it, the sword still heavy and strange in his grip, and watched centuries of quiet keeping come undone in the space of a few breaths.

He felt, unexpectedly, a pang close to grief. This tree had held the sword through however many ages, asking nothing, guarding it without complaint or witness, and its only reward for finally releasing its charge was to wither where it stood, unseen by anyone who would ever know to mourn it. He crouched anyway, sword still in one hand, and laid two fingers of the other against the cracking bark. It made no sense, even to him. He did it anyway. Gray dust clung to his fingertips, dry as old parchment, flaking away and catching briefly in the flat, shadowless light before settling. Somewhere above him, a branch that had still held a handful of leaves a moment ago gave way and dropped, splitting in two when it struck the ground. He counted, without meaning to, how many more branches remained. Not many. He stopped counting before he reached the end, unwilling to watch the number hit zero, and made himself look away, back toward the sword in his own hand. Then the last leaf let go and spiraled down without a sound, and there was nothing left standing to mourn over — only bare, gray wood where a living thing had been.

He stayed crouched a moment longer than the task required, his hand still resting against the dead bark, waiting to see if some deeper part of him would object to what he had just witnessed. It did not. The sword's certainty had already smothered his grief, along with everything else that did not serve the pull now guiding him. He straightened, sword still in hand, already turning his attention toward whatever came next.

The silence returned, absolute. The grove showed no further sign of life. Somewhere beyond the dying trees, a pulse began, faint but steadily strengthening, felt before it was heard. It seemed to answer the authority newly present within him.

He lowered the blade slowly, weighing its strange, perfect balance in his palm, testing it the way he might test any weapon before a first march. No weapon had ever felt this settled in his hand. His face settled into calm. He was already considering the wars ahead.

He took one step toward the edge of the grove, then stopped and looked back once at the dead ash. Nothing here would explain itself to him further. He had not expected it to. He turned and walked on, and the trees parted ahead of him without a sound, closing again once he had passed. Nothing about him — nothing about the world he was walking back into — would be the same again.

Far from the dying grove, in a quiet chamber carved from shadow and old ambition, Lucifer set down the delicate clockwork instrument he had been adjusting, its tiny gears still turning uselessly in the air — hair the white-silver of old frost, eyes the deep red of poured wine, a beauty that had survived the Fall better than anything else about him had, wings black enough to swallow what little light the chamber kept. He carried no weapon anyone in this palace had ever seen him draw; he had never needed one to empty a room simply by entering it. He tilted his head, listening to something no wall could have carried. His expression held no alarm — only pure, unhurried curiosity, the look of a man recognizing a voice from a room he had not entered in a very long time. His lips shaped a silent word: *old*.

The chamber around him held no other sound. Rows of half-finished instruments lined the walls, each one paused mid-assembly, gears exposed, cases open, as though their maker had set every one of them down at the first hint of a stranger sound. Dust had settled on none of them; whatever had drawn his attention away, it had happened only an instant before, mid-motion.

He set the little machine aside entirely now, its gears winding down into stillness, and rose from his seat. For a long moment he simply stood there, head still tilted, motionless, as if the signal might resolve into words if he gave it enough silence in return. It did not. The question stayed open.

A slow, genuine smile spread across his face, unhurried, reaching all the way to his eyes.

Near the chamber's entrance, a courtier — one of the countless nameless functionaries who kept Hell's machinery turning, all title and no name worth remembering — froze mid-step at the sight of that smile. He leaned toward the courtier beside him, not quite whispering, his eyes still fixed on the Morningstar. "Has he smiled like that before?" His voice barely carried past the two of them.

The second courtier kept his gaze rigidly forward, unmoving. "Only when something's about to change."

Neither of them spoke again. They stood a moment longer in the doorway, matching stillnesses, then withdrew in opposite directions without another word: one clutching his ledger to his chest, the other turning abruptly into the corridor and not looking back. Lucifer gave no sign that he had noticed them.

"So," he murmured to the empty room, resuming his work on the little machine, his fingers finding their place among the gears without looking. "One of the old songs is being sung again. How interesting." He said nothing else, and the gears beneath his hands turned steadily on, unhurried as everything else about him.

The smile remained. His gaze stayed fixed beyond the turning gears, somewhere past the walls of the chamber, and he did not speak again.
