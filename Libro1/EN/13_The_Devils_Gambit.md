# Chapter 13 — The Devil's Gambit

Belial reviewed the loyalty rolls with an unnamed adjutant in a side chamber of the war citadel, his questions arriving in the same flat rhythm he used for real matters of supply and rotation. Who answered to whom. Who would be missed, and by how many, and for how long. The adjutant read back the answers without a flicker of suspicion, taking Belial for nothing more than a commander thorough enough to know his own chain of command.

Belial already knew what the ledgers would say before the adjutant finished reading. Aamon was Commander of the Infernal Legions — a rank that made his loss a real cost to Hell's war footing, not a convenient fiction to make the coming betrayal easier. He let the adjutant finish anyway; certainty was a discipline worth keeping, even certainty he didn't need. He asked after Aamon's captains next, one by one, noting which owed their positions to Aamon directly and which merely served under him by rank. The difference mattered, though he gave the adjutant no reason to wonder why. Dubium hung quiet at his hip, its doubt-bearing blows unused for now. Aamon's real cost, Belial decided, with the same cold self-interest he'd spent at the Tower of the Eternals, was simply not a cost he intended to carry himself.

He did not hesitate over that decision, and did not revisit it. He thanked the adjutant, dismissed him, and sat alone afterward turning the plan over in his mind, checking it the way a soldier checks a blade before a fight he has already decided to win.

He found Aamon in his throne hall an hour later and inclined his head — the formality of an announced visit rather than a summons, a courtesy that cost him nothing and let Aamon believe the visit meant more than it did. The hall had clearly been built for performances like this one: high obsidian columns arranged so that anyone standing before the throne had to look up just to be seen, a design Belial suspected Aamon had commissioned himself. Braziers set into the columns threw a restless orange light that never reached the vaulted ceiling. Somewhere above, a current of air moved through the hall with a low, constant moan.

"Commander," Belial said, "I've found something worth your attention. A celestial convoy, moving through a corridor no one but me seems to be watching."

He laid out the plan with careful precision, an ambush timed to a narrow dimensional crossing, framed to flatter the exact appetite he'd studied in Aamon for years: glory sung for eras, a strike personal enough to be remembered as his alone. He understated the risk wherever that would go unquestioned, and inflated the reward wherever that would land soft on ears already hungry to hear it.

He had rehearsed the presentation more than once before walking into this hall, though nothing in his manner tonight betrayed it. Each pause landed where a pause would seem natural rather than calculated. Each detail he withheld until Aamon's own questions drew it out of him, so the commander could feel himself sharpening the plan instead of receiving it whole. Belial had learned, across years serving commanders who valued their own cleverness above almost anything else, that the surest way to sell a lie was never to hand it over finished — only to hand over enough that the mark would insist on finishing the rest himself, and defend it afterward as his own.

Aamon listened with his arms crossed, his expression the same guarded calculation he brought to any gift he hadn't asked for and hadn't yet priced. By the time Belial finished speaking, that expression had already shifted toward appetite.

"Order within chaos is carved by blade and fury," Aamon said, his voice flat and martial even turned inward like this, more to himself than to Belial, turning the plan over as though he'd imagined it first.

He asked two sharp questions about timing and formation. Belial had anticipated both and answered before Aamon finished asking. By the third exchange, the plan had stopped belonging to Belial at all in Aamon's telling of it. He asked nothing about what the coordinates actually meant beyond a convoy worth taking — Belial judged, from the shape of his questions, that his commander saw nothing there but the richest target he'd been offered in a long while. The crossing was, in truth, a place of vast hidden significance and no strategic value whatsoever, a distinction Aamon's questions never came close to reaching.

He rose from his throne as he spoke, pacing the hall in the restless pattern that always overtook him once a plan had taken hold, narrating the coming battle aloud as though addressing an audience only he could see. Belial watched him build the story out loud, adding flourishes Belial hadn't thought to suggest, each one fitting the trap more snugly around the ambition that would carry Aamon straight into it. By the time he finished pacing, the plan bore his voice, his cadence, his hunger for glory sung across eras. Belial's own part in it had already faded, in Aamon's telling, to little more than the messenger who'd brought the opportunity to his door.

"You'll ride with the vanguard," Aamon said, already deciding every detail of the coming battle down to its smallest formation. "A commander who brings me a gift this good earns the right to watch it opened."

Belial inclined his head as though the offer were a courtesy rather than the exact concession his whole visit had been built to extract. Being near the front, close enough to see the coordinates for himself the moment the legion arrived, was not a reward he'd asked for outright — it was the one he had spent the whole conversation quietly arranging for Aamon to offer on his own.

---

Andras watched from the edge of the hall, silent, the way he watched most things that weren't yet his to act on. Aamon's enthusiasm rose beat by beat as he reworked Belial's ambush into a plan he now called his own, and Andras couldn't look away from how clean the whole exchange had been.

He knew the shape of a well-laid trap better than almost anyone in Aamon's service — laying them was the substance of his own work, the blade no one saw coming, the danger dressed as opportunity. Something about the ease of Belial's presentation sat wrong with him, a smoothness that read, to his professionally suspicious eye, less like genuine intelligence and more like a performance rehearsed until it no longer looked rehearsed.

Andras had built his own reputation doing exactly this — dagger-work that vanished before an alarm could sound — and he recognized the texture of it the way a forger recognizes another forger's hand. Belial's timing had been too clean, his answers to Aamon's questions arriving a beat too readily prepared for information he was supposedly sharing for the first time. A lesser observer would have called it competence. Andras, who made his living in the gap between what a plan claimed to be and what it actually was, called it rehearsal, and couldn't shake the feeling once he'd named it, even to himself.

He turned the thought over once, carefully, and set it back down just as carefully. Aamon's fall, if it came, would not open a captaincy Andras could inherit — Aamon was a Commander, and any reshuffling that followed would ripple through ranks well above Andras's reach. Accusing a fellow captain's patron of being played, on instinct alone and with nothing solid to point to, was a gamble that could land on the accuser as easily as the accused. He weighed what he stood to gain from speaking against what he stood to lose from being wrong. The second column was heavier, and it wasn't close. Andras said nothing. He pocketed the unease and waited to see which way the new arrangement of favors would fall before deciding whether it was worth mentioning to anyone at all.

---

Belial spent the following day threading the plan's details through channels he kept in reserve for leaks like this one — a double agent here, a compromised courier there, each carrying a fragment convincing enough to be believed and incomplete enough to seem accidentally overheard rather than deliberately handed over.

He needed Heaven to answer for real, not merely to have the coordinates land somewhere angelic hands might find them. A trap sold to Aamon on Belial's word alone was fragile; one confirmed by an actual celestial response, however small, would carry a weight no persuasion could match. He worked the leak carefully over the following hours, patient in a way few of his rivals would have credited him with, waiting for the one response that would tell him the bait had been swallowed exactly where he needed it swallowed.

He chose his agents the way he chose everything else in this scheme: by what each could plausibly be believed to know, and nothing more. Neither agent knew the other existed, and neither knew the true purpose behind the errand — a precaution Belial treated as reflex, not choice. The compromised courier carried only a fragment, a partial route stripped of anything specific enough to raise real alarm. The double agent carried a second fragment, close enough to the first to corroborate it without completing the picture either fragment alone would have suggested. Information handed over whole invited scrutiny; information gathered in pieces invited belief.

The response came before the day was out — a shift in celestial patrol patterns subtle enough to escape most eyes, but confirmation enough that Aamon treated the news as final proof the convoy was real and the timing his to seize. Belial received the same report with far less relief and far more satisfaction: the last piece of the trap settling where he had left room for it.

Aamon gave the order to march within the hour, his legion falling into formation with the swift, disciplined efficiency Belial had watched him bring to the field before. The route he chose ran uncomfortably close to the dimensional coordinates of the Weeping Sepulcher, close enough that Belial rode the first stretch with his jaw set tighter than his posture let on.

The legion moved in good order behind them, banners low against the wind, soldiers trading the kind of dark jokes that sounded, to Belial's ear, like the confidence of troops expecting an easy victory — unearned, and entirely useful to the man leading them into it. No one asked what the coordinates held; their silence told Belial that Aamon had given them nothing more than the story of a convoy worth taking, the same discipline he'd counted on when he chose this commander to deceive. Belial listened without joining in, cataloguing the confidence in each voice as one more piece of the trap doing its own work.

He kept pace at Aamon's side in silence, his face arranged into the mask of a willing collaborator anticipating glory beside a commander he owed no real deference to, while behind that mask he counted off the few remaining steps of a plan that had stopped being anyone's gambit but his own. The ledgers, the throne hall, the leaked fragments, the march itself — each had fallen where he'd set it. Somewhere ahead, past ridgelines neither of them could yet see, the Weeping Sepulcher waited for a purpose only Belial understood. Whatever the coming hours cost Aamon, Belial had long since decided it was a price he would let someone else pay.
