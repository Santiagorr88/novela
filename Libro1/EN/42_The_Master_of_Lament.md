# Chapter 42 — The Long Wager

Belial carried the spear out of the Weeping Sepulcher balanced across both palms, haft first, the way a man carries something he has not yet decided he trusts. The weight had nothing to do with iron. He had hauled heavier blades across worse ground without once breaking stride. This one settled past muscle, into whatever waited underneath muscle, and stayed there — a grief patient enough, specific enough, that it felt less like a burden than an intention: something that meant to teach him a lesson before it let him grow used to carrying it.

His legion waited where he had left them, drawn along the ridge above the portal's scar, Rovash at their head. Belial gave the order to march within the hour and told them nothing beyond the fact that the war had changed. He did not tell them the rest of it — that he had spent the climb out of the tomb deciding, and undeciding, exactly how much of that change belonged to Hell, and how much belonged to him alone.

He chose a route that ran through contested ground rather than around it, three garrisons Heaven had never fully re-manned after its early losses, sitting squarely between the tomb and Lucifer's seat. He told himself the detour served the campaign. Some smaller, less comfortable part of him understood it served a different question entirely — what a throne might look like, built by a hand that no longer needed another throne's permission to raise it.

The first garrison fell before dusk. Belial drove the spear through the captain holding its gate and felt, in the same instant the man went down weeping, the same vision that had followed him out of the tomb — an archangel of enormous grief, bent under a weight no single frame should hold. It passed as quickly as it came. What it left behind did not: sorrow flooding back through his own chest for every measure he had just spent, as though the spear meant to remind him, each time he used her, exactly whose grief he was still borrowing.

Two more fell within the week that followed, each costing him the same toll, paid slightly more efficiently than the last. He was not mastering the grief. He had simply learned how much of it he could spend in a single afternoon and still stand upright by evening — a narrower, harder-won kind of knowledge than the word mastery allowed for, though he let the word stand, uncorrected, whenever it reached him secondhand, carried back through the ranks by men who needed to believe their commander had already won whatever fight this was.

"Foras has been busy while you were in the ground," Rovash said, falling into step beside him one night, his voice pitched low enough not to carry past the two of them. "Vepar's captains have started talking to men who used to answer only to you."

"Let them talk."

"They're not just talking, my lord. They're counting."

Belial said nothing for the length of several strides. He had built his own rise from exactly this kind of arithmetic — a commander weakened just enough, a faster hand moving in to claim what the weakness left open — and he recognized it now from the other side without needing Rovash to draw him a map of it. "Let them count," he said finally. "A man who counts blades before he owns any of his own has already told you how the fight ends."

He did not say the rest of it, the part that had been building in him since the tomb: that he needed no one — not Rovash, not Foras, not Vepar's captains, not even Lucifer himself — to tell him what a weapon like this was worth to a hand willing to keep it. He could feel, some nights on the march, exactly how little it would take to turn the legion around, to let the throne room wait for a summons that never came, to build whatever came next on ground he had claimed himself rather than ground handed to him by permission.

He let the thought run its full length each time it rose, all the way to its end, because he had learned long ago that a temptation starved of its own conclusion only returned hungrier. Every time he walked it through — the legion at his back, the garrisons already falling, a grief in his own chest that answered to no one else's command — he arrived at the same unfinished sum. Power taken alone stayed alone. It answered to nothing, which meant nothing answered to it either: no throne to lean on when the grief bled him white, no army beyond the one currently bleeding with him, no name attached to what he had done that history would trouble itself separating from simple theft. He had watched enough usurpers burn fast and forgotten to know exactly what that arithmetic cost, weighed against the slower, less satisfying arithmetic of a debt owed by a king who did not forget who was owed.

By the second week he had stopped walking the thought all the way through. He still felt its pull some mornings, the same one that had followed him out of the tomb — but he had begun, without quite deciding to, treating it the way he treated any weapon he had already tested and filed: known, catalogued, set aside for a war he was not currently fighting.

The spear seemed to feel the difference before he could have named it himself. Its weight eased, fractionally, on the mornings he woke already resolved to finish the march rather than abandon it — never gone, not once, but lighter than it had been on the nights the other thought still had the run of him. He did not examine that too closely either. Some knowledge served him better left unexamined, filed the way he filed everything else that helped him survive his own ranks.

Lucifer's fortress came into sight in the third week, black towers against a sky that offered no light of its own, and Belial felt the last of the argument settle into something closer to certainty than to choice — not because the case for keeping the spear had weakened, but because he had finally finished weighing it against the other case, the one built on patience rather than appetite. A king remembers who delivered him a war-ending weapon on bent knee far longer than he remembers who merely used one well. He had learned that lesson climbing his own way up through Hell's ranks, and saw no reason it would stop applying to him now that the weapon in question happened to be his to keep or give away.

He crossed the last stretch of open ground toward the gates with the spear held loose in one hand, its grief settled now into something he could carry without flinching, and let the legion fall back at the threshold. This part of the errand, he had decided somewhere in the second week, belonged to him alone.

He found Lucifer in the throne room rather than the library, a distinction worth noting before he crossed the threshold. His king received him among a scatter of courtiers too well trained to listen openly to anything they hadn't been invited to hear, seated with the loose, unbothered elegance of someone who had never once needed to sit up straighter to be taken seriously. The hall's ordinary murmur thinned to nothing the moment Belial's shadow crossed the doorway, courtiers finding somewhere else to look, the whole room settling into a stillness built entirely around the man on the dais.

Belial knelt and set Lamentum across both palms, point turned aside, haft toward his king.

Lucifer studied the spear a long moment before he studied its bearer. "The weapon that broke a dozen garrisons in a single night," he said, without a trace of mockery, which somehow made it worse, "and its master kneels to hand it away. Tell me why."

"It answers to Hell." Belial kept his eyes on the haft rather than the man above him. "It should answer to Hell's throne."

"You spent weeks learning to survive her. Weeks I watched you master a grief no other hand in my service could have carried an hour." Lucifer rose, and crossed the short distance between them unhurried, black wings pulling what little light the hall held toward him rather than away. "Ambitious men do not usually give away the one thing that finally made them irreplaceable."

Belial said nothing. There was no answer that wouldn't have cost him more than the silence did.

Lucifer's fingers closed around the haft, just above Belial's own, and did not immediately take the weight. "Loyal ones don't, either. Which leaves me curious which of the two I'm currently watching kneel in front of me." He lifted the spear free at last. Belial felt the absence of it before he registered that his own fingers had opened — a lightness where the grief-weight had sat for weeks, gone in a single motion, and beneath the relief, something that felt uncomfortably close to loss.

"I told you once there were old songs about weapons like this." Lucifer turned the spear in his own grip, testing a weight he had never carried before. "I find I would rather hold the answer than hear it secondhand. They will not expect me on that field, and that alone is worth more than one more Lord bleeding himself dry against Heaven's Champion for a victory the histories will forget by the second age." His gaze had already drifted past Belial, toward the doors. "You found her. You broke her to your will before I ever took an interest. That will not go unremembered, whatever field she ends this war on."

Belial bowed his head, and told himself the gesture was gratitude, and did not entirely believe it. He had spent weeks learning to survive the one weapon that had made him feared by his own officers, and had just knelt, of his own choosing, to hand the whole of that mastery to the one throne even his ambition had never once considered challenging. It was obedience, offered freely and without complaint, exactly as it always had been. It cost him more than any of the weeks he'd spent bleeding for her on the march, and he left the throne room telling himself the cost had been worth it — in the same tone he would later use, on a distant ash-grey plain, to tell himself he had won.
