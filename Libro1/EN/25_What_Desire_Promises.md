# Chapter 25 — What Desire Promises

Vual had found him three days before, and in all that time had refused, with a patient discipline his rivals in Dis would have written off as idleness, to take a single step that might alert the mortal. Other hunters of his kind, eager to close the soul-ledger before the eastern front changed hands again, would have descended on the garage the very first night, trusting terror or the strength of their own marks to wrench out the pact before dawn. Vual despised that raw haste; it struck him as the failure of a craftsman who doesn't know how to appreciate the texture of the material he's working with.

He watched Corin Vasse from across the avenue, half-hidden in the grey haze of the delivery trucks; watched him in the roadside diner's booth on two separate afternoons, and followed him at a distance of twenty paces when the mechanic set out for home with his canvas coat over one shoulder. Each observation handed him a new notch for the portrait he was building in his mind. Corin walked like a man who suspects his own foundations, a subtle tension in his shoulders, his gaze fixed on the wet cobblestones as though he expected to find, in the stone, an explanation for a wound that still had no name in his own language. Vual recognized that posture; he had seen it in a hundred reincarnated souls across the ages, and knew that a seducer's first and most important work consists simply of letting the target's own uncertainty do most of the demolition in silence.

The trail had reached him through the broken channels of the eastern front, that constant murmur of informants and deserters who traded in the leftovers of Miguel's great campaign. A soul lost in the neutral plane's purge — a commander named Krass, according to the legions' official record — had slipped back to Earth in the narrow margin the Cycle allowed for violent deaths. Vual had felt no particular pity for Krass's end; the mystical slaughter at Zaphor'el and the sterility of the purified ground were matters for the generals, trivialities of a war that interested him only for the flesh it left ownerless.

What had drawn him to Krass was the cleanness of his fall. Solmire had cut him down mid-way through a desperate stand, stripping him of life with an absolute ferocity that gave his loyalty to Hell no time to wither through the ordinary erosion of the seasons. A soul truncated that way, with a commander's grief and his affection for his soldiers still warm beneath the mail, tended to carry the wound into its next life without ever understanding what was hurting it in the middle of its chest. Corin Vasse did not remember the crystal groves, or the worn bow, or the boy who had fallen into the mud before his boots. But the emptiness of that loss was still there, a wound of brine and old copper that made him vulnerable to any hand offering to clean it, and Vual meant to be that hand before Heaven's hunter finished orienting his own spear in this direction.

He crossed the garage lot on the fourth day, choosing the stretch of mid-afternoon when Navarion's light was beginning to turn the color of dirty sackcloth and the bay's work slowed on its own. Corin was alone behind the cracked wooden counter, a rag soaked in transmission grease in his hand, rubbing, over and over, at a piece of bronze that was already clean. The air in the room sat stagnant, thick with the sour smell of kerosene, cold metal, and the damp sawdust Corin used to soak up oil spills.

Vual walked in without hurrying his step, taking on the bearing of a stranger with an engine problem and the patience of a man with nowhere better to spend the afternoon. He leaned against the counter with an ease that made the wood groan under his weight.

"You're the one they say really listens to what a car's telling him," Vual said, and his smile carried just enough warmth not to raise suspicion too soon.

Corin looked up slowly, with that reflexive caution of men who spend their day measuring tolerances in tenths of a millimeter. He studied Vual's face, the fine tailoring that didn't match the mud of the outskirts, and didn't set down the grease rag.

"Depends who's asking," Corin answered, and his voice, rough from the kerosene, showed no hurry at all to please.

Vual didn't waste the opening on ordinary talk of cylinders and heads, though he let Corin believe for three minutes that the engine business was the only reason they were sitting at the same counter. He asked three easy questions about the sedan repair, listening for the subtle hesitation in the mechanic's voice, the way he avoided saying his own name, and how his gaze drifted toward the corner every time the conversation brushed against his past.

"You strike me as someone who notices things other people miss," Vual said, sliding the line out with the smoothness of a lubricant. "The kind of person who catches a fault in the vibration before the engine even starts complaining."

Corin let out a small laugh, a dry, uncertain sound that betrayed, beneath the grease on his nails, the first crack in his defenses.

"I guess," he said, folding the rag over again. "Or people just don't bother listening carefully."

Vual let the silence settle over the counter, a dense, mineral weight Corin didn't know how to move, before leaning his body one step closer.

"People at Halloran's are still talking about Tuesday night, from what I hear," Vual said, and the name of the bar made Corin's fingers freeze against the bronze. "Something about a whole room going quiet all at once, and nobody able to point to a single word that explained it."

"It wasn't anything," Corin said, too fast for the care he usually put into his answers. "I don't even remember doing much of anything."

"Something doesn't fit, does it," Vual pressed, his voice softening. "Like you're wearing a life that isn't quite yours."

Corin's jaw tightened, a defensive rigidity wiping the mechanic's ease from his face.

"How would you know that?" he asked, and the question was no longer an evasion; it was the confession of a man discovering himself under siege in his own home.

"Because I have the other half of the answer," Vual said, with a calm that left no room for doubt. "You only have to want to hear it."

He offered certainty, not power. Vual knew that raw ambition was a currency lesser men spent too fast, but a soul that had spent centuries reincarnating without memory didn't want Hell's glory; it wanted, with a castaway's hunger, solid ground to plant its own name on. He didn't push further that evening. He let the offer rest on the kerosene-stained counter, letting Corin's own doubt do the work of corroding his composure.

"I don't need you to answer now," Vual said, his tone softening to the edge of mercy. "Most people don't know what to do with the whole truth handed to them at once. It's easier carried a little at a time."

"And if I don't want to carry it at all?" Corin asked, and it was the closest thing to defiance his human pride had managed all evening.

Vual smiled, and on the back of his glove, invisible to the mechanic's mortal eye, Deseum's mystic seams answered the motion with a cold hiss. A single thread of pure longing, thin as a hair and dense as lead, broke free of the invisible chain and crossed the counter, coiling with a noose's precision around the wound in his chest.

"So you're still asking yourself," Vual said, his voice carrying a patience that made no room for hurry. "Same as you have been all week. I'm not in any rush, Corin. Are you?"

Corin shifted where he stood, an involuntary, clumsy motion, his left hand rising on its own to his chest, fingers pressing his sternum as though some cold insect had just woken beneath his skin. Vual watched the gesture with a mute satisfaction, recognizing the thread's hold. He straightened from the counter without adding another word, picked up his coat, and left the bay with the slow gait of a man who knows the net is already cast, and that the fish, no matter how long it swims the pond, already belongs to the fisherman.
