# Chapter 16 — The First Threshold

Belial stepped through the portal and felt the battlefield behind him vanish, replaced in a single stride by a silence so complete it pressed against his ears like a physical weight. The Weeping Sepulcher received him without ceremony, without light — indifferent to his arrival as it would be to his departure.

The ground beneath his boots was black crystal, jagged and uneven, and it chimed faintly with every step — a sound too close to weeping for Belial's taste, thin and wavering, without source. The air was thick with the taste of salt and something colder than salt, something he had no word for yet. Mourning hung over the whole plane like a mist that never lifted, ancient and undirected, grief with no single grave left to mark.

He let his eyes adjust to what passed for light here — a low, colorless glow that seemed to rise from the crystal itself, throwing shadows that didn't quite match where a real light source would have cast them. No wind stirred the air, and yet it moved anyway, in slow currents that carried the salt-taste past him and away. He had crossed strange places before, in centuries of service that had taken him through planes no sane soul would visit twice. None of them had felt this deliberately unwelcoming.

He had not gone twenty paces before something reached him — not a sound, not a sight, but an ache that bloomed behind his ribs, sharp enough that he nearly mistook it for a wound. It came without warning, the way a fever comes, and left just as suddenly.

For one disorienting moment it felt like the tomb itself calling to him across the distance still separating them, a signal of suffering so concentrated that surely nothing else on this plane could produce it. Belial straightened, steadied himself against the ache, and turned toward its source with the same confidence that had carried him through every calculated risk of the last several weeks.

He had learned, across centuries hunting things other demons considered too dangerous to bother with, that the loudest signal in unfamiliar territory was usually the one worth following first. He quickened his pace toward it, his mace loose and ready at his hip, certain that confidence alone would carry him the rest of the way. The ache pulsed once more, sharper, and he found himself almost grateful for it — a landmark, at last, in a landscape that had offered him none.

The crystal path narrowed as he pushed forward, forcing his stride short and careful. When he tried to force his old pace back onto it anyway, the ground answered with a warning of its own.

A jagged edge caught the side of his boot and opened a shallow cut through the leather. A step later his heel came down wrong on ground that gave beneath him just enough to nearly cost him his balance. A third misstep sent crystal shards skittering away with a sound uncomfortably close to a gasp. Belial cursed under his breath and pressed on without slowing, dismissing all three as nothing more than the ordinary hazard of unfamiliar terrain.

He did not stop to consider that the ground might be answering him in the only language it had. Listening to ground was not a skill his long service had ever required — only walking across it. If anything, the small resistance only sharpened his impatience.

The crystal opened into a wider clearing, and something rose to meet him there — a shape built from the same black, weeping crystal as the ground, vaguely humanoid, vaster than any single grief should have been able to hold together. It gathered itself out of the floor the way a tide gathers before it breaks.

It did not attack. It raised nothing resembling a weapon, only stood at the clearing's center with a stillness that felt, despite its size, entirely without menace. Belial had faced enough guardians across enough conquered thresholds to know the posture of one weighing an attack, and this thing carried none of it. When it spoke, the voice did not belong to one throat but to a thousand, fused into a single slow cadence that seemed to come from everywhere at once.

"Nothing is taken here," it said. "Only given."

Belial had not expected the thing he was about to fight to have a voice at all. For one brief instant he stood there, caught off guard by the sound of it more than by anything it said.

"Then give it to me," he answered, contempt hardening back into his voice.

The guardian did not answer with any further words. It answered with the first blow.

The strike came faster than its size should have allowed, a crystalline fist connecting with Belial's shoulder hard enough to send him staggering back several paces. Pain bloomed hot through the joint, and beneath the pain, stranger still, a fleeting wash of sorrow that had nothing to do with the blow itself — as if the impact had carried some fragment of the guardian's own nature into him. He shook it off before it could root any deeper.

He came up swinging, his mace meeting the guardian's next blow with a crack that should have shattered crystal and instead only sent a shudder of pain up his own arm, absorbed rather than returned. He circled left, testing for an opening that never materialized, and struck low, aiming for what might have been a knee on a body built along more ordinary lines. The blow landed exactly where he'd aimed it. The guardian did not so much as shift its weight, the crystal chiming softly with the effort of a strike he had just wasted.

He struck again, harder, and found nothing on the other end but the same steady, absorbing resistance. Belial's frustration sharpened with every failed exchange, each swing faster and less measured than the one before, the kind of escalation that had won him a hundred fights against opponents who eventually cracked. This one did not crack. It simply waited, and waited again, its crystalline form registering each new impact with nothing more than a faint chime. A third strike from the guardian caught him across the ribs and put him on the ground, breath driven out of him, the mace skidding away out of reach. This time the sorrow did not stay fleeting. It poured into him with the blow itself, undeniable and total — the weight of a hand opening again and again to let something irreplaceable go, without ever being allowed to close back around it. He had no name for what he was feeling, and no way to shove it back out — it had come in through the wound itself and settled somewhere he couldn't reach. He lay there with his ears ringing and the taste of salt and blood mixing on his tongue, the crystal cold against his cheek. From this angle the guardian looked less like an opponent and more like a fixture of the landscape, unmoved, waiting for him to decide what came next. He was not dying. He understood that much even through the pain. This was correction, not execution.

The guardian did not press its advantage. It withdrew, retreating toward the clearing's center at the same slow pace it had approached with, settling back into a stance no more aggressive than the one it had first risen from. As it went, it repeated the words it had opened with, carrying no triumph, no cruelty — only a patience that felt older than Belial's own rage.

"Nothing is taken here."

For the first time since he'd crossed the portal, Belial had no answer ready. He lay there watching it recede, and understood its withdrawal did not look like mercy either — only the end of a lesson apparently deemed sufficient.

He lay there a moment longer than his pride would have liked, chest heaving, one hand pressed to ribs that throbbed with every breath, and let the fury build unchecked — there was, for once, no audience left to perform composure for. The crystal chimed faintly around him with every ragged breath, the plane's endless mourning indifferent to whatever indignity had just been visited on him.

He had come into this place expecting what every other plane he'd conquered had eventually given him — resistance that broke, sooner or later, under enough force in the right place. He turned the fight over once more, searching for the moment a different strike might have changed the outcome, and found none. Belial pulled himself upright, wincing at the pull of bruised ribs, and crossed to where the mace had skidded, its head still faintly warm. He weighed it once in his hand and stood breathing hard in the ringing silence the guardian had left behind. Brute force would win him nothing further on this ground. Whatever this place demanded of him, he would have to learn its terms before it gave up anything at all — and for a man who had spent centuries taking whatever he wanted by exactly the method that had just failed him, that understanding felt like its own kind of defeat. He looked back once toward the clearing, where the guardian had already gone still, its outline blurring back into the crystal as though it had never risen at all. Then he turned deeper into the plane, toward wherever the ache in his chest still pointed. He would find another way in. He always had.
