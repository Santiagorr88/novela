# Chapter 53 — Sparks for the Numb

The wounded who could bleed had somewhere to go. The Temple of Shattered Light, the Bastion's central sanctuary, had wards for that — cots in orderly rows, Raphael's apprentices moving between them with the brisk competence of people who trusted their own hands, bandages changed on schedule, fevers charted and answered. Anael's wounded had nowhere so simple. They lay in the same rows, breathing, hearts steady, every visible measure of them intact, and none of it meant anything at all. A physician could close a wound. No one had yet taught a physician what to do with a soldier whose eyes tracked nothing, whose hands took food only when someone else's hand guided the spoon, whose name, spoken directly into his face, produced no more reaction than the sound of rain against a window he had already stopped believing was his to open.

She passed the first cot of the morning without stopping — a young woman who had not moved in nine days, breath so shallow it barely disturbed the blanket, and whom Anael had learned, at some cost, to visit last rather than first, because grief that deep answered nothing offered too early in the day, when Anael's own reserve of patience had not yet had time to fill back up. She knelt instead beside the third cot in the east gallery, where a soldier lay with his eyes open and fixed on a point in the ceiling that held nothing anyone else could see. She did not lift the Lyre of Light yet. She never did, not first. She listened instead — to the ragged, uneven catch of his breathing, the small hitch before each exhale, the rhythm of a body that seemed, to her ear, to have forgotten it was allowed to relax. Hope offered too early sounded like a lie to a mind that had already decided the war was over and lost. She had learned that lesson on soldiers who flinched from her first chord, recoiling from brightness the way a burned hand recoils from a second flame.

Only once his breath had steadied enough to answer hers, twice, three times, did she let the first note rise to meet it — not brighter than what he already had, only steadier, a single sustained tone matched so closely to his own breathing that it might have been mistaken for an echo of it rather than a gift laid on top. His eyes did not move. His breathing did not change. She held the note a while longer anyway, because the temple had taught her, this past fortnight, that absence of response was not the same thing as absence of hearing, and let the silence after the note own its own small failure before she rose, her knees stiff from the stone floor, and crossed to the next cot.

No one had trained her for this particular war. Her whole long service had been built around battle lines — walking the ranks before an engagement, letting courage catch from soldier to soldier the way a struck flint catches dry grass, until an entire front stood a little taller than fear alone would have allowed. That work had an end to it, visible and countable: a line held, a charge that didn't break. This work had no such edge to measure against. It simply continued, cot after cot, gallery after gallery, and she had learned to stop asking it to resolve into anything cleaner than that.

---

Barachiel found her between the fourth row and the fifth, a scroll of the day's rosters half-unrolled against his forearm, and she heard his usual eloquence dulled this morning, worn thinner than she'd heard it in three weeks. Even tired, he still moved through the gallery the way she'd always read him — like a man accustomed to being listened to, though this morning he looked worn thin enough to trade that gift for an hour of someone else carrying the news instead.

"They've reassigned the wards again," he said, without preamble, because preamble had become a luxury none of them could afford anymore. "Raphael's people can't keep both lists — the ones who are bleeding and the ones who aren't answering. Command wants the second list handed entirely to you." He held the scroll out, not quite meeting her eyes, the way a man offers something she read as suspecting was unkind to give. "Forty-one names on it as of this morning. They're calling it an honor. I don't think anyone actually believes that, least of all the clerk who wrote it down."

Anael took the scroll and did not unroll it. She already knew, without reading it, roughly how long it would be — she had watched the number climb by twos and threes every week since the horn sounded, and had stopped expecting it to shrink. "It isn't an honor," she said. "It's just true. Nothing Raphael's hands can mend reaches these ones. Mine might. That's not the same as an honor, Barachiel. That's just the only shape the work can take."

"I know." He hesitated, and for a moment something of his old persuasive warmth surfaced, aimed at her instead of at a crowd, the tone she imagined he once used on soldiers who needed convincing rather than commanding. "I only meant — I'm sorry it's yours alone to carry now. Forty-one is a great deal to carry with one instrument and two hands."

"It was never going to be anyone else's." She rolled the scroll closed and tucked it against her side without breaking stride toward the next row of cots, and let him go back to his own long list of prayers murmured toward squads too far away to hear them, because neither of them had time to stand there comforting the other about a decision already made.

---

She found Ezihel in the narrow supply alcove behind the west gallery, sitting on an overturned crate with the small bronze bell of Lumen resting silent in both hands, not ringing it, not putting it away either, turning it slowly as though the weight of it might tell her something her own thoughts hadn't.

"You've been out here longer than a break allows," Anael said, and sat down across from her on a second crate, close enough that their knees nearly touched.

Ezihel didn't look up right away. When she did, her voice came out level, controlled — the steadiness Anael had come to read as Ezihel's own, held in place by something costing her more than usual today. "Lumen isn't reaching the worst of them. I ring it, and I watch their faces, and nothing moves. I've started to wonder if I'm doing it for them at all anymore, or just because stopping would mean admitting it doesn't work."

"What would it mean, if it didn't work?"

"That some of them are already gone. That I'm ringing a bell over an empty room and calling it care." Ezihel's grip on Lumen tightened, her knuckles pale around the small handle. "You've had weeks longer at this than I have. Tell me honestly — does it ever stop feeling like that?"

Anael let the question sit a moment before she answered it, because Ezihel deserved better than a comfort assembled too quickly to be true. "No. It doesn't. I still walk out of some rooms wondering the same thing you're wondering right now." She reached out and closed her own hand briefly over the bell, stilling it further, though it had not been moving. "But it was never going to cure them, Ezihel. Neither is my Lyre. That was never what either of us was sent in there to do. The work was never 'wake them.' It was 'make sure none of them go through this alone, whether they wake or not.' If that's all Lumen has done today, it hasn't failed. It's done the one thing it was ever built to do."

Ezihel's jaw tightened, and for a moment Anael thought she might argue it. Instead she nodded, once, and turned the bell over in her hands a final time, and said nothing further, which was its own kind of answer.

"Get some water before you go back in," Anael added, rising from the crate. "You'll ring it steadier on a full throat than an empty one." It was a small thing to say, ordinary enough to sound almost careless, but she meant it as precisely as she meant anything she offered a soldier in the east gallery — a fact offered lightly enough to be received without the weight of being comforted at all.

---

She went back to the soldier in the east gallery near the end of her rounds, more from habit than from any real expectation. She matched his breath again, the way she had a dozen times already that day, and let the note rise, and for a long moment nothing in him moved at all, the way nothing had moved every other time.

Then his fingers curled, slowly, around the edge of the blanket pulled to his chest — not toward her, not toward anything she could name, just a small, deliberate closing of a hand that seemed, for that one instant, to remember it still belonged to him. A sound came out of him after it, low and broken, not quite a word, more the shape a word leaves behind when the person saying it has lost most of what it used to mean. She did not ask him to repeat it, and she did not reach for the meaning of it either, certain that chasing it would only frighten whatever fragile thing had just surfaced back under again. She simply held the note a while longer, and let her own hand rest, light and unhurried, over his curled one, and allowed herself, for the length of that single sustained chord, to call it the first real thing he had done since the retreat.

---

He was still by morning. Not the stillness of the fingers closing — the old stillness, the one from before, eyes fixed again on the same unmarked point on the ceiling, breath gone back to its ragged, uneven catch as though the night before had never happened at all. Anael knelt beside him a long while without lifting the Lyre, without trying the note again immediately, letting the disappointment weigh exactly what it weighed rather than dressing it up as either tragedy or nothing.

He had not gotten worse. He simply had not stayed. She made herself hold both of those facts at once, refusing the easier version of either — refusing to call last night meaningless because it hadn't lasted, and refusing to call this morning a defeat large enough to undo it. Some sparks caught. Most, she was learning, did not hold past a single dawn, and no amount of wanting otherwise had yet changed that math by even one name on Barachiel's list. That was the actual shape of the work he had handed her, stripped of whatever kindness she'd heard him trying to wrap around it. She rose eventually, because forty other cots still waited on the day, and left him with the blanket still faintly disturbed where his hand had once, briefly, remembered it was his.

---

She found Ezihel again well past dark, in the same alcove, the bell in her lap this time rather than her hands, her shoulders carrying an exhaustion Anael recognized without needing to ask — another long night spent watching faces that did not change. Anael didn't ask what had happened on her rounds. She could read it well enough in the way Ezihel hadn't looked up when she came in, and in the way the alcove's single lamp had been left to gutter low rather than trimmed.

She sat down beside her instead, closer than before, the Lyre resting silent across both their knees, and said nothing for a while, because Ezihel had already been given enough words for one day and none of them had helped as much as this might.

"It doesn't get easier," Anael said finally, quiet, not a question. "I'm not going to tell you it does. But you keep coming back tomorrow anyway. That's the whole of it, Ezihel. Not the results. Just that." She let her shoulder rest against Ezihel's, a small and wordless weight. "I'll be here again in the morning too. So will you, if I know you at all."

Ezihel exhaled, long and unsteady, the first sound all evening Anael hadn't heard her holding carefully in place, and let her head tip, briefly, against Anael's shoulder.

---

Dawn found Anael already moving down the first row of the east gallery, the Lyre of Light warm against her palm from a night that had given her exactly one closed fist and one lost murmur to carry forward into the next, against forty other names that had given her nothing at all. She did not know, starting the round again, which of the soldiers ahead of her would answer and which would not. She had stopped expecting to know that in advance weeks ago, the same week she had stopped expecting the list to grow shorter.

She knelt beside the first cot, listened for the broken rhythm of a stranger's breath, and waited for it to steady enough to answer hers before she let the first note rise — small, unhurried, offered without any promise attached to it, into hands still too numb, most mornings, to ask for anything at all.
