# Prologue — What the Tree Kept

Serephis had been a kingdom. Then it had been a war, and then it had been this.

The sand had fused into sheets of black glass that nothing walked on. The sky had gone the color of a bruise and stayed that way, stalled over the dunes. Heat rose out of the ground and went nowhere, because there was nothing left standing to stop it. Under the dunes, in straight lines that the wind never quite managed to bury, ran the roads. Cut stone, square edges. Someone had paved this place once, and had expected to keep using it.

At the center of the dead ground there was a grove, and the grove was not dead.

Its trees rose in perfect ranks, each leaf a thin blade of pale metal, and the light between them fell so evenly that nothing in the grove had ever cast a shadow. No wind reached it. No sand crossed its first row. A figure stood among the trunks, as tall as they were and made of the same pale stuff, with a smooth surface where a face should have been. It did not walk. It did not sleep. When something moved at the edge of the grove, it turned, without a sound, to face it, and then it went on standing.

Nothing moved at the edge of the grove.

At the heart of the ranks stood the great ash. Its bark was black and rough as cooled lava, and its roots drank from a pool that held a light without a source, a light that did not ripple and did not fade. Driven into the trunk, up to the guard, through the heart of the tree, was a sword.

It was not metal. It was light that had decided, once, to have weight and an edge, and had not changed its mind since. It did not glow the way a lamp glows. It simply was brighter than anything around it, the way a wound is more present than the skin.

The tree had not been asked to hold it. Nobody had brought it there in ceremony, or hidden it there in fear, or left instructions. Whatever had thrown it across the dark, a long time ago, on the day three sounds rang together and the world learned what breaking meant, had thrown it without aiming. It had landed. The tree had closed around it. And the tree had kept it, through however many ages, the way a stone keeps its weight: without complaint, without witness, without wanting anything back.

Empires rose somewhere past the horizon and did not come this way. Wars were declared, and won, and forgotten, and none of them crossed the black glass. Heaven counted its victories. Hell counted its debts. The grove did not count. The figure among the trunks turned to face whatever moved, and nothing moved, and it turned back.

The pool kept its light. The ash kept its sword. The sword kept its silence, which was not the silence of something dead, but of something that had not yet been spoken to.

It had never been told to wait for anyone in particular. It was waiting for whoever came first.

Somewhere beyond the last rank of trees, out on the glass, a sheet of it broke with the dry sound of bones snapping under a sabaton. Then another. Then, after a pause that was not hesitation but exhaustion, a third.

The figure turned toward the sound.
