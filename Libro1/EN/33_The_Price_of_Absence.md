# Chapter 33 — The Price of Absence

Malthus felt the shift in Hell's court within days of Belial's return with Lament, long before anyone in Foras's own household dared to say the word weakness aloud, and long before Foras himself seemed to notice, or at least to acknowledge, that anything in the court's temperature had changed at all. It arrived the way these things always arrived among their kind, not as open challenge but as a subtle recalculation in how others held themselves in his lord's presence — a fraction less deference in a bow, a fraction more confidence in the messengers who once would have waited to be summoned rather than appearing unbidden. Malthus had served Foras loyally through centuries of courts no calmer than this one, and loyalty, in his considered experience, was no excuse for blindness to what it was meant to protect against.

Foras remained, in every practical sense, the commander he had always been. The court's perception of him was a separate matter, and perception, left unattended, had a way of becoming its own kind of reality regardless of the truth underneath it. Malthus had watched courts turn on far less than a single humbling defeat — not because a lord had failed his household, but because enough individual demons had each independently calculated that the safer wager lay elsewhere. He did not intend to let that arithmetic run its course unchallenged.

He walked the length of his own chambers with the problem, the familiar cold stone underfoot doing nothing to settle the low, persistent unease that had followed him since the first whispers reached his ears, and settled at last on the conclusion he generally reached whenever a threat proved too abstract to strike at directly: that concrete action, however small, would tell him more than continued watching ever could.

He summoned Corvin, one of his own subordinates, to the ritual chamber that evening, the young demon's nerves plain in the careful way he held himself at the circle's edge. The chamber smelled of old ash and older blood, the accumulated residue of centuries of bindings sealed into stone that no amount of time had ever quite scrubbed clean, and Malthus traced the pact-circle without hurrying, chalk and blood and ash arranging themselves into the pattern Maleth demanded of him.

Malthus traced the circle without pause. "Ash doesn't go back to being wood. And blood, once it's spilled, doesn't choose who it serves. Think on that while you can still choose yourself." Corvin's voice, when he answered, carried only the thinnest thread of steadiness. "I'd rather owe you a handful of ash than burn whole with whoever comes after." Malthus nodded, satisfied enough by the answer that he saw no need to explain the parable further.

He drew the blade across his own palm and let three drops fall onto the ash Corvin had scattered at the circle's center, watching the dark liquid spread and catch until the two substances bound into something neither one alone. The sigils ringing the circle flared once, low and steady, and settled into a faint, permanent glow. Corvin's breathing, ragged through the whole of it, eased only once the glow had fully settled.

"You could have waited," Malthus said, not unkindly, once the ritual's last traces had cooled. "Seen which way the court actually turned before committing yourself." Corvin shook his head. "Waiting is how everyone loses, in the end. I'd rather bind myself to a captain I trust now than gamble on guessing right later." Malthus said nothing further to that, though he noted the answer as the kind of clear-eyed pragmatism he valued in the demons who served under him. "Then we understand each other." He extended a hand to help the younger demon to his feet. "Ash given willingly to the fire burns clean."

The first real sign came three days later, delivered in the kind of silence Malthus had learned, across centuries in Hell's court, to read as louder than it appeared. A minor trade route through Foras's territory had gone quiet — no formal inquiry, no messenger demanding explanation, only the absence of the usual traffic that should have passed through it by now. Silence of this specific shape meant exactly one thing in Hell's court: someone was testing the edges of a territory they intended to eventually claim, carefully enough to leave no accusation behind if the testing came to nothing.

Malthus traced the disturbance back through his own informants over the following day. A merchant who traded regularly through the disrupted route reported an unfamiliar face asking careful, idle questions about shipment schedules three days before the traffic stopped. A minor functionary at the territory's edge mentioned a stranger who had spent an unusual amount of time studying the boundary markers without ever crossing them. Neither detail alone would have told him much. Together, laid alongside the silence itself, they painted the outline of someone conducting a careful, deliberate survey rather than any opportunistic scavenging.

He found the confirmation he needed the following evening, examining a discarded ward-seal left behind at the edge of the disrupted trade route — the kind of small carelessness even a careful operative occasionally left in their wake. The seal's construction, the specific arcane signature woven through it, matched nothing Malthus had encountered from any of Foras's other rivals across his long service. He recognized it immediately for what it was: the unmistakable craft of Vepar's own household, the same cold, methodical precision that ran through everything Vepar's people touched.

Malthus held the fragment a long moment, weighing what its presence confirmed. Vepar's own household was sounding Foras's territory directly, not some minor opportunist testing weak borders on its own initiative — a distinction that weighed heavier than the disruption itself. He had crossed paths with Vepar's household more than once over the centuries, always at some careful remove, and had come away from each encounter with the same settled impression: a mind that calculated advantage the way water calculated its own path downhill, patient and inevitable, never in any hurry because it never doubted it would eventually arrive.

He returned to his own chambers that night carrying the ward-seal fragment with him, setting it on the table where he did his clearest thinking, and sat with the decision a long while before finally allowing himself to settle on it. Foras remained, whatever the court's current perception suggested, the commander Malthus had sworn his loyalty to long before tonight's small, patient work became necessary. That loyalty demanded more of him now than quiet vigilance.

He would not bring this to Foras yet. His lord carried enough already, weighing the court's shifting loyalties against the larger war Hell was still fighting against Heaven, and Malthus saw no benefit in adding an unconfirmed suspicion to that burden before he had something concrete enough to act on. Better to handle the opening moves of this quietly himself, the way he had handled every threat to Foras's household across the long centuries of his own service, and bring his lord only the finished picture once it was worth his attention.

He set the ward-seal fragment carefully into the small case he kept for evidence of this weight, sealed it, and allowed himself, for the first time since the whispers first reached him, something close to settled resolve. Vepar's household had made its opening move. Malthus intended to make certain it was also its last uncontested one.

Stolas found Vepar where anyone who knew him would have looked first, standing at the edge of a still black pool deep within his own domain. The water here never moved on its own — no current, no tide, only the faint reek of brine gone stale in stone that no ocean would ever reach again to renew it, a smell Stolas had long since stopped noticing except on days like this one, when Vepar's own mood made everything about the chamber feel sharper than usual. A single dark thread curled through the pool as Stolas approached, unmistakably blood, though he could see no wound anywhere near the water's edge to account for it — some trace Vepar's own dominion had drawn here from a distant battlefield.

"Foras is weak," Vepar said, without preamble, his voice carrying that same resonant quality of something spoken from very deep water even here, standing on dry stone. "And weakness is only another fluid, waiting to find its level." He gestured once at the thread of blood. "Go and learn exactly how far the tide has gone out."

Stolas considered the instruction a moment. "Am I looking for anything specific," he asked, "or just whatever happens to be floating?" Vepar's mouth curved, faint and slow. "Everything floats, eventually. You only need to collect it before anyone else does."

Stolas withdrew to his own working chambers within the hour, Umbra Manus already open across his desk, its pages a deep black that seemed to swallow rather than reflect whatever light happened to fall across the room. He had never especially enjoyed fieldwork, the crude business of moving bodies through physical space to gather information that could, with sufficient patience, simply be extracted from the fabric of the world itself. He began with the oldest bindings first, incantations that reached backward through recorded time rather than forward through space, unearthing the layered history of Foras's holdings one careful excavation at a time.

The excavation finally yielded something he trusted: not a vague rumor, but a name, sealed beneath a binding-ward he recognized immediately as Malthus's own work — a false genealogy layered over the true record, designed to make a careless reader believe the pact had lapsed decades ago when in fact it had not. Unpicking it cost him three further incantations and a correspondingly steeper toll, though the name beneath it more than justified the expense: Threll, a minor Squad Leader within Foras's household, bound to him by pact rather than by any deeper loyalty, who had apparently begun quietly maintaining contact with an emissary from a rival household entirely unconnected to Vepar. The pact itself, sworn to secure a grain shipment through contested territory two decades past, had never formally been dissolved — a loose thread Foras's court believed Malthus's ward had closed for good.

What struck Stolas most was not the pact itself but the silence surrounding it. No record anywhere in Foras's own household suggested anyone had reviewed the arrangement in years, possibly decades. A household this large, this old, inevitably accumulated forgotten obligations of this precise shape. The question, as always in Stolas's line of work, was not whether such debts existed. It was whether anyone still knew where to find them.

"Foras's people," he murmured to the empty chamber, more for his own amusement than any audience, "still think secrets stay buried simply because no one's dug recently." He felt the toll the working had taken only once he finally set the grimoire aside — a familiar, hollow weariness, not physical exhaustion exactly, but something closer to a small, permanent withdrawal from whatever span of years he still had left to spend.

He drafted the report carefully, cross-checking each claim against the record twice before committing it to the page, unwilling to overstate a single Squad Leader's wavering loyalty as something larger than it currently was. In Stolas's experience, Vepar valued precision over enthusiasm every time.

Vepar received the finding the same way he received everything — without visible surprise, though something in the stillness of the water beside him shifted subtly at the detail Stolas offered, a ripple with no obvious cause. "A wavering loyalty," he said finally, turning the information over with the same patience he brought to any calculation. "Small. But small cracks widen, given the right pressure in the right place."

"There will be more," Stolas said. "Foras's household has not been audited this thoroughly in longer than most of his current Captains have served him. I doubt this is the only loyalty that's drifted since." Vepar's translucent features shifted into something that might, on a less unsettling face, have passed for satisfaction. "Then keep pulling the thread. Foras's tide has gone out further than even he knows. I intend to see exactly how far before the water comes back in."

"And if Malthus notices someone's been digging?" Stolas asked, more out of professional thoroughness than any real concern. Vepar's expression did not change. "Then he'll know what every commander eventually learns — that the tide doesn't announce itself before it turns. It simply turns, and by the time anyone notices, the shore already looks different." He turned back toward the still black water, the pool's stale brine settling heavier in the air now that the report was finished, and Stolas took the gesture as the conversation's end. "Bring me the next thread when you have it."

Stolas inclined his head and withdrew, already weighing which buried record he intended to excavate next. He had not expected this assignment to yield anything beyond a single minor confirmation of what Vepar's order had suggested he already suspected. Finding an actual name, an actual thread worth pulling, had exceeded even his own considerable confidence in his method. He intended to make the most of that unexpected fortune, one careful excavation at a time, for as long as Foras's household continued providing this much material to work with.
