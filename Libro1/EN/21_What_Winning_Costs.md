# Chapter 21 — What Winning Costs

Lamentum hung at Belial's hip in a way that shared nothing of the honest, straightforward heaviness his old mace had; it was a cold weight measured not in pounds of iron but in the intensity of what he had felt closing his fingers around the metal. He crossed the last stretch of the plane toward the portal, feeling the spear's low, steady thrum settled behind his ribs like a frigid tenant he had not invited into his house and that, for the first time in his long career, he did not know how to order to leave. Every few dozen steps the dark metal let out a brighter pulse, a pale flash that did not originate in his own will and that forced him to tense his side against the mist of a vision that threatened to unfold again in his mind.

The black crystal beneath his boots grew scarcer and duller as he neared the exit, as though the plane itself had lost all interest in his presence now that the tomb's grey slab lay clean and empty behind him. The clearing where the Crystal Guardian had broken him was already far behind, hidden by the low haze of brine and by the folds of a channel that refused to return the echo of his footsteps. Belial walked on with the Dubium mace still strapped to his other hip, useless and cold since the stone's first lesson, but he refused to shed it, out of that old, stubborn soldier's habit of carrying one metal too many rather than admit it had stopped serving him.

He understood, with every breath that cost him a physical effort in the dense air, that he was not carrying a clean victory out of that realm. He had taken the spear through the pure violence of his will, yes, but the price of that taking was a sediment of grief now settling beneath his iron breastplate for good, a sorrow he refused to accept but also could not fully evict from his thoughts. It rose in him at odd moments, stripped of ceremony, cutting through some ordinary calculation about sulfur supply lines or vanguard rotations, then fading again just as quickly as it had claimed its share of air. It was not defeat, he told himself firmly as the twilight portal widened before his eyes. But it did not feel like triumph either; it was closer to wearing a second suit of armor that could not be removed, a frigid metal sewn to his own skin that remembered the executioner's grief long after the wound had stopped bleeding.

The instant his fingers had closed fully around the shaft in the crypt, a dull vibration had left the Sepulcher's plane, a resonance too large to stay contained within the moment that had produced it. That mystical disturbance was already taking a visible, physical shape on the coasts of Earth, a tempest gathering where no ordinary weather should have had the right to form.

---

The lighthouse keeper had spent thirty years weathering the coast's storms from her cold rocky point, and she knew the temper of every wind that licked the brick of her tower with the familiarity of someone who has learned to count the steps in the dark. She climbed to the lamp room the moment the first clouds, a filthy violet color, crested the horizon, and arrived just in time to watch the whole sky twist and tear itself apart within minutes, sparing her the hours any storm of that size should have needed to build.

The wind reached her from three distinct directions at once, a physical impossibility that made the waves break against the cliff's stones in rhythms that contradicted each other, colliding head-on in crests of foam that fell with no discernible order. She kept the light burning, forcing her hands to grip the mechanism's metal with the firmness her mother had taught her for nights when the air groaned like old wood about to split. She wiped the glass clean twice, shivering under her damp coat, thinking of the tremor that had shaken the coast some nights before — the same one the harbor fisherman swore had nearly thrown his son overboard — and felt the certainty that both disasters shared the same discordant cause. She stayed at her post until dawn, watching the water slowly settle back into its course without offering her a single explanation, her logbook open on the table and her hand still too unsteady to trace a single line on the damp paper.

---

At the Bastion, Iofiel stood beside Gabriel in the small archive room, letting the silence of the tall shelves settle between them with the weight of a wait that had worn thin days before. More nights had passed since the Council authorized the lone scout's quiet departure than Iofiel had allotted for a simple route confirmation, and the rigidity in Gabriel's shoulders was proof that he too had stopped believing in the comfort of ordinary delay.

The scout who finally crossed the threshold wore the weathered, sour look of long marches; the desert's grey dust and brine's white crust clung to the dented bronze of armor that had clearly not come off in many days. Their report was brief, flat, and stripped of any ornament that might soften the blow of it: they had located the black stone coast, had crossed the portal, but the tomb at the center of the crystal chamber stood entirely empty. No guardian to fight, no spear to record; only a slab of grey stone, featureless and cold, bearing no inscription to say who had been there before them.

"Someone got there first," the scout said, their voice dry from disuse, "and took everything without leaving a trace we could put a name to."

Iofiel felt a cold stab in her chest, the exact confirmation of the suspicion she had tried to bury beneath her translation notes weeks before. She dismissed the scout with a tired wave of her hand, thanking them for a service that no longer had the shape of a victory, and turned toward Gabriel, whose face remained in the window's shadow, still as altar marble.

"A single scout," Gabriel whispered at last, and his voice sounded so thin and so wounded that Iofiel felt the temptation to look away out of decency. "We sent a single scout, Iofiel, so as not to risk our resources, and someone else sent an army's worth of resolve."

There was no comfort possible for that arithmetic, and neither of them tried to manufacture a lie to fill the room. Looking at her general's silent profile, Iofiel understood, with a cold nausea, that the prudence the Council had taken such pride in during its three-hour debate had just cost them the first and most important frontier of the war to come.

---

Belial felt the change in Dis's citadel before the first iron gate had even finished closing behind him. The infernal court ran on murmurs and balances of power, and the air in the corridors carried that uneasy fixedness that greets a general whose absence has stretched long enough for his rivals to start dividing his legions on paper. Conversations among the secretaries and quartermasters paused a fraction too long as his boots struck the damp flagstones, and the faces that turned toward him took a beat too long to settle back into their usual deference.

Veteran captains watched him sidelong, weighing with slow glances whether the general's return carried the same strength or whether his convalescence had left some crack through which to slide a corridor dagger. Malgorath's name — that noisy rival who had spent his silence calling in favors across three different courts — had gone conspicuously quiet in the week's ledger; his sulfur debts had proven too thin to sustain his ambition. But the true erosion of Belial's command had never needed so obvious a name; it moved instead in the silence of officers who had already begun their calculations without his signature.

Belial walked the long corridor with the same detached certainty as always, Lamentum's cold thrum pulsing against his bruised ribs, giving them none of the satisfaction of seeing how much it cost him to hold his stride rigid. Two young captains approached him near the armory with a warmth that was visibly overplayed, the sort of hurried enthusiasm men offer when they fear their commander might remember they doubted him before noon. Belial accepted their greetings with a flat nod, filing each face away in the inventory of his memory for the reckoning to come, and moved on toward the chamber at the far end, where he knew the real danger had been waiting for him for three days.

He found Foras in the small audience chamber, a low-vaulted room where walls of cracked obsidian swallowed the light of a single brazier burning at its center. There was no surprise in his many golden eyes, none of which blinked in unison as Belial's shadow crossed the threshold; he held himself in the same serene, oily stillness Belial remembered from their worst dealings, hands folded over his cloak of purple mist, showing no hurry to rise or draw a weapon.

"While you were chasing ghosts in the salt," Foras said, his soft hiss filling the chamber without needing to raise its volume, "someone had to hold this together."

The words fell with the patience of a man who had spent a month occupying an empty seat and knew exactly how many legions owed their bread to his signature. Belial felt Lamentum's notch pulse cold at his hip, a warning that chilled his blood beneath the mail, but forced himself to match his rival's stillness before answering.

"And you held it, Foras," Belial said, his voice as flat and hard as his mace's iron. "Now let go of it. Before we start confusing gratitude with permission."

Neither man stepped forward; neither man's fingers slid toward the pommel of a weapon. But the silence that settled between them in the ring of orange light was long, weighted with everything that rehearsed treason in the shadows tends to leave unsaid before a rival's eyes. Belial understood, with the bitterness of quicklime in his throat, that the Sepulcher's mystical triumph had not erased the political erosion Foras had let grow unchecked in his rear; the throne of Dis remained as unstable as it had been on the first day.

Foras held his general's golden gaze for an unbearable second, weighing the spear's cold against the wound in his side that was healing poorly, and finally inclined his head in a measured bow that conceded nothing and closed nothing for the season to come. He turned and left the chamber by his own door, without waiting for the general to give a dismissal. Belial watched him go in the room's silence, his left hand pressed against his ribs and Lamentum's thrum still pulsing in his bones, understanding that the skirmish was not over; it had simply been postponed until the mud of the front decided which side the next verdict would fall on.
