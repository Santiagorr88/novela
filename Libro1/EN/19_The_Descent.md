# Chapter 19 — The Descent

The path deeper into the Weeping Sepulcher opened before Belial the moment he stopped fighting the ground beneath his feet — a narrow seam through the black crystal that hadn't been visible on his first, furious pass through this same territory. He might have walked directly over it that first day and never known it was there, too focused on the guardian ahead to notice ground that asked, rather than demanded, to be followed.

He walked it slowly, deliberately, the seam narrowing and widening around him in ways that seemed to answer his own pace instead of any fixed shape of the path. The visions began somewhere in the first hour — distant at first, abstract enough to hold at arm's length. The grief of a dying star, vast and cold, felt from this distance like weather rather than experience, something he could watch pass over him without it touching anything underneath. He walked through it the way a man walks through fog, aware of it settling on his skin without ever quite believing it belonged to him.

The loneliness of some extinct species, the last of its kind fading out somewhere he had no name for, arrived the same way — enormous, ancient, safely far from anything Belial himself had ever felt or feared feeling. He noted each vision as it passed, cataloguing them with the same detached precision he might have used on an unfamiliar battlefield, more curious than moved. He counted three in that first hour alone, each fading as cleanly as it had arrived, gone as easily as smoke clears from a closed room. This, he told himself, was manageable. This was simply the plane's nature announcing itself.

The visions did not stay abstract. Somewhere past the second hour, they sharpened, stopped being weather and started being specific in a way that reached closer to him than anything before it had.

He felt, without warning, the grief of a commander watching soldiers die under orders he no longer believed in — not any commander's grief in the abstract, but something that landed close enough to his own long service that he couldn't fully convince himself it belonged to someone else. He saw, in fragments too vivid to dismiss, the faces of soldiers he did not recognize falling in a battle he had never fought, and felt the weight of having sent them there anyway, knowing the cause was already lost. It was not his memory. It felt, for the length of that vision, exactly as though it were — down to the smell of dust and blood he had never breathed, and the names of soldiers he had never commanded. His hand went to the mace's grip before he'd consciously decided to reach for it, some old reflex demanding a target even though nothing here had a body to strike. He made himself let go, fingers uncurling one at a time with an effort that cost him more than the guardian's blows had. A second vision followed hard on the first — a healer's grief this time, watching patients die of something no remedy in her long study had ever touched, one after another, the specific helplessness of skill that had run out of room to matter. His hand twitched toward the mace again, and again he forced it still, the discipline costing him more the second time, not less.

He stood there a long moment breathing through a grief that had no enemy attached to it, nothing to fight, nothing to blame, only the plain weight of it sitting in his chest until he found some way to carry it forward. The crystal chimed faintly around him with every ragged breath, indifferent, as it had been indifferent to everything else this plane had shown him.

The morning had started ordinary enough — the boy's first real trip out past the shallows without his uncle along, the water calm, the nets already half full by the time the sun cleared the ridgeline. The tremor reached the fishing boat without warning, rocking it hard enough that the boy nearly lost his grip on the gunwale before his father's free hand caught his shoulder and steadied him. Even the ridgeline seemed to shudder, dust rising along its slope, and the boy couldn't remember any talk, from his father or his uncle or anyone in the village, of a tremor ever reaching this coast before.

"What was that?" the boy asked, voice higher than he meant it to come out, eyes fixed on water that had gone strange and choppy under a sky that showed no storm anywhere in it.

His father kept both hands on the tiller, knuckles pale with the effort, working to hold their course against a current that had shifted without any wind to explain it, the hull groaning against water pulling from a direction it had no business pulling from. "Sea's just having a bad—" he started, in what sounded to the boy like the same joke his father usually reached for through rougher water than this. He didn't finish it. His voice caught, cracking on a word that should have come out steady, and the boy heard it clearly even over the slap of water against the hull.

They rode out the worst of it in silence, the boat pitching hard for what felt like a long time and was probably no more than a minute, gear sliding across the deck, the nets nearly going over the side before the father lashed them down one-handed, still gripping the tiller with the other. When the water settled back into something like its ordinary rhythm, neither of them spoke for a while.

The boy said nothing further, but something in him settled differently after that crack in his father's voice — not the tremor itself, already passing, but the plain fact that his father, who had never once sounded frightened of the sea, had sounded frightened just now. That sat with him longer, and heavier, than the shaking of the boat ever had. They finished the day's fishing anyway — no good reason not to, and every reason to get back with something to show for the trip — but the boy caught his father glancing back over his shoulder at open water more than once on the way in, toward nothing he could see, as though checking that whatever had passed beneath them wasn't circling back.

Ilariel had drawn the anomaly watch this rotation, a duty other sentinels often dismissed as beneath their training, and had spent the better part of the last several hours logging nothing more interesting than the ordinary hum of a quiet sector. The watch station was small, tucked into a corridor no senior officer had come through in longer than Ilariel could remember, its bank of readings scrolling past in a steady, uneventful stream.

The dissonance arrived without any clear point of origin, a wrongness in the fabric of things that Ilariel's instruments registered but couldn't properly classify, faint enough that a less attentive watcher might have dismissed it as instrument drift. Ilariel checked the readings twice, cross-referencing them against the standard catalogue of known anomaly signatures without finding a single clean match. Ilariel did not dismiss it. The report went up the channel within minutes, careful and precise.

"Anomalous resonance detected, sector seven, no clear source," Ilariel reported to the superior on duty, keeping the transmission clipped despite the unease the reading had stirred.

"Another residual echo from the last engagement?" the superior asked, already sounding half-distracted by something else on the duty roster.

Ilariel pressed the point anyway. "It doesn't read like an echo. Echoes fade outward from a point. This has a direction — like it's moving *toward* somewhere, not just happening."

There was a brief pause on the other end, long enough that Ilariel almost repeated the point before the superior finally answered.

"Log it as directional, then, and keep monitoring the sector. Flag me directly if it strengthens."

It was not dismissal, exactly, but it was not urgency either. A standard acknowledgment mark appeared on Ilariel's log a moment later, the same routine notation the channel generated for a dozen minor readings every week, and the connection closed with a quiet click.

Belial pressed deeper into the plane with the visions still crowding close around him, closer now than any battlefield fear he had ever needed to master. His knees buckled once, briefly, under a vision heavier than the ones before it, and he caught himself against the crystal wall of the passage before he went fully down, breath ragged, vision swimming at its edges for several long seconds before it cleared. He forced his spine straight again, refusing to let the moment become a second one, and pressed on. The crystal underfoot had grown denser here, the ground sloping downward in a long, gradual descent that matched, uncomfortably, the sense that he was moving toward the center of something rather than merely across it.

It was not compassion. He told himself that clearly, more than once, unwilling to grant this place a victory that complete. He had spent centuries cultivating the kind of distance from others' suffering that made him effective at his own work, and he was not eager to discover, this far from home, that the distance had a limit he had never before been forced to test. But after the accumulated griefs that were not his own — the commander's, the healer's, the dying star's, the extinct species' last howl into silence — something in him had changed in a way he could not simply will back. He understood, more clearly than he had before stepping through this portal a second time, that grief of this size did not require an enemy to explain itself. It simply existed, vast and unattached, indifferent to whoever happened to be passing through it. He did not welcome the understanding. He could not, entirely, deny that he now possessed it either.

The path opened fully ahead of him not long after, a channel through the crystal that hadn't been visible from any angle before this one, its walls smoother than the jagged ground he'd walked so far, as though something had worn this route down through long use rather than left it to form on its own. It led toward something he could not yet see but could feel pulling at him with the same unmistakable weight as the visions themselves.

He was diminished by the crossing so far, in ways he did not entirely want to catalogue. He was steadier on his feet than he had any right to expect, but he carried something now that hadn't been there when he first stepped through the portal — a weight that had nothing to do with his healing ribs and everything to do with griefs that were not his own. He wondered, briefly, whether that weight would lift once he left this place, or whether some portion of it was simply his to carry from now on. He followed the path anyway, because turning back had not been a serious option since he first crossed through. The chiming of the crystal followed him into the channel, softer here than in the open plane, almost gentle in a way that struck him as its own kind of invitation. Whatever waited at the end of this channel, he intended to reach it — diminished, perhaps, but decided, and still moving forward one deliberate step at a time.
