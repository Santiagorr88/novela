# Chapter 22 — What Should Not Have Burned

Krass had held this stretch of neutral plane for nearly a decade, long enough that his bow's worn leather and the crystal groves' constant chiming had stopped feeling like a court assignment and started feeling like his own home. He leaned his shoulder against the smooth trunk of one of the glass spires, feeling the cold flagstones beneath his boots, and let his gaze wander over the valley's perpetual twilight.

Beneath the bronzed skin of his arms, the dark lines of his tattoos moved with a lazy patience, a slow flow of living ink that answered the subtle shifts in air pressure and the music of the place itself. That ground served Hell nothing: no sulfur quarries worth mining, no souls worth harvesting in numbers that would justify an official report, not one inch of rock worth defending by Dis's own maps. Krass had stopped looking for a strategic reason years ago. He held the line simply because the standing order was still there, written on some old scroll nobody had bothered to revoke, and because the crystal's chime at dusk was a simple song that helped calm his temper.

The soldier on his left flank was barely old enough to wear chainmail without the weight bowing his shoulders, and his hands, gripping the shaft of an ordinary pike, would not quite settle in the silence of the watch. He was a green recruit, fresh out of the capital's training pits, and the silence of a border where nothing ever happened was wearing him down faster than an enemy charge would have.

"I don't know if I'll be able to hold it, commander," the boy whispered, his voice so low the wind through the groves nearly swallowed it before it reached Krass's ear.

Krass didn't correct him with a whip's discipline. He straightened slowly, letting his own bow rest a moment against his thigh, and gave the boy the one honest truth an old soldier has any right to offer.

"No one knows until they're holding it," Krass said, his voice stripped of any court's martial rhetoric. "I didn't either, the first time."

The boy looked at him with eyes that, in the dim light, seemed too large for his face. Krass didn't wait for the panic to fully settle. He reached for an arrow in his quiver — one of Affectus's silent shafts — and nocked it to the string in a single, practiced motion. He didn't aim at the enemy; he let the shot pass close along the boy's shoulder, near enough for the string's wind to raise the hair on his arm without ever biting the flesh.

The tattoos on his forearm flared with a dense, golden light the instant the gift left the string. What it delivered to the recruit wasn't the easy anesthesia of forgetting — Affectus never dealt in lies. It gave him love, pure, sudden, and at full strength: his own commander's affection for the men he'd shared mud and rancid rations with for months, drawn out and lit with an intensity the boy couldn't ignore. The fear didn't leave his face, but it had to share the space with something far larger, and the recruit's shoulders steadied at once — not because the danger had gone, but because he was no longer alone in facing it.

"Thank you," the boy said quietly, and Krass only nodded, already turning his attention back toward the tree line where the wrongness had first begun to gather.

The first sign of the Host didn't arrive as the blare of celestial horns or the clash of swords on shields; it arrived instead as a physical anomaly in the plane's own breathing.

The crystal groves, which had been chiming with their constant music for ten years, fell silent all at once, every note dying mid-tone in a sudden paralysis that left the air without a single echo. Krass felt the hair on his arms rise at that flat, windless silence.

The formation that crested the rise above moved with a precision that left no room for the ordinary chaos of a border skirmish. There were no probing strikes, no flanking maneuvers, none of the natural hesitation of captains studying the ground before committing their lines. They advanced as a single unbroken wall of light, a perfect, steady, metronomic pace that had already decided the outcome before the first soldier of Hell could raise his pike. Krass remembered the report from the Zaphor'el survivor that the lords of Dis had dismissed as an exaggerated tale. He saw Miguel's silhouette at the head of the wave of light, and understood, with the coldness of a man tallying an inventory of dead flesh, that what they were about to receive wasn't a battle; it was an execution.

"We're not leaving here," Krass said, looking down his line at the faces of soldiers who had followed him through seasons.

There were no speeches about the glory of the Fall, no promises of a throne of bones in the capital. He took up his bow one last time, nocking the arrow he had left, and didn't turn it toward the wall of celestial light bearing down on them — he aimed it wide, an angled shot that crossed the twilight sky to graze as many of his men as the gift would allow him to reach.

He felt the string bite into his fingers and the strain tear at the flesh of his forearms; he had never before tried to project Affectus across an entire company at once, and he understood, as the lines of his tattoos lit with a searing brightness that broke cold sweat across his temples, that his body would not survive to attempt it a second time. He didn't give them a soldier's courage; he gave them his own love for them, his grief for every life about to be lost on that useless stone, lit as bright as he could force his soul to burn. If this was going to be the last heartbeat for his men, Krass preferred they die feeling the warmth of their brotherhood before Heaven's cold light erased them from the map.

The collision reached them a second later. The boy fell before the first celestial blade even touched his line, dropped by a deflected blow that split his chest with a dry crack. He fell directly in Krass's line of sight, his body going still on the crystal flagstones with that absolute stillness that leaves no room for hope.

Krass didn't feel the hot rage of old campaigns, that loud fury that helps soldiers die without thinking. He felt a frigid clarity, an exact pain that tasted of quicklime in his throat. That life had a face, a name, and a voice that had confessed its fear to him an hour before in the valley's dim light. No ledger in Hell, no strategic need of the lords of Dis, would ever balance out what that fallen pike had just cost the world. He rose, unsteady on legs he could no longer fully feel, and turned back toward the collapsing front.

The blow that brought him down arrived before his tactical eye could register its trajectory, a dull impact that tore the ground from under his boots and threw him onto his back against the cracked pavement. He felt no real pain; Affectus still ran through his ribs like a warm sediment, cushioning the bite of the steel. He heard the groves chime one last time, a sound the assault's wind unraveled into an orphaned whisper, and looked up at the starless sky of his dead frontier.

He watched Miguel's legions advance over his fallen line, perfect and untouched, their armor gold without a single dent to testify that a real defense had ever been mounted here. He didn't spend his last seconds hating them. He spent them with the same icy certainty that the recruit's death had brought him: a specific injustice, with a name and a face, that neither side's professed law had ever required of them. He gripped the cold stone with both hands, feeling the crystal crack beneath his fingers, and kept that grief without absolving anyone, forgiving neither the Heaven that erased them nor the Hell that had left them alone in the mud. Affectus's clarity didn't fade from his chest as the plane's light began to go dark in his eyes. It was, in the end, the only thing he managed to carry out of the only frontier he had ever called his own: that proud, immense, unpaid debt, held steady in silence until the night finally closed over his name.
