# Chapter 22 — What Should Not Have Burned

Krass had held this stretch of neutral plane for the better part of a decade — long enough that the crystalline groves and quiet twilight had stopped feeling like an assignment and started feeling like home. His arms bore the marks of every season he'd spent here, ink shifting slow and constant beneath his skin, the way it had since the day he bonded with it.

It served Hell nothing, strategically. No resources worth extracting, no souls worth harvesting in any real number — nothing that justified a squad's presence here beyond a standing order nobody had bothered to revoke. Krass had stopped questioning it years ago. He held the line he'd been given, day after quiet day, tattoos shifting faintly along his forearms with whatever mood passed through him, his bow unstrung across his back more often than not, Affectus's arrows quiet in the quiver beside it.

The groves chimed softly at dusk, an ordinary music he knew the way a man knows his own street. He'd watched whole seasons pass without a skirmish worth reporting, watched squads rotate in and out while he stayed, and in all that quiet time had never needed his arrows for anything darker than talking a homesick soldier through a bad night.

The soldier on his flank tonight had not once stood a real line before — his hands wouldn't quite stop moving even with nothing yet to do. He was young even by the standards of a squad that ran younger than most, fresh out of a training company that hadn't prepared him for the particular quiet of a border nobody expected to see real action on.

"I don't know if I'll be able to hold it," the boy admitted, voice low enough that Krass had to lean in to catch it over the wind through the groves. He didn't answer right away with the bow. He answered first, the way any commander worth trusting answers a soldier admitting fear before a fight neither chose. "No one knows until they're holding it," Krass said. "I didn't either, the first time." The boy looked at him like the admission had cost something. He worked his jaw a moment, eyes fixed past Krass's shoulder on the dark tree line. Only then did Krass reach for his bow, nocked an arrow he had no intention of loosing at any enemy, and let it graze the boy's shoulder close enough to work without wounding him, the tattoos on his forearm flaring brighter as the gift left the string.

What it carried into him wasn't a dulling of the fear — the gift never offered that mercy. It gave him love instead, sudden and full-force: his own quiet affection for the soldiers beside him, the ones he'd trained with these past months, drawn up and set burning bright enough he couldn't mistake it for anything smaller. Fear mattered less once it had to share space with something that large — what remained was the plainer fear of failing people he now knew, with total clarity, that he loved. The boy's shoulders steadied, not because the fear had left, but because something else had arrived to stand beside it. He looked up at Krass, dread shifted into resolve — still afraid, but no longer alone with it.

"Thank you," the boy said quietly, and Krass only nodded, already turning his attention back toward the tree line where the wrongness had first begun to gather.

The first sign of Miguel's force reached them not as noise but as a wrongness in the air — a silence too total for an approach this size. The groves that had chimed gently every dusk fell quiet all at once, every note dying mid-tone. Krass had commanded soldiers long enough to know the shape of a real battle before it began: ragged edges, uncertainty, the small chaos that crept into even the most disciplined assault.

This carried none of that. The formation advancing on his line moved with a precision that left no room for a fight — each angel so coordinated with the next that Krass understood, watching it unfold, he wasn't looking at a battle at all. No flanking, no probing strikes, no pause to reconsider the ground: only a single unbroken wall of light advancing at a pace that never wavered, already certain of an outcome it saw no need to question. He'd heard secondhand of a demon officer elsewhere on this front who'd described an assault the same way — not a fight, an execution — and had written it off as exaggeration. He recognized it now from the inside, watching his own line about to receive exactly what that officer had already survived once.

He knew, watching the formation close the distance, that nothing his squad did in the next few minutes would change what was coming. He looked once down the line at faces he'd known for years, a few for only weeks, and understood this would not be a night any of them walked away from telling stories about. He'd led plenty of nights that ended in that kind of story. He didn't expect this to be one of them. He raised his bow anyway, his last arrow already nocked, and turned it not toward the approaching light but toward his own soldiers braced along the line.

He loosed it wide, a single shot angled to graze as many of them as the arc allowed, catching the last of the dying light as it crossed the space between him and the line. He felt the gift work across the whole line at once instead of one soldier at a time — a strain he'd never attempted before and wouldn't survive to attempt twice. Not fear. Not even courage. He gave each of them, in the last moments he had any say over what they felt, their own love for the soldiers beside them, drawn up and set burning as bright as he could manage — a feeling most of them had carried quietly for years without saying it aloud. If this was the last thing any of them felt, Krass meant it to be that, and nothing colder. His own tattoos flared across his arms with the effort, brighter than he'd ever felt them burn, and he let it pass through him without holding onto what it cost.

The boy went down before the fighting properly reached him, caught by a blow meant for someone else, dropping directly in Krass's line of sight before Krass could reach for anything that might have stopped it. He fell without a sound Krass could hear over everything else breaking loose, and lay still enough to leave no room for hoping otherwise.

It was not rage that moved through Krass. Rage was loud; what he felt instead arrived cold and exact, a clarity he hadn't expected grief to carry. He had lost soldiers before, across centuries of holding lines like this one, and every loss before this one had come wrapped in the same hot, familiar anger. This one didn't. This wasn't an abstract cost of a war neither side had started kindly. This had a face he'd spoken to an hour ago, a voice that had admitted its fear to him in words he still heard clearly — *I don't know if I'll be able to hold it* — and Krass had told him no one did until they were already holding it, and the boy had believed him, and held it anyway, right up until the moment holding it stopped mattering. He understood, kneeling beside a body that wouldn't answer him, what this was. It had a name. It had cost something specific and irreplaceable, and no accounting of strategic necessity in Hell's ledgers was going to balance that loss back out. He didn't have time to kneel there long. The line was already breaking further down its length, soldiers falling in twos and threes, and whatever grief he owed this death would have to wait for a reckoning this battle would never grant him. He rose, unsteady, and turned back toward whatever remained of the fight.

The blow that took Krass down came moments later, faster than he had any chance to prepare for, and he felt it arrive with a strange calm, his mind a pace behind his body, watching the rest happen from a small distance away. There was no pain worth the name — only a sudden loss of the ground beneath him, the crystalline chime of the grove reaching his ears one last time as he went down hard onto the cold stone.

He didn't spend his last conscious moment hating the angels advancing over his fallen line, precise and untouched, their formation as coordinated now as at the start of the assault. He spent it instead on the same cold, exact recognition that had taken hold when the boy fell — a specific injustice, with a face and a name, that no war either side claimed to be fighting had ever required. Ground worth nothing to anyone had cost him a soldier who'd trusted his word an hour before he needed it most, and no strategic ledger in Hell or Heaven had a column wide enough to hold that debt. The clarity didn't fade the way the sound of the fighting did, or the chime of the groves. It was, in the end, the last thing Krass carried out of the only ground he'd ever called his — not the years of quiet watches, not the centuries of small kindnesses handed out one arrow at a time, only that one injustice, held clear and unforgiven until it too went dark.
