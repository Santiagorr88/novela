# Chapter 43 — The Summit

At the highest point of a peak higher than any the world had ever charted, where the air thinned past anything a mortal lung could draw and hold, a figure sat cross-legged upon bare stone, spine straight, hands open on its knees, head tilted forward. Centuries of snow had gathered across the folds of its robes without disturbing the stillness beneath them, ice building layer upon layer along shoulders that had not shifted, had not so much as trembled against the wind, in longer than any record of this world could measure.

It had not moved. It had not breathed. It had not thought, in any sense a living mind would recognize as thought, in millennia uncounted. Frost thickened slowly over every visible surface until the figure and the granite beneath it had become nearly indistinguishable from one another — a shape carved from the mountain's own heart rather than seated upon it. Ice closed over the folds of its robes season upon season, until the fabric itself disappeared beneath a shell of pale, unbroken white. Nothing about the shape suggested decay, or growth, or any of the small daily erosions that marked time everywhere else in the world below. It simply persisted, the way a boulder settles into a riverbed until the current wears a permanent course around it.

No bird crossed this height, no insect, no living creature willing or able to test air this thin against its own survival. The silence here was not the absence of sound. It was the absence of anything that could have made one — a stillness so complete that when the wind finally rose, it seemed almost an intrusion. On the rare nights the clouds thinned enough to reveal them, the stars burned with a clarity no lower elevation could match. The figure never looked up to see them. Whether it saw anything at all, in whatever sense seeing still applied to eyes that had not opened, by any outward measure, since before the glaciers below carved their first valley, no one remained to ask.

No cartographer of Heaven, Hell, or the mortal world beneath both had ever charted the summit — not because any will had hidden it, but because no expedition mounted by any of the three had survived the ascent long enough to record it. The air here held too little of what a body needed to sustain itself; even the most disciplined lungs could draw only a handful of desperate breaths before the world at the edges of vision began to dim. It remained untouched not by mystery but by altitude alone.

Below the final approach, where the lower slopes still permitted breath and footing, generations of climbers from a dozen scattered kingdoms had left their markers — cairns of stacked stone, a rusted length of rope still anchored to an outcrop, the bleached remains of an expedition that had come further than most and still turned back with half its number lost to the thinning air. None had reached the summit. Even the mountain's own name never had: each kingdom called it something different, and none of those words had climbed this far either. Whatever this height was called in the valleys below, at its peak it remained without a name of its own — simply the place the air ran out, and the world ended.

Something moved through the world far below, faint enough that no single point of origin could have accounted for it alone — not one note but an accumulation, dozens of small dissonances rising at once from places too distant and too varied to trace to any one source. It was the sound, if it could be called sound, of a tension that had been building the way pressure builds behind a dam wall no one thought to inspect, finally finding its way toward release.

It was not an earthquake, not in any sense the mortal world would have recognized. No ground split beneath any city, no ocean rose to swallow any shore. The disturbance moved through something far less visible than stone or water — a discord felt rather than witnessed, present everywhere at once and nowhere specific enough to name. It offered only its arrival, distant and unmistakably real, spreading outward toward every corner of a world that had, for a long time now, been drawing tighter around a strain it had not yet been asked to bear.

The world's ordinary business continued below, unaware. No sky darkened. No sea stilled. Merchants in a dozen scattered ports went on loading their ships; soldiers in a dozen scattered armies went on sharpening their blades; not one of them registered so much as a flicker of what had just passed through the deeper architecture beneath all of it. The tremor moved through some layer of the world ordinary attention never reached, leaving the surface exactly as undisturbed as it had been a moment before.

It moved upward all the same — through layers the mortal world had no name for, past the last thin edges of the mountain's lower slopes, climbing higher still until it reached the summit, thinned by distance to something that should have arrived as nothing at all.

It reached the stone beneath the seated figure regardless, and the stone cracked.

A single hairline fissure traced across the granite's frost-covered surface, beginning beneath the figure's stillness and spreading outward before it stopped — no wider than a strand of the ice that covered it, in a place no living creature would ever travel far enough to witness. But this was ground that had gone unmarked since before the first cairn was ever stacked, granite that had outlasted empires without once shifting beneath the weight of the millennia pressing down on it. Here, in a silence this absolute, so small a fracture landed with the force of something far larger.

The ice around the new fissure shifted, releasing into the air the faint, mineral tang of freshly broken stone — a smell this ground had not carried in longer than anything living could measure. It was the first true movement in that patch of ground since the figure seated upon it had first settled into its stillness. A thin dusting of frost broke loose along the crack and drifted before settling again into the same unbroken white. No sound accompanied it. At this altitude, sound could carry no real distance.

The figure did not react. Whatever passed for awareness in something this still, this old, gave no sign of registering the change in the stone that held it. The crack remained regardless — the first new mark this ground had taken since long before the wind wore this route down the mountain.

The wind rose in the crack's aftermath, howling across a summit no sound had crossed in longer than there had been ears below to hear one, scouring frost from stone that had held its shape since before this height had a name at all. The figure did not stir. Snow kept gathering along shoulders that had not shifted in millennia.

The fissure remained, thin and pale against the granite, curved along part of its length into something that might have resembled a question mark carved into the heart of the world, had any eye been left at this height to trace it. Nothing else about the summit had changed. The wind continued its circuit, the snow its slow accumulation, and fresh frost gathered along the fissure's edges without erasing it — small, and entirely new, against ground that had taken no mark since the first flake of that same ice ever settled upon it.

Far below, the disturbance that had produced the crack kept fading, dissipating back into the depth it had first risen from, leaving no trace anywhere it had passed except this one thin line in ancient stone. No record would ever mark the moment. Nothing beyond the mountain itself, and whatever remained of the figure seated upon it, would ever know the tremor had reached this high.

The figure did not move, not at once, nor in the time that followed. Then, for the first time in longer than anyone living could measure, it began to stir.
