# Chapter 44 — The Eve of Ruin

Uriel stood at the center of the Bastion's war room when the first reports began arriving, close together and wrong in a way nothing in his long service had prepared him to expect. No single front had fallen. A dozen had, nearly simultaneously — garrisons across the length of Heaven's defensive line buckling within moments of each other, with no visible cause any crystalline screen could yet identify.

He had commanded through sieges, through ambushes, through every conventional shape war had ever taken. This bore none of those shapes. The first wave, whatever it was, carried no visible weapon and required no physical breach — garrisons that had held firm through worse simply stopped fighting, weapons lowering in hands that had raised them without hesitation minutes before. Lamentum, Uriel already knew, coldly, before a single confirming report reached him, had found its full voice at last. That voice needed nothing so crude as a blade — only despair, carried straight into soldiers who had never raised a weapon against it and never would.

The war room filled quickly with the clipped, overlapping urgency of officers drilled for a hundred crises shaped nothing like this one. Aides crossed from screen to screen relaying fragments too disjointed yet to form a coherent picture, and Uriel spent his first minutes doing nothing but take the scale of it in — not one front collapsing under concentrated pressure, but a dozen at once, each report arriving close enough on the heels of the last that no officer had time to process one crisis before the next demanded equal attention.

He had faced Belial across a battlefield more times than he cared to count, had traded blows with him directly on at least three occasions he could recall without effort. Never once had he sensed anything from his opponent that hinted at this. Whatever Belial had brought back from the Weeping Sepulcher, it had changed the shape of the war, and Uriel understood, standing amid the mounting chaos of his own war room, that working out why would matter far less in the coming hours than simply surviving it.

A sentinel posted along the wall at Xylo's Gate felt the wave arrive not as force against his body but as memory — every doubt he had ever buried beneath the plain language of duty rising at once, fully formed, impossible to set back down. He remembered, in a rush that hit harder than any wound ever had, every order he had followed without fully believing in its cause, every comrade he had watched fall for reasons that had stopped making sense long before tonight.

His hands opened before any decision reached them. The weapon he had carried without complaint for longer than he cared to count struck the stone at his feet, and he didn't reach to retrieve it. He stood amid the gathering chaos of his own garrison, weaponless, the doubt that had come with the wave pressing on him with a weight far heavier than the halberd he'd just let fall, and found he had no wish to pick up either one.

Around him, soldiers he had trained beside for years mirrored the same collapse, some sinking to their knees, others standing motionless, expressions suggesting they too had just been handed something they couldn't set back down. No enemy stood visibly before any of them. Whatever had struck the garrison had come from somewhere no stone wall could stand against, and none of the training that had prepared them for a physical assault offered them anything useful against this one.

He thought, standing there amid the wreckage of his own resolve, of the doubt that had surfaced clearest among everything the wave had unearthed — a question he had buried decades earlier about whether the cause he served still deserved the certainty he'd once given it. It had never fully left him, only gone quiet beneath the weight of duty and habit. Tonight it had found its voice again, louder than any defense he had left to answer it with.

Reports kept flooding the war room faster than any mind could absorb them, screens flaring one after another with news that grew worse with each arrival. *The garrison at Xylo's Gate has laid down its arms.* *The Adamant Guard is not responding. They are weeping.*

Uriel watched the screens multiply the same message across a dozen fronts, each dressed in slightly different words describing the same collapse. No battle line reported physical breach. Every one reported something no shield or counter-formation could touch — soldiers who had simply stopped believing the fight was worth continuing, weapons abandoned not through defeat but through something that looked, on every screen, disturbingly close to grief.

Officers called out casualty estimates that meant less than they ever had, since no actual violence had produced them — garrisons rendered useless not by loss of life but by loss of will, an outcome none of the war room's planning had ever seriously accounted for. Uriel listened to the tally climb, front after front reporting the same failure in different language, and felt the scale of tonight's crisis settle into something he had no framework for measuring.

A junior officer near him read a fresh report aloud, his voice audibly starting to shake. *The eastern garrisons have gone silent. No further transmissions received.* Uriel absorbed it without visible reaction, filing it alongside everything else accumulating across the war room's screens — though silence from an entire sector, he understood, was a far graver development than any collapse reported so far.

He crossed to the officer's station and studied the last transmission himself, searching the fragmented message for anything that might clarify what "silent" meant here — retreat, collapse, or something worse no one had survived to report. The message offered nothing further. He set the question aside, alongside a dozen others accumulating faster than the war room could answer them, and turned back to whatever remained actionable in tonight's catalogue of failure.

He shouted orders anyway, decades of command reflex overriding his growing certainty that they had nowhere useful to land. Reinforcements to Xylo's Gate. A counter-formation along the Adamant line. Every command that had served him faithfully across centuries of conventional warfare, thrown now at an enemy that answered to none of it, because the enemy had no physical position for reinforcements to hold or counter-formations to break.

He had built his reputation on decisive, immediate assault — fire enough to purify anything foolish enough to stand in its path, a solution that had answered every threat until tonight. This one offered him nothing to burn. It moved through his soldiers' doubts and memories, spaces his flame had no way of reaching, and left him standing at the center of his own war room with orders forming and dying on his tongue before he could find anyone left to receive them.

He considered, briefly, marching to the nearest front himself, halberd in hand, trusting his own presence might steady soldiers whose will had buckled where their courage hadn't. He abandoned the thought almost as quickly as it arrived. Whatever moved through his garrisons tonight answered to nothing his flame was built to counter, and a commander who charged blindly into a battle he didn't understand risked compounding tonight's losses, not reversing them. The stillness that followed sat uneasily against every instinct centuries of decisive action had trained into him.

For the first time since this war had begun, Uriel had nothing left worth commanding, and stood in the war room's gathering chaos with his hands empty of anything resembling a solution.

Gabriel found his brother in a corner of the Bastion far from the war room's mounting panic, seated in silence, and didn't speak right away. He had come with no speech prepared, no argument to counter whatever resistance Miguel still carried from the night Iofiel read them the prophecy. He had come only with the truth, and judged that it would have to be enough.

He had spent the walk from the war room turning over how to frame what he'd come to say, discarding one approach after another as too forceful or too gentle for what tonight required. He wasn't here to command his brother back into a fight Miguel had every reason to fear. He was here because the war unraveling across a dozen fronts left him no honest alternative to asking, and because he suspected, reading the stillness over his brother, that Miguel already understood the shape of what was coming before either of them spoke.

Gabriel kept his voice quiet. "He has the spear. He is breaking them."

He did not frame it as an order. He had given Miguel enough orders across this war to know how little weight one carried against whatever held his brother in this silence. What he offered instead was a fact, delivered without embellishment — a weight meant for his brother to carry alongside him, not beneath him.

He watched Miguel's expression for some sign of how the fact had landed, and found little he could confidently read — not refusal, not yet acceptance, something quieter than either. He had learned, across their shared service, when to press his brother and when silence did more work than any word could. Tonight called for the latter. He said nothing further, and didn't move to leave, content to let the fact settle in the space between them for as long as Miguel needed.

Miguel looked past Gabriel toward the far wall, where Solmire still rested against the stone right where he'd kicked her days earlier, untouched since. He understood what she was — something cursed and alien, forged before the Divine Word itself, wielding him as readily as he wielded her. He understood, with equal clarity, that she remained the only power in his possession capable of meeting Lamentum on anything resembling equal terms.

He turned Gabriel's words over once more, weighing the fact against every private reason he'd spent the last several days assembling for staying where he sat. Belial was breaking entire garrisons without raising the spear against a single soldier directly. Whatever doubt had kept Miguel frozen in this corner, it offered no honest answer to soldiers losing the will to fight for reasons that had nothing to do with their own courage. He had no illusions left about what picking the blade back up would cost him. He had far fewer about what leaving her where she lay would cost everyone else.

He rose slowly, crossed the room's short distance, and knelt to retrieve the blade he had refused to touch since the night his own hand erased fleeing stragglers without his permission, certain, kneeling there, that this would be the last time he ever reached for her. His heart carried no lightness taking up the weight again, no return of whatever trust had once existed between them. He simply understood, closing his fingers around the familiar, cold hilt, that Gabriel's fact left him no alternative.

The blade's weight settled back into his grip exactly as it always had, familiar and unwelcome in equal measure. He held her a moment longer than the retrieval required, still bracing, as he always did the instant his hand closed around her, before finally turning to face his brother across the room. Whatever the battlefield asked of him now, he would carry it with a blade he no longer fully trusted, in a war that had just shown him far more of its true shape than he'd been ready to see. Miguel rose with his brother's fact now fully his own to carry as well.
