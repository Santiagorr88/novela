# Chapter 14 — Dreams of Laughter

Miguel had not slept a single clean stretch of night since the instant his fingers first closed around Solmire's hilt. The exhaustion was no longer a fatigue of the muscle; it had become a gray sediment gathering on his eyelids, making the Bastion's air too heavy to breathe. He knelt beside his bed, reaching for the old morning prayer's handhold, forcing his tongue through the verses of the Divine Canticle that had been his shield before dawn for three eras. But the words felt worn out, flat as lead coins polished by too many hands.

Sleep claimed him mid-verse, and it was no rest: it was a freefall into a borderless void.

The forge rose before him with the same roar of collapsing suns and stellar metal he remembered from Serephis. He watched the maker's hammer strike a dense sun-anvil, and watched whole galaxies come loose like useless sparks, beautiful and irrelevant. And over the noise of metal and burning fire ran the laughter. A joyful, colossal laugh, entirely without moral purpose — the sound of an artificer who forges no weapon of justice, but simply bends the light for the brutal, idle pleasure of watching it bend.

But this time, beneath the laughter's constant peal, Miguel heard something else. A second vibration, low, broken, and plaintive. It was not the hammer's echo; it was an off-key note pressing up from below, a lament of salt and old stone that seemed to weep against the maker's joy. The harder Miguel tried to isolate that second voice to give it a name, the more it slipped through his fingers, merging with his own dread of witnessing an absolute that cared nothing for his faith, his laws, or his own existence.

He woke with a muffled gasp that tasted of chalk in his throat, the bedclothes soaked in a cold sweat that clung to his ribs like a second armor. He lay still, staring at the dark ceiling of his chambers, waiting for the hammering in his own chest to settle back into a rhythm he recognized as his. His hands, when he raised them to the room's pale light, were a general's: weathered, marked by the bronze of the reins and the rust of campaigns. The relief of finding they were still his own was a pang so sharp he felt ashamed of his own relief.

Across the chamber, Solmire rested in its wooden stand.

The sword was not asleep. It gave off a pale, steady, flat glow that didn't return the room's twilight but seemed to digest it. Its low hum filled the alcove's space so completely it left no seam for the air to move freely. Miguel approached slowly, barefoot on the cold flagstones, and stopped a hand's width from the blade without daring to touch the metal. For months he had worn this sword as proof of his grace, the solid vindication of his march into the desert. Tonight the metal rested there like a tenant of starlight who refused to leave him alone, demanding that he bend his spine and his breath to a song he did not know how to sing.

A soft, hesitant knock landed on the heavy wood of his outer door. Miguel tensed, recognizing the rhythm at once; it was a call that carried none of an alarm's urgency, only the caution of the brother who had spent three nights cauterizing his wings on burning ground. He opened it to find Gabriel standing in the corridor, still dressed as though he had come straight from his own chambers.

"I felt something, Miguel," Gabriel's voice said from the corridor, filtering through the seams of the stonework along with the night's cold. "Through what's left... of what still runs between us. I didn't know if I should come."

Miguel thought the admission had cost him something; he heard it in how long Gabriel let the silence sit before finding the rest of the sentence.

Miguel stood motionless in the center of the room, looking at the disturbed sheets. Love for his brother was there, but pride came treading on its heels: he could not let the Herald of Resonance, the angel who read the truth in every voice, discover that the General of the Heavenly Host was having nightmares in which his own sword's maker mocked his laws.

They stood there a moment, neither finding the next thing to say, the space between them oddly formal for two brothers who had once shared everything without asking permission. Not so many years ago, Gabriel would simply have walked in and sat at the foot of the bed, and Miguel would have let him without a second thought. Gabriel's eyes moved once, briefly, past Miguel's shoulder toward the disturbed sheets, then returned to his face without comment. Whatever bond still ran between them, thinned by months of unnamed distance, had carried enough of tonight's terror across its fraying thread to bring his brother to this door in the middle of the night.

Gabriel did not ask directly what Miguel had dreamed. He stood in the doorway instead, searching Miguel's face for an opening he didn't seem to know how to request outright.

Miguel did not offer him one. "It was nothing, Gabriel," he said, his voice forced into firmness, coming out flatter and colder than he intended. "A misalignment of exhaustion. Go back to your chambers."

Gabriel held his gaze a moment longer, as if weighing whether to press past the closed door Miguel had just set between them. "You know you can tell me," he said, quietly, an offer made without much confidence it would be accepted.

Miguel said nothing further. Then Gabriel nodded once, a small, resigned motion, and stepped back into the corridor. He left without another word.

The silence that followed through the wood was long, a held breath that Gabriel let settle before his footsteps sounded again on the corridor's flagstones, fading with a slowness that tasted of resignation. Miguel stayed with his palm pressed against the cold wood, understanding, with his pulse beating in his throat, that every lie he used to protect his legend was leaving him a little more alone at the heart of his own Bastion.

The need to speak became physical, a pull that forced his feet to move through the empty corridors before his mind decided where they were headed. The Bastion lay steeped in a low glow that never quite went out, a twilight of cold stone where his bootless steps produced no echo at all.

He reached Raphael's wing. He remembered the conversation from months before, after Zaphor'el's purification, when the healer had warned him, with his emerald eyes, that the plaza's ground had been left barren, stripped of every memory of life. At that council, Miguel had wrapped himself in his General's armor, answering with the easy certainty of a man who has a weapon to prove his decisions right. *To save the garden*, he had told him, *the earth must be scorched.*

Now, standing before Raphael's door, the lie of that certainty crumbled in his throat. Raising his hand to knock on the infirmary's wood meant something more than asking for help; it meant kneeling before the healer, admitting the sword was no blessing and that the forge's laughter was devouring him from within. Miguel had been the pillar everyone came to when Heaven's ground trembled; he did not know how to reverse the direction of that debt, did not know how to be the one who begs for mercy.

He let his hand fall slowly. He turned in the corridor's silent stillness and began the long walk back toward his sealed chambers, carrying with him the one out-of-tune truth he would not dare share with anyone.
