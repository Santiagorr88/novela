# Chapter 6 — Whispers in the Burning Halls

Court, on an ordinary evening, ran on a grinding sort of dullness that smelled of cold ash, cheap ink, and the coarse sweat of demons who had spent centuries disputing the same border garrisons. Belial watched it from his customary place near the dais. His black armor, which seemed to breathe with a slow, invisible pulse, drew its plates together with a faint hiss that kept the lesser lords a prudent ten paces away. Before Lucifer's empty throne, two minor dukes argued bitterly over toll rights on a sulfur caravan, each affecting a ceremonial contempt that fooled no one anymore. All of it was a threadbare theater, a small war of ledgers that reset itself every season without anything actually changing.

Then the great bronze doors at the far end opened, and the small politics of Dis dissolved into sudden silence.

The survivor who crawled down the central aisle no longer looked like a soldier. His armor hung in pieces, his left wing dragged along the floor like a grease-stained rag, and his breath came in such uneven measures that the air itself seemed to cost him a physical effort. He collapsed to his knees before the dais without waiting for leave, his eyes bloodshot and fixed on nothing.

"It wasn't a battle," he whispered, and his voice, cracked and scoured raw by sulfur, filled the hall like a file rasping over iron. "It was an unmaking. Miguel's sword didn't cut. It left no flesh to heal, no bodies to bury. The light simply passed... and where a comrade had stood, there was nothing left. No scream, no trace, no memory that blood had ever been spilled there."

A ready, shrill laughter spread at once among the lords of the front, relieved to treat the wounded soldier's panic as a simple lie meant to frighten the recruits. Foras, standing a few paces from Belial with his cloak of violet mist hanging motionless on his shoulders, tilted his many gold eyes toward the survivor with a pity that looked like contempt.

"Ghost stories," Foras said, his voice flat and dry, carrying none of the Council's urgency. "Good enough to frighten those who still need to believe in Heaven's victory so they don't tremble on watch."

Belial did not turn toward him, holding the survivor's gaze, feeling the first pull of a cold certainty settle beneath his ribs.

"Then be glad you believe in nothing, Foras," Belial said, his voice low and unbreakable, cooling the air of the hall at once. "You'll have nothing to lose when everything you've built turns out to be the next piece of reality that sword decides to erase."

Later, alone in his private chambers, Belial allowed himself the anger he had not shown in the throne room.

His walls held the accumulated trophies of a very long career — banners taken from broken legions, weapons stripped from rivals who no longer had use for them, a throne carved, deliberately and without apology, from the bones of the last commander who had seriously challenged his position. He had walked past these trophies ten thousand times without a second glance, and tonight he found himself studying each one in turn, as though counting them might settle something the report had unsettled. Every object in this room existed to remind him, and anyone who entered it, exactly what he was. He had built that reputation inch by inch, century by century, and he did not intend to watch it made irrelevant by a sword he had not even seen.

This was not, he told himself, about Heaven's war effort. Heaven's war effort had never much interested him one way or another — territories changed hands, generals rose and fell, and none of it touched the deeper contest that actually mattered to him. This was a challenge laid at his own feet, whether its bearer knew it or not — a power operating by rules Belial did not control, in a war he had spent an eternity mastering the rules of. He would not be rendered obsolete by something he did not yet understand. He intended to understand it, no matter what that required.

He crossed to the throne of bone and sat, letting the familiar weight of the room settle back around him the way it always did when his composure needed rebuilding. On any other night it would have been enough. It worked less well than usual tonight.

He remembered, unbidden, a humiliation centuries old and never quite healed — an early campaign, long before his current rank, when a rival commander had dismissed his tactics in open council and won the following battle through sheer force of numbers alone, making Belial's careful strategy look like cowardice dressed as cleverness. He remembered the specific silence that had followed that battle, the way the other officers had avoided his eyes for weeks afterward, more clearly than he remembered most victories from the same era. He had eventually surpassed that rival and buried the memory beneath centuries of victories since, and had not thought of the man's face in longer than he could easily calculate.

That old contempt returned to him now, faint but unmistakable, the way it always did whenever anything threatened to make him small again. He set his jaw against it and let it harden into resolve. He would not be made small again. Not by a sword, and not by whatever contempt Belial had heard beneath Foras's remark.

Belial found Lucifer where he usually found him when the court grew too loud for his taste: in the immense library of the west wing, a space of shadows so tall the ceiling dissolved into a twilight of black stone. Endless rows of shelves held thousands of treatises whose skin-bound pages had not been opened in three ages, and the air there hung thick with a mineral dust that seemed to refuse to settle on the moldings.

Lucifer sat at his worktable, lit only by the amber glow of a sacred oil lamp. His long, pale fingers held a fine bronze needle, adjusting the gears of an open astrolabe, a metal cosmos that turned uselessly in the air of the room with a slow, unhurried ticking. His hair, the color of silvered frost, fell over his shoulders, and his eyes, the deep red of spilled wine, stayed fixed on the small machine.

"You've heard the report from the front by now," Belial said, planting himself before the table without preamble, his black armor hissing with a taut vibration. "What is that sword?"

Lucifer did not look up from the gear. With a leisureliness that seemed to mock the general's urgency, he set a small bronze wheel in place, savoring the silence before deciding it was worth breaking with words.

"There are old songs about such things, Belial," Lucifer said, his voice silken and slow, finding its own echo in the emptiness of the library. "Songs older than this war — or so claimed those who still knew how to sing them, before we tore out their tongues." He turned a page of a neighboring book with a dry rasp of parchment. "Some notes are best left unsung."

"That's not an answer," Belial growled, his tattoos flaring with a taut, violet light beneath his skin.

Lucifer closed the book with the same deliberate care he gave every movement, and finally raised his wine-dark eyes to the general, a slow, idle smile curling at the corners of his mouth.

"No," Lucifer agreed, turning back to his astrolabe with absolute detachment. "It isn't. But it's the only truth that will do you any good today, general." The gears began turning again beneath his hands, constant, cold, unhurried.

Belial left the library with more questions than he had entered it carrying, and beneath the questions, a suspicion he could not shake: that Lucifer knew more than he was saying, and had chosen, for reasons of his own, to say nothing at all. He did not ask a third time, nor a fourth. He had learned long ago that pressing Lucifer past the point he intended to answer never produced more truth, only a more elaborate way of declining to give it.

The Tower of the Eternals rose in a forgotten corner of Dis like a needle of black iron, humming faintly with a vibration that belonged to no demon's notion of time. Belial climbed the spiral stair, feeling the air thicken with each landing, heavy with unresolved paradoxes and the chalky smell of pages disintegrating in the shadow. On the seventh landing, a door of solid iron blocked his way, guarded by the tower's first ward: a seal of static light that waited for a toll before it would yield the threshold.

Belial turned to the minor demon following him. It was one of the countless nameless creatures that swarmed the citadel's lower levels, a servant with ashen skin and sagging shoulders, brought specifically for this moment. There were no screams, no struggle, none of the ordinary haste of cruelty. In the logic of Dis, the servant understood that its body was a tool, and that becoming the key to a forbidden door was the closest it would ever come to its existence carrying any measure of worth.

The servant stepped forward, looked at Belial one last time with dull eyes that had already given up on hope, and pressed its flat palms against the cold metal of the seal.

The ward took it instantly.

It was not hot fire that consumed it, but a pale, glacial, mineral light that climbed its arms, turning its flesh to translucent stone in a single heartbeat. Its shape froze mid-step, its mouth opening on a scream that never managed to produce a single physical sound in the stillness of the tower. The servant became a statue of gray crystal, its eyes fixed on nothing, bearing the weight of the threshold on its stone shoulders.

Belial walked past the statue without brushing it, without sparing it a second glance, already reaching his long, thin-fingered hand toward the forbidden shelves of the archive that finally opened before him.
