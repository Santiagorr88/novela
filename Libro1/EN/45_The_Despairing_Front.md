# Chapter 45 — The Despairing Front

Camael held the center of the line with the Unbroken arrayed on either side of him, veterans every one, disciplined past the point where fear had ever found lasting purchase in any of them. Weapons steady, formation unbroken, every soldier along this stretch of the defensive perimeter held the place centuries of drill had taught them to hold, waiting on a fight their years in the line had trained them to want.

He felt no dread in the waiting, only the familiar, coiled readiness that preceded every battle he had led them into. Whatever Belial intended to throw against this line, Camael trusted the Unbroken to meet it the way they'd met every threat before — force answered by greater force, an equation that had held, without exception, across this war.

The line stretched further than any glance could take in, ranks of veterans shoulder to shoulder along ground the Unbroken had held through engagement after engagement, never once yielding a position they'd been ordered to keep. Camael had built this legion himself, soldier by soldier, across centuries of relentless training and hard-fought campaigns, until the name Unbroken stopped being an aspiration and became simply accurate. He walked the line now with the same restless energy he brought to any wait before a fight, checking formation, checking morale, finding both exactly as expected.

He had faced worse odds than tonight's numbers suggested, had held ground against forces far larger than whatever Belial was massing beyond the horizon. His spear hummed faintly in his grip, eager in the way it always grew before a fight worth having, and Camael allowed himself, for the last calm moment before whatever came next, the simple satisfaction of a soldier standing exactly where he belonged, surrounded by soldiers he trusted absolutely.

Jeremiel worked a whetstone along Iustitia's edge a few paces from Camael's position, his motions easy and unbothered, attention split between the blade and the mood of the soldiers near him. "If Hell's cooks are half as bad as ours," he said, loud enough for the nearest ranks to catch it, "Belial's army will surrender from the food alone before we ever raise a weapon." A ripple of laughter moved through the soldiers nearest him, tension easing by some small, measurable degree despite everything massing beyond the horizon.

Camael let the moment pass without correction, though under any other circumstance the discipline he demanded would have made the joke a distraction worth silencing. He understood Jeremiel's timing too well to interrupt it. Morale was Jeremiel's craft the way force was Camael's own, and the laughter moving through the ranks told him plainly enough that the craft was working.

He had served alongside Jeremiel long enough to recognize the pattern by now — a joke here, a quiet word there, timed to the exact moment a formation's nerve needed steadying. Camael commanded through force, because force was the tool his nature had given him. Jeremiel commanded through something quieter, and Camael had long since stopped questioning why the Unbroken held their nerve through engagements that broke legions twice their size. Half of it was training. The other half, he suspected, was standing this close to a man who could make weary soldiers laugh on the edge of a battle Camael had no honest guarantee any of them would survive.

Jeremiel glanced up from the whetstone, his expression shifting into something more private once the soldiers around them had returned to their own preparations. "Are you afraid?" he asked, quiet enough that the question belonged only to the two of them.

Camael considered the question with the same seriousness he gave any question Jeremiel meant in earnest. "Fear is just another enemy. I haven't given this one a name yet." Jeremiel huffed something that might have been a laugh and returned to the whetstone, and Camael felt, in the silence that followed, a gratitude he rarely let himself acknowledge for the steadiness Jeremiel brought to every line he stood beside.

He didn't say the rest of what the question had stirred in him — that naming an enemy was, for Camael, most of the work of defeating it, and that some part of him had spent the walk to this line searching, without success, for the shape of what waited on the other side. Belial he could name. Belial he understood, in the plain way one warrior understands another built for force and consequence. Lamentum, the primordial spear Belial had brought back from the Sepulcher, Camael had heard only fragments about, secondhand and unconfirmed — and found the uncertainty harder to sit with than any confirmed threat would have been.

The pulse arrived from the direction of Belial's advancing vanguard, without sound, without any physical force a shield or blade could meet. It moved through the ranks like a wave of pure hopelessness, indifferent to the discipline that had carried these soldiers through every threat they had faced together.

Camael felt it pass through his chest and braced against it by instinct, the same reflex that had carried him through every threat this war had thrown at him. It did not strike like a blow. It settled instead, cold and total, somewhere flesh and bone had no defense for, and for one disorienting moment the truth of it landed whole and unwelcome — exactly what Lamentum had been forged to do.

He held his footing against it through what he could only credit to centuries of discipline, though the effort cost him more than any physical exertion ever had. Along the line, he heard weapons shifting in grips that had held them steady only moments earlier, a low unease rippling outward through ranks with no precedent for this — wavering against a threat that hadn't even fully arrived yet.

Jeremiel went down first, tears already tracking silently down a face that had been laughing minutes earlier, the blade slipping from fingers that had held it steady through a hundred engagements as his knees found the ground without a sound. Camael saw him fall before he registered any other soldier faltering — the sight landing with a force no physical wound from Jeremiel could have carried.

He crossed the short distance between them on instinct, still expecting, out of old habit, that this would resolve the way every previous crisis on this line had resolved — a wound tended, a formation reformed, order restored through enough will and enough force. Jeremiel didn't look up as he approached, tears still falling in the same unbroken silence, his hands empty now of the blade that had rested in them a moment earlier. Whatever held him upright on his knees, it looked to Camael like nothing belonging to the man he'd spoken with only minutes before.

The collapse spread outward from there, soldier after soldier lowering weapons they had raised without hesitation only moments earlier, sinking down in tears that offered no visible cause and no visible enemy to explain them. Camael understood, his own formation crumbling in front of him, that whatever this was, it left the body entirely capable of standing while stripping away every reason a soldier had left to do so.

He counted, from reflex more than any usable purpose, the soldiers still upright against the ones already lost to whatever had moved through them. The ratio worsened by the second — veterans who had held this exact ground through bloodier engagements than tonight had proven so far, brought down now by something that hadn't required a single enemy soldier to cross the field between them.

He recognized several of the fallen even at this distance, faces he had trained personally, soldiers who had stood beside him through engagements that tested every ounce of courage this collapse had somehow bypassed entirely. Their tears fell in the same unbroken silence Jeremiel's had — no sobbing, no cries for help, only a quiet, total surrender to whatever grief had found its way into each of them.

Some, disarmed and unseeing in the middle of ground still contested by an advancing enemy, would not survive what came next. He could not yet spare the attention to grieve that fact. He could only watch it beginning to happen, powerless in a way his long service had never once prepared him to be.

Camael moved through the wreckage of his own formation, still standing by pure force of will, and did the only thing he'd ever known how to do in the face of a faltering line — he led. He seized two soldiers by the shoulders, hauling them bodily upright, shouting orders that had rallied broken formations more times than he could count, trusting the one thing that had always worked before — his own presence — to return to them whatever the wave had taken.

It did nothing. One of the soldiers, back on his knees within seconds of being forced upright, looked up at Camael with an expression that carried neither defiance nor cowardice — only what looked like a sorrow so complete it left no room for anything else. Camael felt something close to genuine fear take hold in him for the first time in the encounter — not fear of dying, a possibility he'd made peace with centuries ago, but fear that the one tool his existence had been built around, the sheer force of his own leadership, had simply stopped mattering against whatever this was.

He tried a second soldier, then a third, repeating the only approach he'd ever needed, through sheer refusal to accept the evidence at his feet. Each attempt ended the same way — a body raised briefly upright by his strength alone, collapsing back the moment that strength moved to the next soldier in reach. He had led broken formations back into cohesion more times than he could count, had rallied soldiers from far worse wounds than whatever held these men motionless now. None of that offered him anything useful against grief with no visible source and no visible end.

He released the soldier's shoulders slowly, understanding arriving with a weight heavier than any physical blow he had ever absorbed. Command meant nothing here. Courage meant nothing here. Whatever answered a threat like this, it wasn't going to be anything Camael carried in his own arsenal.

He stood there a moment longer amid his broken formation, spear silent and useless in his grip, and felt something in himself harden into a resolve colder than the fury that usually carried him through a losing engagement. He could not out-force what had just moved through this line. He understood that now, in a way no report or warning could have taught him. What remained to him was smaller than he would have liked, and he intended to use every piece of it regardless.

He forced the understanding into something he could still act on, some fragment of his old resolve reasserting itself against everything he'd just watched fail. He could not fix what had already happened to Jeremiel and the others scattered across the churned ground around him. He could, at minimum, make certain the rest of Heaven's command understood exactly what they were facing.

He dispatched the message himself, unwilling to trust its weight to anyone who hadn't just watched it happen firsthand: *They are not breaking our lines. They are breaking our souls.* He sent it knowing it would reach the Bastion within the hour, certain no report delivered from a distance could fully capture what this front had just endured.

He looked once more across the ground his legion had held minutes earlier, soldiers scattered in postures no training had prepared them for, Jeremiel still among them somewhere in the churned dark. Whatever remained of the Unbroken tonight would need more than his force of will to hold together through what came next. He gathered what soldiers still answered to command, formed what defense the remaining line could offer, and held his position anyway, because holding was the one thing he still knew how to do.

The Unbroken had earned their name across centuries of holding ground no other legion could hold. Tonight, for the first time in all that history, Camael understood exactly how close that name had come to no longer meaning anything at all.
