# Chapter 50 — Sparks for the Numb

The Host's wounded who could still bleed had at least one physical destination to turn to within the Bastion's common wards. The Temple of Shattered Light did honor to its own name, consecrated beneath an immense vault of fractured crystal that the original builders had designed so that celestial clarity would fall across the flagstones in broken, prismatic sheets, rather than project itself in one single, dazzling clean shaft. Anael had walked beneath that splintered glass roof a thousand mornings before this campaign, and even with the urgency of mourning settled over the courtyard, she caught herself lifting her gaze out of pure habit, watching the old colors of the broken windows still doing exactly the same geometric work they always had.

The Bastion's central sanctuary held wards perfectly prepared for iron's ordinary casualties: cots arranged in symmetrical rows, where Raphael's apprentices moved with the readiness and competence of people who trusted their own bandages, taking fevers and tending soldiers' wounds on a strict, military schedule.

But the soul-wounded crowding Anael's galleries presented nothing so simple that a surgeon could mend it with chalk or salve. Those soldiers lay motionless in their respective cots, breathing regularly, hearts steady beneath the cloth, but that same bodily integrity meant nothing against the void governing their stares. A physician of the Bastion knew how to close an honest cut from a demonic blade; but none of them had yet been taught, in the Scriptorium, what to do with a sentinel whose eyes tracked no movement in the room, whose hands took the feeding spoon only when a caregiver's finger guided the fist, or whose own name, spoken directly into the hollows of his face, produced no more reaction than the dull sound of drizzle striking a shuttered window.

---

Anael passed the first cot of the eastern row without breaking her methodical pace, glancing sidelong at a young soldier who hadn't moved from her position in nine days, her breathing so shallow it barely stirred the folds of the grey blanket covering her chest. Anael had learned, at the cost of her own fatigue, to visit the gallery's gravest cases at the end of the day rather than the beginning, understanding that grief this deep and this stripped of light answered to nothing offered too early, while her own reserve of patience and spirit hadn't yet had time to refill in the temple's silence.

She knelt instead beside the third cot of the eastern row, where a soldier of the Adamant Guard lay with his eyelids open and fixed on some bland point of the ceiling's masonry that held nothing anyone else in the room could register.

She did not lift the Lyre of Light right away; in fact, she never made that gesture on first contact. She limited herself instead to listening, in the column's half-light: the ragged, dry, uneven catch of the wounded man's breathing, the small trembling hitch that preceded each of his exhalations, and the rhythm of a body that seemed to have forgotten it had permission to relax its muscles against the straw mattress.

Anael knew that hope offered with too much haste only sounded like a lie or a decoration to a mind that had already decided, in the mud of the plain, that its war was over and lost forever. She had already made that mistake with the war's first wounded, watching them flinch at her chords and pull back from the Lyre's clarity with the same physical revulsion a burned hand shows a second flame.

Only once she felt the soldier's breathing steady enough to answer the rhythm of her own — once, twice, three times in the silence of the flagstones — did Anael let the first note of the Lyre of Light rise into the air. It was no brighter or louder tone than the wounded man's own stillness, but a subtle, sustained chord aligned so precisely with his own breathing that it could easily have been mistaken for an echo of his chest rather than a gift laid upon the sill.

The soldier's eyes did not stray from the beam, nor did his chest alter the measure of his breath. Anael held the note a while longer anyway; the temple had taught her, over these last weeks of retreat, that the absence of response in a veteran of the cloth was not the same as the absence of listening, and she let the room's ensuing silence absorb its own small failure before rising, knees stiff from the granite, and moving on to the next cot in the row.

No one had trained her for this particular labor. All her prior service had been built around the order of battle lines, walking the ranks before a charge so that courage would catch from mail-coat to mail-coat like struck flint catching dry grass at the border. That soldier's trade had a visible, countable, clear ending: a position held on the line, or a charge that didn't break against Dis's iron. This work, by contrast, had no such edge to measure the staff's effectiveness against; it simply continued, cot after cot, gallery after gallery, and she had learned to stop asking the crystal to resolve into anything cleaner than the ordinary mourning of the cots.

---

Barachiel found her between the fourth row of cots and the fifth, holding a half-unrolled sheet of parchment against his forearm that carried the day's reassignment lists. Anael noticed that the communications captain's usual eloquence seemed entirely dampened this morning, showing a deeper wear than she'd seen him register across the last three weeks of watch. Even tired and with his cloth damp, Barachiel still moved through the sick gallery with that same distinction of a man accustomed to being listened to in the Bastion's courtyards, though his features betrayed that today he would have preferred any other messenger to carry the report.

"They've reassigned the wards again," Barachiel said, without preamble, and though he tried to keep the words in their usual musical cadence, the tone came out thin, threaded through with exhaustion, because the courtesy of a preamble had become a luxury none of them in the temple could afford any longer during the vigil. "Raphael's people can't carry both casualty lists at once — the ones bleeding from iron, and the ones not answering the channel of control. Bastion command wants the second list to pass entirely into your hands."

He held out the soldier's parchment, not quite meeting her eyes, like a man offering a charge he suspects is cruel to give a fellow watchman.

"There are forty-one names on it this morning," Barachiel added, glancing away toward the damp flagstones. "The offices are calling it an honor. I don't think anyone in the courtyard actually believes that, least of all the clerk who had to write it down in the Scriptorium."

Anael took the cold military parchment from his hands but chose not to unroll it. She already knew well enough, without reading the ink, roughly how long the accounting of grief would run; she had watched the number of soul-casualties climb by twos and threes every week since the Horn of Retreat sounded, and had long since stopped hoping the figure would shrink on the council's crystal screens.

"It isn't an honor, Barachiel," Anael said, and the softness of her voice held the plainness of the sentence without needing to raise itself for the stone to return the truth of its edges. "It's simply true. Nothing Raphael's hands, or his apprentices', can mend reaches these men. Mine, with the Lyre of Light, might. That isn't an honor; it's simply the only shape the work can take in this corner of the Bastion."

"I know," Barachiel answered, hesitating, and for one brief instant something of his old, persuasive herald's warmth surfaced through his features, aimed at her in the privacy of the column. "I only meant to say — that I'm deeply sorry this list is yours alone to carry, Anael. Forty-one soul-wounded is too many for one instrument and two hands."

"It was never going to be anyone else's," she answered, rolling the parchment closed again and tucking it against her side without breaking stride toward the far end of the gallery, letting him return to his own task of murmuring old communications prayers toward squads already falling too far from the Bastion to hear them, because neither of them had time to spare comforting each other over a decision command had already made on paper.

---

Anael found Ezihel in the narrow chalk-and-supplies alcove that opened just behind the west gallery, sitting in silence on an old overturned wooden crate near the shelves of ointments. Lumen's small bronze bell lay inert between her bare hands; she wasn't ringing it, but she wasn't putting it back in its case either, only turning it slowly over her fingertips, as though the metal's own weight might tell her something her own thoughts couldn't resolve on the sill.

"You've been out here longer than a break allows, Ezihel," Anael said, settling onto a second overturned crate across from her, close enough to the corner that their knees nearly touched in the half-light.

Ezihel didn't look up from the bell right away. When she finally did, her features showed that same firmness and control Anael had always associated with her service, held today by a tension she was noticeably having more trouble hiding under the tallow lamp's light.

"Lumen doesn't reach the worst of them," Ezihel said, and the tone came out flat, stripped of any vibration of comfort. "I ring it over the cots, I watch their faces... and nothing moves under the cloth. I've started wondering, Anael, whether I'm still ringing the bronze for them at all, or only because stopping would mean admitting to the Scriptorium that the tool has stopped working in my hands."

"And what would it mean, Ezihel, if it truly didn't work?" Anael asked, holding her patience in place.

"It would mean some of them are already gone entirely, that the soul has slipped out of them somewhere on the ash plain. It would mean I'm ringing a bronze bell over an entirely empty room and calling it care."

Ezihel's fist closed harder around Lumen's handle, her knuckles going pale around the tempered metal.

"You've had weeks longer at this gallery than I have," she added, and her eyes sought Anael's with an urgency that admitted no ornament. "Tell me honestly — does it ever stop feeling like this?"

Anael let the question hang in the alcove's damp air before offering an answer, understanding that her fellow soldier deserved better than a manual comfort assembled too quickly to sound earned.

"No," Anael said, gently. "It doesn't stop feeling like this, Ezihel. I still walk out of some rooms in the east gallery wondering exactly what you're wondering right now, facing that bronze."

She reached out her pale hand and rested it briefly on the bell, stilling its turning, though the tool hadn't been moving at all.

"But our staff was never meant to cure them, Ezihel. Neither was your bell Lumen. That was never what command sent either of us into these wards to do. The work was never 'wake them' from Lamentum's wave. It was simply 'make sure none of them go through this mourning alone, whether they wake before dawn or not.' If that's all your bronze has bought today for a single cot in the west gallery, then it hasn't failed on the sill. It's done exactly the one work Lumen was ever consecrated for, in the first age."

Ezihel's jaw tightened under the lamp's flickering light, and for a moment Anael thought the girl would press her objection further. Instead, she simply nodded, once, and tucked the bronze bell back against her side without adding a single word to the truce, which was its own quiet form of answer.

"Drink some water before you go back into the ranks," Anael told her, rising from the wooden crate. "The bell will ring considerably steadier if your throat isn't so dry."

It was a small, ordinary instruction, almost careless in its plainness, but she said it with the same care she offered any note of her Lyre in the east gallery: a simple fact offered with just enough lightness that Ezihel could receive it without the burden of feeling comforted in her weakness.

---

Anael returned to the soldier of the Adamant Guard near the end of her round, more from an old watchman's habit than from any real expectation that the sill had changed during her absence. She matched her wounded man's breath again, just as she had a dozen times already that afternoon, and let the first note of the Lyre of Light rise toward the ceiling beams.

For what felt, by the wounded man's breathing, like a long moment of silence, nothing in the soldier's body shifted; his mail lay motionless and his gaze fixed on the same point of masonry, exactly as it had through every prior hour of her census.

Then, with a mineral slowness, the fingers of his right hand curled, slowly, around the edge of the grey cloth blanket pulled up to his chest. It was not a movement toward Anael, nor toward the Lyre, nor toward anything she could name with a military term; it was only a small, deliberate, trembling closing of a hand that seemed, for that one impossible instant, to remember it still belonged to the body lying on the cot.

A sound rose from his throat afterward: a low, hoarse, broken, continuous tone that wasn't quite a word, but the empty shape a sentence leaves behind once whoever speaks it has lost most of what it used to mean at the Bastion's edge. Anael did not ask him to repeat the sound, nor did she search for its meaning in the Scriptorium's books, certain that any haste to interrogate the sign would only frighten whatever fragile thing had just surfaced back down beneath the cloth.

She simply held the Lyre's note a while longer, keeping the frequency tempered and steady, and let her own hand rest, light and easy, over the soldier's closed fist, allowing herself, for the length of that single sustained chord, to call it the first real spark the wounded man had produced since the slamming of the Empyrean Gates.

---

By morning, however, the soldier was once again completely still. It wasn't the stillness of fingers closing around the cloth blanket, but the old stillness from before: eyelids open, gaze fixed and motionless on the same unmarked spot of ceiling, breath gone back to its ragged, uneven, dry catch, as though the previous night's chord had never crossed the gallery's space at all.

Anael knelt beside the cot for a long while without raising the Lyre of Light from her side, without trying to force the note again before the soldier's breath was ready to receive it, letting the moment's disappointment weigh in her own chest exactly what it had to weigh, without dressing the absence up as either heraldic ornament or tragedy.

The wounded man hadn't gotten worse; he simply hadn't stayed on the surface of the cloth. Anael forced herself to hold both facts in her mind at once, refusing the comfort of the easier version of either: she refused to call the previous evening's closing of the hand meaningless because it hadn't lasted until dawn, and she refused to call this morning a defeat large enough to force her to set the list down. Some sparks caught in the grey mud of the ash; most of them, she was learning at a mineral cost, failed to hold past a single dawn, and no amount of will on her part had yet changed that arithmetic by even a single name of those Barachiel had handed her on the parchment. That was the actual shape of the work the retreat had left in her hands, stripped of whatever kindness the census had tried to wrap around it. She rose from the flagstones eventually, because forty other cots still waited on her round, and left the soldier with the blanket faintly disturbed at the edge where his hand had once, briefly, remembered it was his.

She found Ezihel again well into the night, in the same alcove of ointments, Lumen's bell resting inert in her lap and the candles burned down to rancid tallow. Ezihel carried an exhaustion in her shoulders that Anael didn't need to ask about to identify: the grief of an entire round spent watching faces that had not returned a single sign of life to the bronze. Anael didn't ask for her gallery's report; she simply sat down beside her on the wooden crate, very close to her, letting the Lyre rest silently across both their knees without offering any more words, understanding that her companion had already been given too many useless sentences over the course of the day.

"It doesn't get easier, Ezihel," Anael said, quietly, and the tone carried none of her earlier mockery, only the plainness of an honest truth. "I'm not going to tell you that it does. But you come back tomorrow anyway. The work is only that; not the bell's results, only the bronze's persistence over the stone."

She let her shoulder rest against Ezihel's, a small and wordless weight in the middle of the closed sanctuary.

"I'll be here in the morning too," Anael added, watching the stained-glass window's fading light. "And so will you, if I know you at all."

Ezihel let out a long, unsteady, trembling sigh, the first sound of cloth Anael had heard her produce without it being held in place by the rigor of her own captain's discipline, and let her forehead rest, briefly, against Anael's shoulder, sharing the cold horseshoe-ache of her grief in the middle of the night.

---

Dawn found Anael already moving down the first row of the east gallery, the Lyre of Light warm against her palm from a night that had given her exactly one closed fist and one lost murmur to carry forward into the next, against forty other names that had given her nothing at all. She did not know, starting the round again, which of the soldiers ahead of her would answer and which would not. She had stopped expecting to know that in advance weeks ago, the same week she had stopped expecting the list to grow shorter.

She knelt beside the first cot, listened for the broken rhythm of a stranger's breath, and waited for it to steady enough to answer hers before she let the first note rise — small, patient, offered without any promise attached to it, into hands still too numb, most mornings, to ask for anything at all.
