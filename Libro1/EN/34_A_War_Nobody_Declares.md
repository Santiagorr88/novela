# Chapter 34 — A War Nobody Declares

Malthus found Stolas exactly where his own informants had placed him, standing at the edge of Threll's household with the confidence of a man who no longer felt any need to hide his purpose. The confirmation stung more than Malthus cared to admit, even to himself. He had suspected the wavering loyalty was real. Seeing Vepar's own instrument standing openly in the middle of it, already finished with the business that had brought him here, made the suspicion far harder to hold at arm's length.

Malthus drew Maleth's chalk from beneath his sleeve and marked the first line of the circle at his feet without yet committing to closing it. "You've been busy." Stolas regarded him with a dry, unbothered attention that struck Malthus as too composed for a man just caught in the act. "Busy is one word for it. Thorough is the one I'd choose myself." Malthus felt his own composure sharpen past the point he preferred to hold it at. "A ward broken doesn't stay broken forever, Stolas. What one hand seals, another hand can seal again — you've only bought yourself a little time, not the ground itself."

"Ground your own household forgot existed," Stolas said, unruffled, as though the distinction mattered more than the trespass itself. "I only found what was already lying open, Malthus. If you'd rather I hadn't, perhaps someone should have kept better watch over what belonged to you in the first place." The observation struck exactly where Stolas had meant it to. Malthus recognized the uncomfortable truth folded into it and had no intention of admitting it aloud.

"That's not an answer," Malthus said. "It's a justification. There's a difference, whatever your grimoire tells you." Stolas's expression didn't change, though his posture shifted, weight settling more evenly — a stillness Malthus read as a man no longer expecting this to end without violence. "Then let's not waste more words on the distinction. You came here for a confrontation. I'd rather not disappoint you."

Stolas raised the grimoire before Malthus had finished the circle, its pages already parting to whatever malediction its opening promised. Malthus abandoned the half-drawn ring and traced a rapid sigil in the air instead, ash and blood gathering from nowhere and everywhere at once into a shield-glyph that hardened just as Stolas's spell reached it. The malediction struck the glyph and unraveled rather than broke it, time bleeding backward off its surface, leaving the air around them briefly, visibly wrong.

Malthus felt the strain of the shield-glyph even after it held — a familiar cost, a small portion of his own vitality spent to keep the malediction's unraveling from spreading further into the courtyard. He had no time to dwell on it. Stolas was already reforming his stance, the grimoire's pages settling into a new configuration, and Malthus read the shift as a scholar pressing his advantage, not retreating from a strike that had failed to land cleanly.

Malthus dropped back to the unfinished circle and closed it, completing the ring Stolas's opening strike had interrupted, Maleth's full pattern flaring to life beneath his boots. The circle answered instantly, drawing on the same discipline that had sealed Corvin's pact days before — this time bent toward binding rather than loyalty, meant to hold Stolas fast, not simply defend against him. The grimoire answered a heartbeat later, a ward turning the binding sigil's own power back against itself, forcing Malthus to abandon the working half-formed. He circled Stolas across the household's open courtyard, testing his opponent's discipline as much as his raw power, unwilling yet to commit to a strike that would make retreat impossible for either of them.

Neither of them spoke during the exchange, the silence between spells carrying its own weight, each combatant reading the other's stance for any opening the last failed strike might have left. Malthus had faced skilled opponents before, soldiers whose strength lay in raw aggression, in the crude arithmetic of force against force. Stolas fought nothing like that. Every casting arrived measured, deliberate, calculated for maximum effect at minimum exposure — the style of a man unwilling to rely on anything as unreliable as courage alone.

A third exchange followed, faster than the first two, Malthus driving a sigil of unmaking directly at the grimoire itself rather than at Stolas, hoping to force the scholar to choose between defending his weapon and defending himself. Stolas chose neither cleanly, twisting aside at the last possible moment while the grimoire absorbed only a glancing edge of the working, its pages flickering dark for an instant before settling. The cost showed plainly on Stolas's face for the first time since the confrontation began — a flicker of something Malthus read as cracks beneath the composure his opening remarks had promised.

"Enough."

The single word carried weight enough to still Malthus mid-motion — Foras's presence filling the courtyard's edge with the serene authority Malthus had served under for centuries and never once seen genuinely challenged. Foras did not raise his voice further. He rarely needed to. "Faith rots quickest when replaced by pride, Malthus — and Vepar is only waiting for ours to blind us badly enough to hand him the excuse he needs. Don't give it to him. Contain this with patience, not with rage; patience is the one superiority no one can ever take from you."

Malthus lowered the half-formed sigil still hovering at his fingertips, the heat of the confrontation cooling rapidly beneath his lord's quiet reproach. He understood the correction even as it stung — not weakness, but a deliberate refusal to hand an enemy exactly the justification it was searching for. He watched Stolas across the courtyard, unwilling to look away first, and saw the other Captain make no further move either, the grimoire closing itself without any visible instruction.

Stolas felt the confrontation end the instant Foras's voice reached the courtyard — its quality of command settling the matter more thoroughly than any further exchange of spells could have. He had not come here for a fight, whatever the last several minutes had produced. He had come for confirmation, and confirmation — Threll's household now openly declaring where its loyalty lay — he had already gotten, well before Malthus arrived to interrupt the process.

He allowed himself, standing there in the confrontation's abrupt aftermath, a brief, honest assessment of how close the exchange had run. Malthus fought with more precision than Stolas had expected from a mystic whose reputation rested primarily on ritual and parable — each sigil arriving faster and more cleanly aimed than his own research into the man had prepared him for. He tucked the observation alongside everything else he'd learned today, useful regardless of how the confrontation had resolved.

The exchange had cost him more than the earlier excavation had — the same familiar weariness the grimoire always extracted, only sharper this time. He had not expected to spend quite this much of himself on a confrontation he had not sought out. He accepted the cost anyway, the way he accepted every cost his gift demanded, and set it alongside the rest of the day's accounting without complaint.

He inclined his head toward Foras with the respect one commander's Captain generally showed another commander, and withdrew without offering Malthus anything further by way of explanation or apology. There was, in Stolas's own accounting, nothing left worth explaining. The excavation had already yielded everything it was going to yield. What remained now belonged to the two commanders whose court this quarrel concerned, not to the Captains who had merely carried out their instructions.

Malthus received the formal confirmation within the week, delivered through the same quiet channels that had first brought him word of the trouble — Threll's household had severed its remaining ties to Foras entirely, the old forgotten pact finally honored openly at last, no longer left to linger in ambiguous silence. Weighed against the raw strength of Foras's remaining household, it was a minor loss. It was also the first loss of its exact kind Foras's court had suffered in longer than Malthus could recall — a small, real crack where none had existed before.

He considered, reading the confirmation a second time, whether he might have prevented it had he moved a day or two sooner, had he brought the ward-seal fragment to Foras's attention the moment he found it. He did not linger on the question. Regret accomplished nothing that clear-eyed accounting couldn't accomplish better, and he had learned, across centuries in Foras's service, that dwelling on a decision already made rarely improved the decisions still to come.

He thought, too, of Corvin, and of the binding he had sealed only days before this matter came to a head — a reminder that loyalty, properly tended, could still be secured even in a court unsettled enough to lose men like Threll. One loyalty lost. One loyalty freshly bound. The accounting, weighed honestly, was not entirely one-sided, whatever the raw numbers might have suggested to anyone watching from outside.

He carried the news to his own quarters that evening, weighing it against everything the confrontation had cost and everything it had, in the end, prevented. Foras appeared undiminished in every way that mattered — his command intact, his position secure, his composure showing no visible sign of the doubt the court's whispers had suggested since Belial's return. The loss of a single wavering Squad Leader was a small sting next to the open war Foras's restraint had just quietly avoided.

Malthus reported the full accounting to Foras personally, the loss of Threll a quiet, bitter footnote to an otherwise contained afternoon. Foras received it without visible disappointment, the same serene composure he brought to everything settling over the news. "A small price," Foras said, "for keeping the larger war exactly where it belongs — against Heaven, not against ourselves." Malthus said nothing further, though he suspected, hearing it, that his lord's calm cost him something Foras would never openly admit to carrying.

He studied Foras a while longer, searching for any trace of that cost that might show itself, and found nothing — the same unshakable serenity Foras had worn through every crisis Malthus had ever watched him weather. His lord's composed stillness struck Malthus, standing there, as perhaps the discipline Foras had spent centuries perfecting, the very same discipline he had just demanded of Malthus in the courtyard — patience worn so thoroughly it no longer looked like effort from outside.

"You could have let me finish it," Malthus said finally, unable to set the thought aside. "Stolas would have paid for what he took." Foras's expression did not shift. "And Vepar would have had exactly the excuse he was hoping for. A dead Captain is a grievance no commander can simply set aside, however justified the killing actually was. I'd rather lose Threll than hand Vepar a reason to make this open." Malthus inclined his head, the logic settling into place beside everything else he already understood about why his lord had intervened when he did.

Neither side had claimed a clean victory, and neither, Malthus suspected, had been meant to. Whatever Stolas reported back to Vepar, Malthus understood at least his own half of what this brief, contained skirmish had revealed — that Hell's united front, unshaken through centuries of war against Heaven, had just shown its first real crack in the quiet, deniable space between two commanders who answered, nominally, to the same larger cause. It was a small crack. Malthus did not expect it to remain the only one.

He returned to his own chambers that night carrying the thought with him. Foras's restraint had held today, and had held for good reason. Perhaps restraint was not infinite, Malthus thought, not even in a commander as disciplined as the one he served. Somewhere ahead, in some confrontation neither he nor Foras could yet name, that patience might be tested beyond what even Foras's composure could absorb. Malthus intended to be ready long before that day arrived.
