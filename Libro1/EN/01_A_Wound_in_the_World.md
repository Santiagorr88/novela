# Chapter 1 — A Wound in the World

Miguel had been crossing Serephis for twelve days, and by now the desert no longer felt like ground but like the scorched corpse of a kingdom. The heat did not fall from that sickly sky; it rose out of the earth itself, where the sand had fused into sheets of black glass that splintered under his sabatons with the sound of dry bones breaking. Every stride cost him a small surrender of will to the cracked gold of his armor, plate no armorer in the Host had ever managed to weld shut since the Onyx Gates campaign. Under hair gone ash-white long before its time, his eyes still burned a faint blue. Recruits fresh to the Host swore they could feel him crossing a yard before they turned to look, the whole line straightening before anyone gave the order; here there was no line to straighten, only sand. The wind carried a taste of salt and spent lime, old and mineral, the aftertaste of some ruin he had not yet learned the shape of. He was the General of the Celestial Host, and he knew that kind of wound: the bite of demonic steel, the burn of infernal fire — wounds with names, wounds that closed.

Miguel understood that kind of war. He understood the logic of leaving nothing behind. But what drove him across the ash was not a map, and not a Council order. It was the hollow. A perfect cold, fist-sized, just beneath his sternum. His gauntleted hand rose to it on its own, a thousand times a day, seeking the touch of tempered steel against flesh that refused to remember why it was missing something. The pain was not a warning. It was a question driven into his own breath: *what was taken from me?*

The dune broke apart under his boot. Beneath it was stone.

He stopped. Not for the wound — the pull never changed — but because the stone was cut, not shattered. A paving block, its edge still square. He brushed the sand away with two fingers: a second block beside the first, a third beyond it. A buried road, running straight toward the same horizon he was already headed for.

Serephis had not always been empty.

He knew this the way he knew most things about the deep past: doctrine, not memory. A kingdom here, before the Fall. He had never learned its name. At some council, someone had told him the desert was not a ruin but a weapon: a people who could no longer win their war had unmade their own land rather than hand it over — drained the water table, salted the earth, and let the wind do the rest for a thousand years. He hadn't believed it then. It sounded like the kind of story armies tell each other to make retreat sound like strategy.

He believed it now, crouched over a road built for feet that had stopped walking it before his own kind had names.

For a moment he let himself see it whole: the road wide enough for ten abreast, worn to a shine down its center by feet that had crossed it every day for centuries; somewhere along its buried length, footings too broad for any single door, the ghost of a gate built to pass an army through at once rather than turn one back. Whoever raised this had not built to survive a retreat. They had built to never need one.

For a moment the ache in his chest answered the stone with a low, almost sympathetic throb, as though he recognized something of himself in it. He did not like the thought. He stood, did not look at the road again, and moved on.

For days the ache had been a dull throb, easy to bury under duty. Now it pulled in one direction, and it pulled with authority. Miguel knew that kind of certainty: it belonged to the Divine Word, to true command settling into the soul without asking permission. That this call carried the same certainty terrified him. He stopped and closed his eyes a moment. When he opened them, his body had already turned toward the pull. His mind took one more step to consent.

The stillness of the plain broke on a chord of harmony, a clean note that found its pitch in Serephis's thick, sulfurous air. Gabriel arrived the way the first gust of cool air arrives before a storm, his robe of blue and silver free of the armor's heaviness. Miguel felt love, and close behind it, the weight of his own pride: he did not want his brother to see him consumed, hunting a phantom ache across a wasteland that belonged to no one anymore. He straightened. The exhaustion would not be hidden. Gabriel showed no judgment. He showed worry, which was worse.

"The Council is concerned, Miguel," Gabriel said. His voice was gentle, the fraternal chord that always calmed the war councils, but there was an off-key tremor running through it now, a weariness Miguel had never heard from him in the war room. "They fear this is a deception, a lure the enemy crafted to draw you out. Your absence puts fear in the Host."

*Council. Duty. Fear.* The words arrived like news from another life. The ache flared anyway. Miguel straightened, feeling the pull of metal against his ribs.

"They think me a fool," Miguel said, his voice rough, like sand dragged by the wind. "Or a traitor."

"They think you are their general." Gabriel shifted his weight, and the motion revealed the parchment hidden against his robe. It was sealed in wax, black and dense, the formal mark of a Council order. Gabriel did not touch it. Did not name it. The stillness told Miguel that his brother preferred to be family rather than the executioner of an official order.

The shift from envoy to brother struck deeper than any order could have. For one breath, he forgot everything else.

He had lived a version of this moment before. That was why he could not make himself turn around.

Onyx Gates had cost him his wing. Miguel remembered the smell of burnt feathers, the fortress groaning as it sank into the abyss, and Gabriel — no one had sent him — kneeling at his side for three eternal nights, cauterizing the broken wing with nothing but his own light, talking the whole time about old songs and Uriel's temper so the silence would not be left free to decide that Miguel should die. "Silence," Gabriel had told him then, "is what lets a wound decide it's mortal."

Miguel asked him, once the fortress finally stopped groaning, why he hadn't sent a healer.

*Because you don't yell for help in the voice you use for people you love,* Gabriel had said. *You go yourself.*

He heard that voice now, under the pleading. It would have been easier if Gabriel had come as an envoy, all official grief and official warning. He had come as the brother who once spent three nights on a burning floor for him. That debt could not be outwalked.

The memory let him go and returned him to a different waste, with a different wound. His jaw, clenched for days, loosened for one second. Gabriel's gaze softened; his hand rose, hesitated in the flat desert air, and fell back, dead, to his side.

"Come home," Gabriel pleaded now. "Whatever this wound is, we face it together. As we always have."

For a moment Miguel wavered. The Bastion, home's familiar harmonies: a promise he had almost forgotten how to want. Then came the colder thought. Returning meant carrying this void forever. The Council would offer comfort, not answers; they would try to heal a wound they could not see. He had to go on. He had to *know*.

"I cannot." Miguel looked down at the polished stones of the buried road beneath his feet. "This call is a command more fundamental than any the Council can give me. It is a truth I must find, or I am nothing." He turned toward the pull. The choice had already made itself.

Gabriel did not argue. His light dimmed a fraction. A single tear of light traced his cheek, and he inclined his head. "May you find what you seek, brother." Then he was gone, folded back into the space between worlds, the sealed parchment still unmentioned against his ribs. Miguel stood looking at the empty space, wondering what exactly he had just been spared.

The heat and the silence returned, heavier than before. He let the regret surface once, fully, and set it aside. The choice was made. He marched on.

He walked for days through unchanging desert, until it changed without warning. One step on burning sand; the next, on cool moss, with a stillness that tasted of metal in the wake of a lightning strike. He had reached the grove. Trees with leaves of pale metal rose in perfect ranks, casting an even light that refused to throw any shadow at all. His tactical mind read the ground at once: *ambush*. Nothing in creation was this perfect, this stripped of the ordinary friction of life.

The silence watched. He tried to recite a verse of the Divine Song, the exercise he had centered himself with before a thousand battles, and could not recall the words. Every leaf that cracked under his boots sounded like a shout in a library. He felt measured, and did not know by whom, or for what.

He saw it first as a flaw in the light: one of the trees, some forty paces off, no longer matched the angle of the others. He turned and found a figure, tall as the trees themselves, made of the same pale material, its face a smooth surface without a single feature. It did not move. It raised no weapon; its posture said it needed none. Miguel had learned to read intention in the shoulders of ten thousand enemies before the first blow fell. Here he read nothing. Not hostility, not welcome. Only attention.

"I have come for what is mine," Miguel said, and heard, even as he said it, how little he knew of the claim he was making.

The figure did not answer. Its stillness was answer enough, and Miguel's certainty seemed suddenly small, and borrowed. He advanced toward the grove's center, waiting for the line he was not meant to cross. The line never came. The figure did not follow or block him: it turned, smooth and soundless, to keep facing him as he passed, like a compass needle. Miguel kept walking, that gaze resting on his back long after the trees hid it.

At the heart of the grove stood the great ash. Its bark had the texture of cooled lava, rough and black, and its roots drank from a pool that shimmered with a static light. Driven into the trunk was a sword. Not forged metal: living light that had chosen to have weight, an edge, a shape. The instant he saw it, the void in his chest went mute. It was not a healing; it was a sudden, absolute silence, as if someone had laid a hand over a ringing bell. The sword's name rose into his mind not as a memory but as a law that had always been there: *Solmire*. He did not think, *that is a sword*. He thought, with absolute and terrifying recognition: *that is what was taken from me.*

He stopped before the ash. His hand trembled as he raised it. For a heartbeat, the name *Miguel* felt like a borrowed title. His fingers reached for the hilt. Behind his eyes, a low hum began to build, resonant with the hollow beneath his ribs. If he touched it, would he still be the General of the Host — or would he become the first executioner of a truth without limits?

The moment skin met hilt, the grove vanished.
