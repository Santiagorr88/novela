# Chapter 1 — A Wound in the World

The march across Serephis was an exercise in unraveling. Not of the body, though the heat fused sand into sheets of black glass beneath his sabatons and the sky hung a color no healthy sky had any business wearing. This was an unraveling of the soul. Miguel, Supreme Commander of the Celestial Host, knew the clean pain of battle — the bite of demonic steel, the burn of infernal fire. Those were honest wounds, wounds with edges you could name and close. This was different. This was a hollow ache lodged in the center of his being, a space where something had been taken. His gauntleted hand went to his chest by instinct, a gesture he had repeated a thousand times since the ache began. He was fundamentally incomplete, and one question drove him across the blistering wastes: *what was taken from me?*

He did not think of himself as a man chasing a weapon. He told himself he was chasing an answer — that the ache was a wound from some battle scrubbed from his memory, and that once he understood it, he could return to his life whole. He did not let himself examine how thin that hope had worn.

The dune broke apart under his boot, and beneath the crust of glass-sand, Miguel found stone.

He stopped. Not because the wound in his chest had changed its pull — it hadn't, it never did, a compass needle with no patience for detours — but because the stone was cut, not shattered. A paving block, its edge still square after however many centuries the desert had spent trying to erase it. He crouched and brushed the sand away with two fingers, uncovering a second block beside the first, and a third beyond that, a buried road running straight toward the horizon he was already walking.

Serephis had not always been empty.

He knew this the way he knew most things about the deep past — as doctrine, not memory. A kingdom here, before the Fall settled its architecture over everything older. He had never learned its name. Someone, at some council or another, had mentioned once that the desert itself was not a ruin but a weapon — that a people who could no longer win a war had unmade their own land rather than hand it to what was coming, had burned the water table and salted the air and let the wind do the rest for a thousand years. He hadn't believed it at the time. It had sounded like the kind of story armies tell each other to make retreat sound like strategy.

He believed it now, crouched over a road built for feet that had stopped walking it before his own kind had names.

For a moment — brief, unwelcome — the ache in his chest answered the stone with a low, sympathetic throb, as though something in him recognized something in it. A land that chose its own ending rather than survive as someone else's territory. He did not like the shape of that thought, so he stood, and did not look down again, and let the buried road vanish back beneath his next stride.

For days the ache had been a dull throb, easy enough to bury beneath duty. Now it pulsed with a clear, directional authority, dragging him forward whether he willed it or not. It reminded him, cruelly, of the clarity of the Divine Word — the way true command settled into the soul as unarguable fact. This new call wore the same certainty. Its origin terrified him. He stopped, grit crunching under his boots, and closed his eyes a moment against the fear of it. When he opened them, he had already turned toward the pull, a body surrendering before the mind agreed to it.

The oppressive stillness broke on a chord of pure harmony. Gabriel had arrived, and his presence was a physical relief, a sudden coolness on sun-scorched skin. Soft light spread around his brother, dispelling the gloom for a pace. Miguel felt love, sharp enough to hurt, and close behind it, shame. He did not want Gabriel — the Herald of Sacred Resonance, keeper of a thousand tongues — to see him like this: unraveled, hunting a phantom ache across a cursed waste. He straightened, a futile attempt at a commander's bearing, but the weariness was carved too deep to hide. Gabriel's face held no judgment. To Miguel it looked like something worse: a raw, aching worry.

"The Council is concerned, Miguel." Gabriel's voice carried that same first harmony, but strained now with an audible plea. "They fear this is a deception — a lure the enemy crafted to draw you out. Your absence puts fear in the Host."

The words — *Council, duty, fear* — arrived like echoes from another life. Beside the ache in his chest, they seemed irrelevant. The ache flared with fresh heat regardless. Miguel took a half-step back, an arm crossing instinctively over his ribs.

"They think me a fool," Miguel said, his voice rough with disuse. "Or a traitor."

"They think you are their commander. Lost, and in pain." Gabriel's voice gentled. "They think of their brother."

Something else rode with his brother that Gabriel did not name. Miguel caught the edge of it as Gabriel shifted his weight — a folded parchment tucked close against his ribs, sealed in wax too dark and too formal for a simple message, the kind of seal that belonged to a Council order rather than a brother's private word. Gabriel made no move to produce it. He did not so much as glance toward it, and something in that careful stillness told Miguel more than the parchment itself could have. Whatever it authorized, Gabriel had apparently decided, on his own, not to need it yet.

The shift — envoy to brother — affected Miguel more deeply than any order could have. For one shared breath, he forgot everything beyond the two of them.

There was a version of this moment Miguel had lived before, and it was the reason he could not make himself turn around.

Onyx Gates had cost him his wing — a siege that had gone wrong in the specific, catastrophic way sieges go wrong when solid ground stops being solid, and he had come out of it on his back in a collapsing fortress, unable to feel the arm that should have been screaming. Gabriel had found him there. Not sent — found, on his own, having broken from a command post he had no business leaving. Three nights he had knelt beside him in the dark, cauterizing what could be cauterized with nothing but his own light and his own hands, talking the entire time about nothing — old songs, a joke about Uriel's temper, the taste of something they'd once eaten on a world neither of them could name anymore — because silence, he'd said afterward, was the thing that let wounds decide they were fatal.

Miguel had asked him, once the fortress finally stopped groaning, why he hadn't sent a healer instead.

*Because you don't yell for help in the voice you use for people you love,* Gabriel had said. *You go yourself.*

He heard that voice now, under the pleading. The same low register, the same tenderness that, to Miguel, seemed to conceal exhaustion. It would have been easier if Gabriel had come as an envoy, all official grief and official warning. He had come instead as the brother who once spent three nights on a burning floor for him, and that was a debt Miguel could not simply outwalk, however strongly the wound pulled him onward.

He kept walking anyway. That, too, felt like something he would owe for later.

Miguel was thrown back to the ruin of that memory now, standing here in a different waste with a different wound, and his jaw, clenched for days, loosened for one unbearable second. Gabriel's gaze softened; his hand rose, hesitated, and fell back to his side, the gesture unfinished, the history between them heavier for it.

"Come home," Gabriel said. "Whatever this wound is, we face it together. As we always have."

For a moment Miguel wavered. The thought of the Bastion, of home's familiar harmonies, sang like a promise he had almost forgotten how to want. Then came the colder thought: returning would mean carrying this void for eternity, a permanent reminder of his incompletion. The Council would offer comfort, not answers. They would try to heal a wound they could not even see. He had to go forward. He had to *know*.

"I cannot." He met his brother's eyes for the first time since Gabriel had arrived — his own gaze no longer weary, but lit with a terrible, crystalline resolve. "This call is a command more fundamental than any the Council can give me. It is a truth I must find, or I am nothing." He turned, angling his body toward the pull. The choice had already made itself. He was alone now.

Gabriel's light dimmed a fraction, harmony faltering into something like a held note that would not resolve. He offered no further argument. His message was delivered; his plea, spent. A single tear of pure light traced his cheek, and he inclined his head, sorrow written plain across features that rarely showed it. "May you find what you seek, brother." His voice had thickened with grief. Then he was gone, folded back into the space between worlds, the sealed parchment still riding unmentioned against his ribs. Miguel found himself, watching the space where his brother had stood, wondering what exactly he had just been spared.

The heat and hush of Serephis returned, intensified by Gabriel's absence. The memory of his brother's sorrowful eyes became one more weight for Miguel to carry. Miguel let the regret surface once, fully, before setting it aside; only the certainty of the choice itself remained. The choice was made. He turned and marched on.

He walked an age through unchanging desert, until, without warning, it changed. The transition struck like a physical blow: one step on hot, grating sand, the next on cool, yielding moss. The oppressive air gave way to a still coolness that carried a metallic tang, like the moment after lightning. He had reached the grove. Trees with leaves of pale, leaf-thin metal rose in ranks around him, casting a bright, even light that fell on nothing — no shadows anywhere, a flat and depthless world. His tactical mind screamed *ambush*. Nothing in creation was made this perfectly; it felt manufactured, a lure dressed as a sanctuary. His hand drifted to the space where a sword hilt should have waited, an old habit that now only measured his own vulnerability. Beautiful as it was, this place held the answer he had come for, and he would not let its perfection deceive him.

The quiet here felt watchful. It made him feel like an intruder in a place sacred but not holy. Wet stone. Something like the air after a lightning strike. No breeze stirred the leaves; the temperature never wavered. He tried to recite a verse of the Divine Song, the centering exercise he had used before a thousand battles, but the encompassing stillness left him unable to recall the words. Every soft crunch of fallen leaf beneath his boots felt like a shout inside a library. He could not shake the sense of being measured, though he had no idea by what, or for what.

He felt it first as a change in the light rather than a shape — one of the metal trees, some forty paces off, no longer quite matching the angle of the others around it. He turned toward it fully and found a figure standing there, tall as the trees themselves, wrought of the same pale, leafless material, its face a smooth continuation of its shoulders, unbroken by feature of any kind. It did not move. It did not raise a weapon, though something in the set of its shoulders suggested it did not need one. Miguel had stood before ten thousand enemies in his long service and had learned to read intention in a stance before a single blow fell. He read nothing here he recognized. Not hostility, not welcome. Only attention, absolute and unblinking, and he found himself unable to guess what it was actually waiting to see.

"I have come for what is mine," Miguel said, and heard, even as he said it, how little he actually knew of the claim he was making.

The faceless figure offered no answer. Its stillness felt, somehow, like a reply anyway, though Miguel could not have said what it had actually replied — only that his own certainty suddenly felt small and borrowed beside it. He took one step toward the grove's center, testing the silence for whatever line he was not meant to cross. No line came. The figure did not follow or block him. It merely turned, smooth and soundless, to keep facing him as he passed, like a compass needle holding north. It seemed content, as far as Miguel could tell, simply to watch him go. He could not have said what that meant, and did not have the luxury of stopping to guess. He walked on, the faceless figure's gaze a weight against his back long after the trees closed between them and hid it from sight, a weight he suspected — though he could not have said why — he had not actually left behind at all.

At the grove's center stood a great ash, its bark the color of cooled lava. Its roots reached into a pool of starlight so pure it looked poured rather than reflected. Sunk into the trunk was a sword — not forged metal, but living, solidified light. The instant he saw it, the ache in his chest went silent, the void filled by something that pulsed in the same rhythm as his own hollow wound. A name surfaced in his mind unbidden, not remembered so much as *rediscovered*: *Solmire.* It felt less like learning a word and more like finding one that had always belonged to him, waiting. His steps, cautious a moment before, now pulled him forward almost against his will. This was not the thought *that is a sword*. It was recognition, absolute and terrifying: *that is what was taken from me.*

He stood before the ash, hand trembling as he raised it. The blade's presence pressed on him like an older, more fundamental truth. For a heartbeat, the name *Miguel* felt like a title someone else had given him to wear. A low hum built behind his eyes, not sound but vibration, resonant with the hollow space beneath his ribs. His fingers hovered inches from the hilt, caught between hunger and dread. If he touched this, would he still be himself afterward? Or only an extension of the blade's will?

The moment skin met hilt, the grove vanished.
