# Chapter 36 — The Rift That Shouldn't Exist

Thoria led her patrol along the border plane's quiet edge the way she led every patrol, thorough, her eyes moving in the same steady, unblinking sweep she brought to any stretch of ground she'd been given to watch. The assignment carried no urgency worth the name — a routine reconnaissance of drift patterns along a boundary that had given Heaven no trouble in longer than most of her soldiers had served. She preferred assignments like this one. They rewarded the discipline she had built her reputation on — patient, incorruptible, immune to the laziness that crept in only after enough uneventful rotations convinced a soldier vigilance was optional.

She held the patrol to its usual formation, checking each soldier's position with the same unblinking attention she gave everything else, Alther resting easy at her side rather than drawn. Nothing here today warranted the blade's readiness. She kept it close regardless. She had learned long ago that the day discipline slackened because nothing seemed to require it was invariably the day something finally did.

The border plane stretched quiet and unremarkable in every direction, the kind of terrain that struck most soldiers under her command as tedious to patrol, drift patterns shifting only slightly from one rotation to the next in ways that rarely amounted to anything worth reporting. Tedium was not a word Thoria applied to it. She had built her entire service on the conviction that vigilance mattered precisely because most days demanded nothing of it — that the discipline itself was the point, regardless of whether any given patrol turned up a genuine threat. Her soldiers had learned, over enough rotations serving under her, to hold the same standard, whether they shared her conviction or simply obeyed it.

She called a brief halt near the plane's midpoint, as she always did, to let the rear guard confirm their instruments against the patrol's official charts. It was, like everything else about today, routine — a small check that had turned up nothing to justify the pause in all her long service. She held the halt anyway, unwilling to let habit erode into carelessness just because it had never yet been rewarded with anything more than confirmation of what she already expected.

The ground beneath the patrol's rear guard folded inward without warning, a silent, instantaneous collapse that no instrument any of them carried had registered a moment before it happened. Thoria turned in time to see three of her soldiers vanish into a tear that hadn't existed an instant earlier — the space where solid ground had been now simply gone, replaced by something that read, to her trained eye, as neither plane nor void but some unstable seam between the two.

She did not hesitate. She crossed the remaining distance and followed her soldiers through the tear before it could close, blade drawn now, long discipline insisting it would matter wherever this led. The fall through the seam lasted only a moment, disorienting and without direction, and then solid ground returned beneath her feet in a place that resembled nothing she had ever charted in centuries of routine border patrol.

The remaining members of her patrol followed close behind, either choosing to cross as she had or caught by the same collapsing ground before it sealed itself shut. She did a rapid accounting the moment her feet found solid footing again — relief settling briefly in her chest when the count came back complete. Every soldier who had entered the border plane with her that morning had made it through the tear intact, however disoriented the crossing left them.

The terrain around her offered nothing familiar to orient against. The light here moved wrong, shifting in slow, uneven pulses that answered to no source she could identify — the surface beneath her boots the color of wet ash, throwing no shadow no matter the angle the light struck it — and the ground carried a faint, persistent instability, as though the very concept of solid footing hadn't been fully decided here yet. The air carried a taste faintly metallic, like a coin held too long on the tongue, and a cold that had nothing to do with temperature. She had crossed strange terrain before, across a service long enough to have seen more of it than most soldiers under her command could imagine. Nothing had felt quite like this — not hostile, only unsettlingly undecided about what it was. By nightfall her soldiers had a name for it — the Hollowseam, a seam between planes with nothing sewn inside — and Thoria, who distrusted naming what she did not yet understand, caught herself using it anyway.

Vem had not expected the assault to go this smoothly, and had said as much, loudly, to anyone in his strike team close enough to hear him over the structure they were bringing down around a minor angelic supply cache. Tiltas hummed warm in his grip, the staff's own pleasure at this flavor of controlled devastation running up his arm in a satisfaction he never bothered hiding. Ground, in Vem's opinion, was always more interesting the moment you stopped treating it as something fixed.

The assault had begun as a straightforward demolition, nothing especially glamorous by the standards of the missions Vem usually got assigned — a supply cache, lightly defended, its destruction meant to slow rather than cripple whatever angelic operation depended on it. Vem never much cared about the strategic weight of a target. He cared about the work itself — the joy of finding exactly the right angle to tilt a structure's foundation until the whole thing folded in on itself, more satisfying to watch than a demolition strictly required. His strike team had long since stopped commenting on how much time he spent perfecting a collapse that would have worked perfectly well without the flourish.

He caught, in the middle of admiring his own handiwork, one of his squad mates shouting about the ground near the cache's northern edge behaving strangely — a warning Vem might have taken more seriously had it arrived a few seconds sooner. He turned toward the shout just in time to watch it resolve into something well past strange behavior — a genuine tear opening in the ground itself, swallowing the shouting soldier whole before Vem had fully processed what he was looking at.

He tilted the last support plane with a flourish he didn't strictly need, the cache's outer wall folding in on itself with the same visual delight he brought to every demolition, and felt, in the same instant, the floor beneath his own boots do something the staff had nothing to do with. The ground tore open under him without warning, swallowing him and two of his strike team whole, and Vem had just enough time to register the novelty of falling through a collapse he hadn't personally caused before the fall scattered every other thought from his mind.

He landed hard on ground that felt subtly wrong beneath him, even through the disorientation of the fall — not the familiar give of ordinary earth, but a surface still deciding what texture it intended to have. He pushed himself upright quickly, old instinct overriding the lingering dizziness, and took rapid stock of his surroundings the way any soldier learns to after an unexpected fall into unfamiliar terrain. Two of his squad had made the crossing with him, both looking as disoriented as he felt, and beyond them, in the slow-pulsing light of wherever they'd landed, he caught his first glimpse of figures that were plainly not demonic.

He felt, cataloguing the scene with the quick read he brought to any unfamiliar situation, fascination more than alarm. An angelic patrol, here, wherever here was, looking as deposited and disoriented as his own squad had been moments before. The coincidence struck him as more curious than threatening, though he understood well enough that the angels across the strange terrain were unlikely to share his enthusiasm for how odd this had all just become.

Thoria's patrol reassembled around her within the new terrain's disorienting first minutes, three soldiers found and accounted for, formation reforming along the familiar lines of their training even in ground none of them recognized. She caught the demonic presence before she saw it clearly, the blade's own resonance sharpening in her grip the way it always did near a soul-parasite bond worth severing, and raised it fully the moment silhouettes resolved out of the slow, uneven pulses of light on the far side of the terrain.

"Defensive formation... Do not engage yet — nobody knows what this place actually is," she called to her own soldiers, voice carrying the flat authority she brought to any command. From the demons' side, a voice answered back, looser and less guarded than her own, carrying an edge of amusement layered over obvious confusion. "Hey, sentinel — if this is an ambush, it's the worst-planned one I've seen in centuries!" Neither side closed the distance. Both held their ground, weapons ready, neither committing to the strike the moment seemed to be building toward.

Thoria held her position, eyes fixed on the demon who had spoken with the same unblinking attention she gave any unknown threat, weighing what sounded like genuine confusion in his voice against the practiced readiness of his stance. Something in the terrain itself unsettled her more than the demons facing her across it — a wrongness in the ground and light that no drill, no rotation, no stretch of her service had prepared her to categorize. She did not lower the blade. She also did not give the order to advance; some old discipline insisted that whatever this place was mattered more, right now, than whichever side stood on the other end of it.

The ground shifted again beneath both groups at once, a slow, groaning instability that drew visible attention away from the standoff and toward the ground. Across the terrain, the restless demon lowered the staff he'd been leveling at her patrol and turned it instead toward the ground, calling something sharp to his own squad with the cadence of an order rather than a taunt. Thoria understood, feeling the same instability move beneath her own boots, that the terrain represented a threat neither side currently had any real answer for. Whatever had brought both patrols here, the unstable seam between planes plainly did not care which side any of them had arrived on.

"Your ground's doing that too?" the demon called across the distance, some of his earlier bravado thinning into what sounded, to Thoria's ear, like genuine unease. Thoria didn't answer immediately, weighing any exchange against the mounting evidence that neither side could currently afford to treat the other as the primary danger. "It is," she said finally, flat and clipped. "Hold your position. Whatever this place is, attacking each other in the middle of it seems a good deal less urgent than surviving it."

The demon said nothing further for a long while, and Thoria used the silence to study him more carefully than the chaos of first contact had allowed — a wiry, restless figure who seemed unable to hold entirely still, weight shifting from foot to foot as though stillness didn't come naturally to him. Whatever caused the ground to behave this way, she suspected he understood it no better than she did, and found, weighing that shared ignorance against everything else the last several minutes had thrown at her, a small, wary measure of the same caution she gave her own soldiers extending, however reluctantly, toward him as well.

Neither side lowered their weapons fully. Both sides, nonetheless, held their distance rather than closing it, an uneasy stillness settling over the unstable ground between them while every soldier she could see kept at least part of their attention on the terrain instead of the standoff. Thoria did not trust the pause to last. She trusted it enough, for now, to let it hold.

She kept the blade raised throughout, unwilling to concede even this much to instinct alone, and watched the demons across the unstable terrain do much the same, weapons held ready without ever quite closing the last distance between the two groups. It was not trust, whatever this brief, wary stillness was. From what she could observe of the demons' own bearing — weapons drawn but unadvancing, attention split as visibly as her own soldiers' between the standoff and the ground — neither side appeared willing yet to risk spending strength on the other before understanding this place better.

The ground shifted once more beneath her boots, smaller this time but no less unsettling, and Thoria felt her soldiers tense around her at the movement, the same wary attention she gave the terrain now clearly shared throughout her patrol. She had no name yet for this fractured seam, only the growing certainty that it deserved one. It had already demonstrated, twice now, that it answered to neither side's expectations of solid ground. She intended to understand it fully before she risked anything further, weapons drawn or not.
