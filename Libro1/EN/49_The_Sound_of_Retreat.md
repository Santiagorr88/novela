# Chapter 49 — The Sound of Retreat

Gabriel stood motionless at the command dais, his eyes fixed on the great crystalline screen as it flickered and finally died in the exact instant Lucifer's final blow fell on the ash plain. The mystical connection cut out with a thin, dry hiss, in the same sliver of time that Solmire's golden light went dark beneath the mud, leaving the crystal frame empty and grey as the stone of a well. There were no blaring alarms in the war room in that first second; the silence that settled over the dais was an absolute void of frigid air, an absence of weight so dense that Gabriel felt his own body's light flicker weak and helpless, like a tallow candle caught in a wind it had no way to guard against.

Around him, the assembled officers and archangels absorbed the blow each in their own private register of grief. Uriel's jaw hung slack, what looked like a counter-attack order or a burst of holy fire dying unspoken on his lips before the sentence could even finish forming in his throat. Raphael slowly closed his eyes against the impact of the darkened crystal, a clinical, familiar grief marking the hollows of his features without the smallest effort to hide the break in front of the room's aides. Gabriel simply kept staring at the screen's grey emptiness, feeling the gaze of every scribe, aide, and tactician turn slowly toward his shoulders. The entire dais had frozen in its respective postures of cloth and steel, and Gabriel understood, with the cold of newly born responsibility settling at his temples, that now that the Champion had left the line, the weight of deciding the road's end fell to his name alone.

"To arms!" Uriel's voice finally tore through the room's stunned stillness, his fury, dull and hot, sounding through the chamber like the first crack of a hearth fire searching for something physical to burn rather than look at its own fear. "We'll form the squadrons in the courtyard! We'll avenge him on the plain before Hell can pull back its standards!"

Gabriel raised a steady, gauntleted hand to check his brother's impulse before Uriel's rushing fire could spread to the rest of the officers, his own authority settling into the vacuum of command with a fixity that needed no raised voice to become law.

"No," Gabriel said, and the word fell with the dull weight of cold granite onto the cloth-draped table. "Look."

He pointed a long finger toward the tactical screens ringing the room's perimeter, where the lights of fifteen separate fronts flickered in the same faltering, silent collapse. No losses were being reported to iron, no breaches in the walls' masonry; the legions were coming apart from a loss of faith, the echo of Lamentum's grief traveling beneath the chainmail to remind them that the symbol around which they had sung the anthem was no longer there to hold them up. Gabriel studied the numbers with the same cold, detached accounting of an auditor who no longer has figures enough to balance the rear guard. To fight now, with cloth-clad troops laying down their halberds to surrender to a mute grief in the mud, would not avenge Miguel; it would simply add a useless tally of names to an inventory of losses the afternoon had already devoured without a shred of mercy. Uriel held his gaze, jaw tight, the fire of his halberd Ignis Lux guttering into rank smoke, and offered no further reply. His brother's silence confirmed for Gabriel that the logic of raw force had run out of ground to stand on tonight.

Gabriel turned toward the herald waiting rigid at the foot of the railing, and the order came out of his mouth thin and stripped of any official ornament.

"Sound the retreat."

The young angel watched him without moving, his eyes wide with the astonishment of a recruit who suspects he's misheard the instruction. In all his centuries of service, and in all the long memory the Bastion kept of its campaigns, no officer of Heaven had ever once spoken that particular order; the Horn of Heaven had been consecrated and cast for nothing but the bright, triumphant tone of the charge.

"Sound it," Gabriel repeated, and for the first time the noble exhaustion of the brother cracked the harmony of his voice into a dry register that admitted no further delay.

The herald raised the bronze with visibly unsteady hands, and the Horn of Heaven sounded a single long, funereal, low note that carried out over the dead plain like a lament belonging to no known music. It carried no promise of advance; it was a sound of endings, of debts called due, of a grief so absolute that soldiers posted at the farthest fronts let their pikes fall the moment it reached them on the cold air. The withdrawal began in a silent, broken, sombre order, columns of cloth and bronze falling back down the road with heads bowed and the soul-wounded carried on shoulders without anyone needing to give a single marching order. Gabriel listened to the note fade into the distance, knowing, with the ache of black wax pressed against his ribs, that he had just rewritten forever what the bronze of his own army meant.

Far from the Bastion, on the grey ash of the plain that had just swallowed the duel, Belial approached the site of the collapse with slow, heavy steps, carrying in his own body the cold horseshoe-ache of Lamentum's grief. Miguel lay motionless in the mud, and a short distance from his slack hand, Solmire rested on the ash, her living light reduced to a dull, inert glow the dust of the road had already begun to claim. Belial reached his free hand toward the sword's hilt, certain, with a courtier's stale pride, that a victor's hands were owed both blades before the afternoon was out.

His fingers had barely finished closing around the solidified light when the blade answered with a searing, violent, mocking brilliance that burned his palm straight to the bone. The impact was a pulse of irreverent rejection that threw his arm back before he could grip a single fraction of the metal, sending Solmire skidding across the ash until she vanished into the smoke of the clearing. Belial let out a cry that had no air left to become sound, sinking to one knee in the mud as the wound in his palm opened into a crust of charred flesh that refused to heal under the gusting wind.

At his other side, the spear Lamentum reacted instantly to the contact with the dissonance: a pulse of jealousy, sorrow, and pain ran through the dark wooden shaft, threading up his wounded arm like a current of icy water that settled directly behind his ribs. Belial held his burned hand against his chest, feeling the two primordial forces shriek against each other inside his own essence with a ferocity that cracked his composure, and understood, with bitter saliva in his throat, that his victory over the celestial Champion had just handed him a tenant he would probably have to fight every dawn of his service.

His smile, when it finally came, belonged to a man who had won the entire world, and felt, even as he smiled, his own mind beginning to fracture under exactly what that victory had cost him.

Gabriel stood at the threshold of the Empyrean Gates, watching his soldiers' disciplined column file past, every face carrying the same hollowed, grey stare the Horn's note had sown through the ranks. He had given the order that brought them back to the Celestial City, and standing there, under the vault's flat glow, he still hadn't found a way to make that withdrawal read, on any of his own terms, as anything other than a betrayal of everything his brother had died defending on the line.

The moment the last cloth-clad sentry crossed the threshold stone, Gabriel turned to the gatekeepers and gave the final, heaviest instruction of the night. The great gates of Heaven, which had stood flung wide open since the very instant of their consecration so that harmony could flow unhindered between the worlds, began their slow, dull, massive closing for the first time in the history of creation. The slam of iron and marble rolled out through the Bastion's empty courtyards with the boom of weary masonry that snuffed out the hiss of the outer wasteland at a stroke.

Gabriel remained there alone in the closed courtyard's half-light, his back against the column's granite, and finally allowed himself, in the privacy the shuttered Bastion now afforded him, the grief for the brother who would never again cross that threshold at his side. The silence that settled over the Celestial City after the doors fell was the thickest, heaviest, most absolute of his entire long existence, and he carried it whole in his chest, resolved to watch over a border that had gone dark before the ash plain.
