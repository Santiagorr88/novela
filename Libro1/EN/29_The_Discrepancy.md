# Chapter 29 — The Discrepancy

Daith returned to the scriptorium the next morning no closer to an answer than he had left it, and found, within the first hour, that neither had the argument itself gone anywhere overnight. He and Kurel worked the fragment in an uneasy parallel rather than any real partnership now, each of them circling the same disputed stanza from opposite directions without managing to actually close the distance between their positions. The rest of the hymn had come along steadily enough in the time since, stanza after stanza yielding to Daith's methodical transcription with no further complications. Only the final section remained, the one containing the word that refused to hold its shape, sitting between them like a wound neither of them had found a way to close.

He had spent part of the previous night turning the disagreement over in his own head, testing Kurel's reasoning the way he might test an unfamiliar witness statement against known facts, searching for the flaw that would let him dismiss it cleanly and return to the work procedure demanded. He found, instead, something closer to confirmation than refutation: a search through the archive's own precedent records that night had turned up no comparable case, no other fragment anywhere in the Codex Mortalis whose language had ever behaved this way on the page. The absence unsettled him more than Kurel's caution had. Iofiel expected a complete translation. Gabriel needed it soon. Neither fact had changed overnight, and neither of them, Daith told himself, actually required his own private doubts to be resolved first.

The scriptorium, when he arrived, showed no sign that Kurel had slept any better than he had. The hymn fragment sat exactly where they had left it the night before, undisturbed, the disputed stanza still bearing the faint discoloration where both their hands had rested against it during the worst of yesterday's argument. Kurel was already at the table, reading the final lines under his breath again, the same slow, deliberate cadence Daith had watched him use a dozen times over the past two days without once managing to hear in it whatever Kurel himself was listening for.

He reached for the final stanza mid-morning, telling himself it was simply the next reasonable step in an assignment already running behind schedule, and began transcribing it letter by letter the way he had transcribed everything else in the hymn — without waiting for Kurel's approval this time, without so much as announcing that he had decided to proceed. It was, in the narrowest technical sense, well within his authority to do. The stanza was legible. His methods had never required a second signature before committing anything to the record.

He had not announced his intention beforehand, had barely allowed himself to consider whether he should. Reaching for the stanza anyway was its own argument — asking would have meant risking another refusal, and proceeding without asking meant he did not have to hear one. He told himself this was simply efficiency, the ordinary momentum of a man returning to work he had already half-completed. He did not entirely believe himself, and pressed forward anyway.

Kurel's hand came down over the parchment before Daith's pen reached the disputed word itself, firm enough to stop the transcription outright without quite becoming violent about it. The suddenness of it startled Daith more than he cared to admit, his pen skidding a fraction off its intended line before he managed to still his own hand. "Move your hand, Kurel," Daith said, an edge of genuine anger entering his voice for the first time since the argument had begun the day before. "Not until you tell me why you're in such a hurry to finish something we haven't even understood yet," Kurel answered, not moving the hand at all.

"I'm not in a hurry, Kurel. I'm doing my job." Daith kept his own hand steady around the pen, unwilling to give the moment any more physical drama than Kurel had already introduced to it. "You started transcribing the one line we both agreed needed more attention, without a word to me first, the moment my back was turned long enough for you to try it," Kurel said, his voice carrying none of Daith's rising heat, which somehow made the accusation land harder rather than softer. "That's not doing your job. That's doing an end run around a disagreement you didn't want to keep having."

Daith opened his mouth to object and found, to his own irritation, that he had no immediate rebuttal ready. It had, in fact, been exactly that. He had known, reaching for the stanza, precisely what Kurel's reaction would likely be if he asked first, and had chosen the version of events where he did not have to find out for certain. He said none of that aloud, unwilling to let Kurel know how precisely the accusation had landed.

"We didn't agree it needed more attention, Kurel. You decided it did, and I've been living with that decision for two days now." Daith heard how thin the distinction sounded even as he made it, and pressed on anyway rather than concede the point outright. "There's a difference between respecting your instincts and being held hostage by them indefinitely. I've given you the benefit of the doubt since yesterday morning. At some point that benefit runs out."

"Because an incomplete record helps no one," Daith said, the words arriving with more heat than he had intended, months of professional conviction compressed into a single well-worn argument. "You want to wait until you fully understand something before committing it to paper. I understand that instinct, Kurel, I do. But we don't have the luxury of understanding everything before we act. Sometimes the record has to exist before the understanding catches up to it." Kurel did not remove his hand. "And sometimes," he said, "the record existing before the understanding catches up is exactly how something gets loose that should have stayed contained a little longer. You're not wrong that waiting has a cost. I'm not wrong that moving too fast has one too. We just disagree about which cost is worse."

"Then tell me what you actually think is in that word," Daith said, quieter now, some of the heat draining out of the demand even as he made it. Kurel held his eyes for a long moment before answering. "I don't know," he said. "That's the entire problem, Daith. I don't know what it is, and neither do you, and the not-knowing is exactly the reason I won't let you seal it shut before either of us actually does."

"That's not an answer. That's a reason to keep not answering forever." Daith heard the frustration sharpen his own voice again and made no particular effort to soften it this time. "At some point, Kurel, 'we don't know yet' has to become 'we tried to find out.' You can't hold a hymn hostage to your own instincts indefinitely. Eventually someone has to decide the waiting itself has become the greater risk." Kurel's expression did not change, though something in his posture seemed to settle more firmly, as though bracing against an argument he had heard some version of before and had yet to find persuasive. "Maybe," he said. "But I'd rather be the one who waited too long than the one who spoke a name before anyone understood what answering it would cost. You're right that waiting isn't free, Daith. I've never claimed it was. I just think the other cost is worse, and I think you know that too, somewhere underneath how certain you're trying to sound right now."

The accusation landed closer to true than Daith wanted to concede aloud, and he said nothing for a moment, the silence itself doing more work than any further argument might have. Kurel did not press the advantage. He only waited, patient in the particular way that had always unsettled Daith more than any direct confrontation could have, as though he had all the time in the world and fully expected Daith to eventually arrive at the same conclusion on his own.

Daith withdrew his hand from the parchment eventually, more out of exhaustion than any real concession, and set aside the few opening lines he had already managed to copy before Kurel's hand had stopped him, unwilling to file a transcription he could not finish properly. He spent the remainder of the afternoon reviewing the hymn's less contentious stretches instead, cross-checking his own earlier transcription against the original script for anything he might have rushed, letting the final stanza sit unfinished between them. Kurel said nothing further about the exchange, returning to his own slow, patient reading of the earlier stanzas as though the confrontation had never happened at all, though the covered corner of the parchment stayed exactly where his hand had left it, a small, deliberate boundary neither of them chose to test again that day. He told himself, working back through the safer passages, that he had simply chosen the more efficient use of his time rather than backed down from anything. The explanation didn't sit right, and he knew it — but he kept that particular doubt to himself, unwilling to hand Kurel a victory he had not technically earned.

His pen moved through corrections and annotations across the safer passages with its usual precision, but some part of his attention kept drifting back toward the covered stanza, toward the particular stillness with which Kurel had guarded it all day without once needing to repeat his refusal aloud. Daith had worked beside plenty of colleagues over the centuries who held stubborn opinions about matters of procedure, and he had rarely found himself troubled by any of them the way this particular disagreement was beginning to trouble him now.

Alone that evening, walking the quiet corridor back toward his own quarters, he allowed himself to consider, briefly and privately, whether Kurel might actually be right. It was an uncomfortable thought to sit with, precisely because dismissing it would have been so much easier than examining it honestly. Daith had built his entire career on the principle that thoroughness protected people — that an incomplete record was a record that eventually failed someone who needed it whole. He had not, until now, had real cause to consider that the opposite might also be true — that a record completed too soon could fail someone just as badly.

He thought, walking, of every case in the archive's long history where an incomplete translation had cost someone dearly — a covenant left ambiguous, a testimony filed with gaps a clever advocate later exploited, a judgment overturned because the record simply hadn't held enough of the truth to stand on. He had cited those cases to Kurel more than once over the years, the Perren testimony above all, a case he could still recite from memory, always as settled proof of his own methods. He found himself, tonight, unable to summon a single case in the other direction — no record he could name that had been finished too soon and done comparable damage — and was not certain whether that was because no such case existed, or because he had simply never gone looking for one.

He did not reach any conclusion before he reached his own door. He only knew, letting himself inside, that the certainty he had walked into the scriptorium with two days ago had not survived the argument intact, whatever he had actually said to Kurel's face about it.

Neither of them raised the subject again before the day ended. Kurel would not yield the seal; Daith would not concede the principle; the final stanza sat exactly where it had that morning, unfinished, its corrupted word still refusing to hold a single shape between readings.

He returned to the scriptorium the next morning to find nothing had shifted overnight — except, he noticed, that Kurel had taken to reading the stanza to himself more often than the day before, under his breath, at odd moments between other tasks, as if checking something that would not stay checked. Daith said nothing about it. Something would eventually have to give — Gabriel's patience, if nothing else, or the deadline Iofiel had made clear was not indefinite. For now, the two of them worked around the wound in the hymn rather than through it, each waiting, Daith suspected, for the other to break first.
