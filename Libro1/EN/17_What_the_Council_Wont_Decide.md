# Chapter 17 — What the Council Won't Decide

Iofiel had spent weeks digging through the depths of the Silent Choir, and by now she had learned to measure the passing of time by the thickness of dust gathering on the sleeves of her grey wool habit. The corner she claimed for herself was narrow, a cold stone alcove where the light of a single oil lamp barely managed to hold an amber circle against the shadow of the tall shelves. To her left, Memnon rested propped against the lectern's wood; the staff of old memory sat inert, but Iofiel could feel its low hum at the base of her skull, a steady vibration that helped her keep her attention fixed when the pages of the Codex Mortalis began to blur before her eyes.

The volume's leaves were alarmingly fragile, thin as the skin of a dried fruit, and they crackled with a harsh complaint every time her gloved fingers forced them to give way. The passage she was hunting wasn't announced by gilded rubrics or the clean hand of the official scribes. It lay buried in a section she'd already passed over twice, hidden beneath a crust of old mold that smelled of spent lime and cellar damp. She cleaned the surface with the pad of her thumb, careful not to tear the dry fiber, and the cold reached her before her mind had finished translating the characters. This was no campaign gloss, no garrison inventory. The text spoke of a spear. A spear of painful truth, forged in salt and in mourning, whose name — Lamentum — filled the roof of her mouth with a dry, metallic taste, as though she'd held a bronze coin under her tongue for a full day. The parchment offered no coordinates, none of the clean line of a map her staff might have verified; it spoke only of a shore of black stone that no cartographer of the Host had ever bothered to record on any chart. Iofiel copied the lines twice into her notebook in quick strokes, feeling the archive's stillness go suddenly too thin around her, as though the whole of the Silent Choir were waiting to see what she meant to do with the wound she had just dug up.

Iofiel did not carry the notebook to the Council chamber; she went instead to the reading nook in the north wing, a small, low-vaulted space where Gabriel liked to seek the refuge of quiet when the Bastion's music grew too loud for his taste. She found him standing at the window, his robe of blue and silver falling without a fold onto the flagstones, watching the slow stars with that tired stillness that had settled into his shoulders since the shadow-plane campaign.

Gabriel took the page she offered, and the sapphire of his eyes caught the lamp's reflection with a fixedness Iofiel read as suspicion. He read the translation twice in silence, and the room seemed to cool half a degree with every line, the air going flat, stripped of the sacred resonance Gabriel usually carried with him without meaning to.

"Are you certain of the translation, Iofiel?" he asked, and his voice, usually sweet and fraternal, came out thin, stripped of its usual echo.

"As certain as three independent readings can make me," she answered, keeping her tone in the flat register of a measurement. "It's a fragment, Gabriel. There are no location marks, only the description of the tomb and the name of the metal."

Gabriel set the sheet down on the wooden table with excessive care, as though he feared the paper might come apart at the slightest carelessness, and looked back to the stars. His hand brushed briefly against a folded parchment he carried hidden beneath his robe — the Council's black wax seal — before falling back to his side with a leaden weight.

"Bring it to the Council tomorrow morning," Gabriel said gently, without turning. "Let me sit with this tonight first. I need... to think about what this truth will cost my brother before Uriel tries to turn it into another one of his campaigns."

Iofiel did not press him to say more. She left with the strange, small relief of having handed a weight to someone else, at least for a few hours, before it became everyone's weight to carry. She did not sleep well that night either, turning the fragment's phrasing over long after there was anything left to translate.

The war room felt narrow the next morning, though its dimensions of white stone remained the same ones that had housed three ages of military planning. The real change sat at the head of the table: Miguel's chair stood empty, a tall oak back that seemed to swallow the cold light from the windows with an insistence that made his absence louder than any of the arguments already crossing over the map.

Iofiel laid the pages of her notebook out at the lower end of the table, keeping her translation notes and the text's uncertainties in plain view of everyone. Raphael listened, seated at his end, his emerald eyes fixed on the characters with that clinical stillness of a healer measuring a wound before deciding which tool to use. But Uriel did not give her time to finish presenting the second page.

He rose to his feet with a clatter of his incandescent armor, and the air of the room seemed to warm half a step before his voice cut through the presentation.

"If we have found this spear in an old book, Hell already has its scouts hunting the trail through the mud," Uriel said, his gauntleted finger cutting an aggressive line through the air toward the eastern border. "We move now, Gabriel. We send the vanguard before Belial has the chance to put his hands on a steel we don't know how to stop."

Gabriel did not rise. He held Uriel's gaze, jaw tight, hands folded over the wool of his robe.

"We can't afford to divide our forces over a fragment this thin, Uriel," Gabriel answered, and the hardness of his tone caught Iofiel off guard by how unadorned it was. "Not with the northern borders demanding reinforcements, and not... not with everything else already unsettled between us."

The reference to Miguel needed no name spoken aloud; the empty chair at the head of the table did the work of translation for all of them, and the silence that followed among the captains was a physical weight no one dared to move for a long moment.

"Thin or not, it's the only lead we have," Uriel shot back, "and leads like this don't stay thin forever."

Nothing in Uriel's words suggested he had anyone specific in mind, and Iofiel didn't either. She had turned the question over herself in the quiet hours before this meeting, without finding an answer that satisfied her — only the uneasy sense that whoever it was, they were unlikely to wait for a Council to finish debating.

The two of them had clashed before, plenty of times, but this argument carried an edge Iofiel hadn't heard from either in a while — Gabriel's caution sharpened, she suspected, by Miguel's absence more than by the spear itself, and Uriel's urgency sharpened by exactly the opposite instinct, a refusal to let caution become the reason nothing ever got decided in time.

"Everything is unsettled elsewhere, Gabriel," Uriel pressed on, leaning both hands against the table, "because we keep waiting for certainty before we act. Certainty is a luxury we stopped being able to afford months ago."

Gabriel's jaw tightened — the first real crack Iofiel had seen in his composure — though his voice stayed level.

"I'm not asking for certainty. I'm asking that we not gamble what little strength we can spare on a fragment of a sentence."

Neither seemed willing to yield the floor, and for several exchanges the debate simply repeated itself in slightly different words, each man restating his own logic of war as though restating it more forcefully might finally make the other hear it.

It was Zadkiel who broke the deadlock, and he did it without raising his voice above the flat register he used for taking the minutes of an audit. His pale obsidian armor caught the candlelight with a glacial fixedness, and his silver eyes stayed fixed on Iofiel's notebook, ignoring the tension still vibrating between Uriel and Gabriel.

He rested his hand on Decretum's pommel, the great hammer of judgment lying inert against the arm of his chair, and the room seemed to still under the weight of the metal.

"Show me the location, Iofiel," Zadkiel said. "Not a poetic description of black stones. A location."

Iofiel opened her mouth to explain, for the third time, the Codex's gaps, but Zadkiel raised a single finger, and the explanation died in her throat before it found its pitch.

"Then what we have is a guess and a good translation," Zadkiel concluded, and his voice landed as final as the strike of a stone mallet. "And I do not commit soldiers of the Host to a guess. Bring me a location, Iofiel, and I will give you the vanguard myself. Until then, this Council will not authorize a punitive expedition on the strength of a sentence that might be mistranslated."

Zadkiel's verdict sealed the table. None of the officers dared argue with the Arbiter, and the three-hour debate ended by dissolving into the war room's ordinary exhaustion. What emerged from the table was neither the fast campaign Uriel wanted nor the full caution Gabriel had argued for; it was bureaucracy's middle ground: a single scout, sent discreetly and without banners, to confirm whether the black stone coast existed at all before the Host had to sign another marching order.

Gabriel accepted the compromise with a nod that told Iofiel he'd expected nothing better and hoped for nothing worse. Uriel accepted it more grudgingly, arms folded in a posture Iofiel read as a man counting it a delay rather than a decision, though he offered no further argument once the room had settled. Zadkiel inclined his head once, apparently satisfied the standard of evidence had been met halfway, and gathered the hammer back into his grip as he rose without another word.

The meeting dissolved with the ordinary haste of officers with supply matters to attend to, and Zadkiel was the only one who did not move from his seat. He waited until the last of the aides crossed the threshold before turning to Iofiel, who was still gathering her translation notes into the leather folder.

"One more thing, Captain," Zadkiel said, and his tone shed the weight of the tribunal for the dryness of an inventory. "The reincarnation figures for the last cycle. Do you have the exact number, or only the census estimate?"

Iofiel blinked, caught off guard by a question that felt a hundred leagues from the debate over Lamentum.

"The number," she answered, hunting the figure in her memory. "Sariel's reports on Earth are precise about that, if nothing else. There's a modest rise in northern Navarion, but nothing that should alarm the Council."

Zadkiel nodded once, noting the number down on a small wax tablet he carried hidden beneath his breastplate. He offered no explanation for why he wanted the figure, and Iofiel didn't dare ask one of a man who rarely asked spare questions.

"Have the next cycle's count ready for me when it comes in," Zadkiel said, rising, gathering Decretum up in a single effortless motion. "And don't mention this accounting to Uriel. He has enough heat in his head already this morning."

Iofiel watched him go down the long corridor, feeling the anticlimax of a morning that had begun with a mythic spear and ended with an audit of souls. She stayed behind alone in the empty room, staring at the blank space in her folder where the word Lamentum still waited for a real answer. She knew a single scout was the responsible decision, the only one the Council could make without gambling the security of an already wounded fortress. But looking at the tall windows and the thick silence the Council had left behind, Iofiel understood that responsibility and haste were rarely written with the same pen, and that the gap between the two would be paid for in the mud of the front long before the scout's first report could find its way home.
