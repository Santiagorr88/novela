# Chapter 17 — What the Council Won't Decide

Iofiel had been picking through the Codex Mortalis for weeks, one fragile page at a time, in the same quiet corner of the archive she always claimed — folded into a chair clearly built for someone smaller, Memnon propped within easy reach, the stillness of someone who had read enough endings to stop being surprised by how a story got there — searching for anything that might explain the unrest she kept hearing about in corridors she wasn't meant to be listening in. What she found instead, buried in a section she had passed over twice without recognizing its weight, was a spear.

Not merely a spear — a weapon the text called, in a phrase that took her three separate translations to settle on, a spear of painful truth, sealed somewhere beyond the reach of ordinary judgment. The passage offered no clean coordinates, only a fragment of description: a place beyond a shoreline of black stone no map she knew had ever bothered to name, distant from any territory Heaven or Hell had claimed. Iofiel read the passage four times before she trusted herself to believe it, her staff resting against her shoulder, and felt the particular cold that comes only from finding something true that nobody wanted found.

She had spent enough years in this work to know the difference between a fragment worth reporting and one worth setting aside, and this one belonged, unmistakably, to the first. The text around it was damaged badly enough that whole clauses were simply gone, eaten away by whatever slow catastrophe had left the Codex Mortalis in this state, but what remained held together with a coherence that felt less like coincidence and more like a deliberate warning. She copied the passage twice more by hand before closing the volume, an old habit from decades of watching fragile texts crumble the moment anyone grew careless with them.

She did not take it to the Council first. She took it to Gabriel, alone, in the small archive room he used when he wanted quiet rather than company — Gabriel was the one person she trusted to hear bad news without needing to win an argument about it first.

He read the passage twice, his expression giving away nothing beyond a stillness that deepened as he went. The archive room held its usual hush, shelves of older, more fragile texts lining every wall, and Iofiel waited through the silence without filling it.

"You're certain of the translation," he said finally.

"As certain as three independent passes can make me," Iofiel answered, keeping her voice level. "It's fragmentary. I don't have a location, only a description."

Gabriel set the page down carefully, as though it might tear under too much handling, and was quiet again a moment longer than she expected.

"Bring it to the Council tomorrow," he said. "Let me sit with it tonight first."

He did not explain what sitting with it meant, and Iofiel did not ask. She left with the strange, small relief of having handed a weight to someone else, at least for a few hours, before it became everyone's weight to carry. She did not sleep well that night either, turning the fragment's phrasing over long after there was anything left to translate.

The war room felt smaller than usual the next morning, though nothing about its dimensions had changed — only a tension Iofiel had learned to recognize over centuries of standing at the edges of rooms like this one. She arrived early enough to set the fragment's pages out in careful order before the others filed in.

She laid the fragment out for the assembled archangels in the same order she'd used with Gabriel the night before, translation notes included, uncertainties marked plainly. Raphael sat quiet near the far end of the table, listening with what struck Iofiel as the attentiveness of someone gathering information rather than preparing an argument. Others further down shifted in their seats with an impatience Iofiel read as a room already anticipating a fight. Uriel was on his feet before she'd finished the second sentence.

"If we can find this weapon, so can Hell," he said, pacing the length of the table, his boots striking the stone floor in a rhythm that matched the urgency in his voice. "We move now, before anyone else has the chance to."

Nothing in Uriel's words suggested he had anyone specific in mind, and Iofiel didn't either. She had turned the question over herself in the quiet hours before this meeting, without finding an answer that satisfied her — only the uneasy sense that whoever it was, they were unlikely to wait for a Council to finish debating.

Gabriel answered without rising from his seat, his voice carrying the weight Iofiel had noticed creeping into it over the recent months.

"We can't afford to divide our forces on a fragment this thin," he said. "Not with everything already unsettled elsewhere."

The reference needed no further explanation; every face at the table carried the same unspoken understanding of what "unsettled" meant lately, and Iofiel had no doubt Miguel's absence sat at the center of it.

"Thin or not, it's the only lead we have," Uriel shot back, "and leads like this don't stay thin forever."

The two of them had clashed before, plenty of times, but this argument carried an edge Iofiel hadn't heard from either in a while — Gabriel's caution sharpened, she suspected, by Miguel's absence more than by the spear itself, and Uriel's urgency sharpened by exactly the opposite instinct, a refusal to let caution become the reason nothing ever got decided in time.

"Everything is unsettled elsewhere, Gabriel," Uriel pressed on, leaning both hands against the table, "because we keep waiting for certainty before we act. Certainty is a luxury we stopped being able to afford months ago."

Gabriel's jaw tightened — the first real crack Iofiel had seen in his composure — though his voice stayed level.

"I'm not asking for certainty. I'm asking that we not gamble what little strength we can spare on a fragment of a sentence."

Neither seemed willing to yield the floor, and for several exchanges the debate simply repeated itself in slightly different words, each man restating his own logic of war as though restating it more forcefully might finally make the other hear it.

It was Zadkiel who finally broke the deadlock, and not in either direction the table had been pulling toward — pale obsidian armor, silver eyes that had a way of landing on a person like a verdict already reached before he'd said a word. He had said nothing through the whole exchange, arms crossed, those same eyes fixed on the fragment rather than on either man arguing over it. Uriel, mid-argument, had twice glanced toward him and twice looked away before finishing the thought, as though some part of him already knew how the sentence would be graded.

"Show me the location," he said, when the room had quieted enough for his voice to land. "Not a description. A location."

Iofiel opened her mouth to explain again that the text offered no such thing, and Zadkiel continued before she needed to.

"Then we have a description and a guess, and I will not commit soldiers to either."

His objection carried none of Uriel's urgency and none of Gabriel's caution — only a flat, procedural refusal to move resources on anything less than verified ground, the same standard she had watched him apply to every decision that had ever crossed this table. Decretum rested against the arm of his chair, unused, the way it always did in council rather than combat. Zadkiel himself sat as still through the whole exchange as the hammer did, silver eyes moving between speakers without ever betraying which argument was gaining ground with him.

Uriel started to object, and Zadkiel simply repeated himself, once, in the exact same words, until the objection died for lack of anywhere further to go.

"A guess is not a plan," Zadkiel added, when the silence had stretched long enough to make his point. "Bring me a location, and I will bring you soldiers. Until then, I will not spend them on a translation, however careful the translator."

He said it without looking at Iofiel directly, though she felt the acknowledgment in it — not an insult to her work, only the plain limit of what her work alone could purchase from this room.

The Council did not deny Uriel's urgency entirely, nor did it grant Gabriel the full caution he'd argued for, the final hour of debate settling into exhaustion more than agreement. What emerged was a compromise that answered part of each objection: a single scout, sent quietly, to confirm whether the fragment led anywhere real before any larger force committed to finding out.

Gabriel accepted the compromise with a nod that told Iofiel he'd expected nothing better and hoped for nothing worse. Uriel accepted it more grudgingly, arms folded in a posture Iofiel read as a man counting it a delay rather than a decision, though he offered no further argument once the room had settled. Zadkiel inclined his head once, apparently satisfied the standard of evidence had been met halfway, and gathered the hammer back into his grip as he rose without another word.

Iofiel watched the three of them disperse toward their separate corners of the room, already speaking with aides about logistics that had nothing to do with the fragment she'd brought here, and felt the strange anticlimax that always follows a decision this hard-won — all that argument, distilled down to a single scout and a quiet nod from three men who would likely not think of this meeting again until the scout's report came back. The meeting broke apart around her in the ordinary, unceremonious way meetings always did, the fragment she had spent weeks uncovering already folded into a single procedural line item on someone else's list.

Iofiel remained behind after the room emptied, her staff still against her shoulder, and let herself sit with the arithmetic of what had just happened.

A single scout was the responsible choice, by every measure the Council used to weigh its decisions — cautious, proportionate, unwilling to gamble real strength on a lead this thin. She had watched this same calculation play out a hundred times before, and couldn't recall the Council ever reaching a different conclusion when the evidence was this uncertain. She understood the logic completely, and agreeing with it did nothing to make it sit easier in her chest. Responsible and fast were rarely the same word, and the gap between the two had a way of being paid for later by people who had no seat at this table — soldiers on the ground somewhere, most often, who would never know a scout's cautious pace had been chosen over a faster, riskier one, or why. She gathered the fragment's pages back into their folder and sat a while longer in the quiet the Council had left behind, turning over a math that never quite balanced. Somewhere beyond this room, she suspected, someone was already moving with none of the caution this table had just spent an hour agreeing to — and no fragment in any archive was going to slow that down for her.
