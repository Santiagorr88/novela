# Chapter 20 — The True Tomb

The channel ended in a doorway that had not existed, as far as Belial could tell, until the exact moment he stood in front of it — not carved, not built, simply present where a moment before there had been only smooth crystal wall. He paused before it, one hand resting against the frame, listening for any further warning the plane might offer him, aware of how easily this could be another test disguised as an entrance.

He stepped through into a chamber colder than anything the outer plane had shown him, the air carrying less salt and more of something Belial could only think of as pressure, a weight against his chest that had nothing to do with his still-mending ribs. No chiming crystal answered his footsteps here. The silence was total, heavier than any he had crossed through so far.

The chamber's walls held none of the jagged black crystal that marked every other part of this plane — smooth instead, worn to a grey so pale it caught no light at all. He stood a moment just inside the threshold, letting his eyes adjust to a darkness that never quite resolved into true dark, breath fogging in air cold enough to make the sensation strange, and felt, for the first time since crossing into the Weeping Sepulcher, a reluctance to take the next step.

At the chamber's center stood a single slab of grey, featureless stone, unmarked by any name or inscription, alone in a room otherwise entirely empty — no altar, no guard, nothing to mark its significance beyond the weight already pressing against Belial's chest. Resting atop it lay a spear so plain it took him a moment to accept that this was the thing he had bled for.

No ornament. No radiance worth the name — only a faint, pale glow along its edge, dark metal otherwise, a weapon that made no claim to the kind of power Belial had spent his whole ambition chasing. He circled the slab once, studying it from every angle a commander might use to size up an unfamiliar opponent, boots silent against stone that swallowed even the small sound of his footsteps. It looked, if anything, like something a common soldier might have carried, not a weapon a scholar's fragment had called a thing of painful truth.

His first reaction was contempt, quick and instinctive — the same reflex that would have greeted any lesser demon presenting him a gift this humble. He had crossed a plane of guardians and grief for this, bled for this, humiliated himself before a thing made of crystalized sorrow for this, and the reward at the end of it looked like it belonged in some mortal armory, forgotten among a hundred others just as plain. Weeks of careful planning, a Commander's life spent to open the door, two journeys through a plane that had already put him on the ground once — all of it, apparently, in service of this single unremarkable length of dark metal. The contempt did not survive contact with what the spear actually radiated once he let himself feel past its plainness: an old, bottomless sorrow, patient and enormous, that pressed against him the way gravity presses rather than the way a threat presses. It was not weak. It felt restrained, and the restraint itself felt like the most dangerous thing in the room.

He reached for it anyway. Wanting had never waited on his own consent before, and it did not start now. The moment his fingers closed around the shaft, the vision took him whole.

He felt its making, slow and deliberate: water closing over metal that did not want to be shaped, salt and grief pressed into it the way pressure turns coal into something harder than it began, one patient season at a time. Only a long, deliberate patience moved through the process, the kind that comes from doing a necessary thing rather than a wanted one, over and over, until the doing itself had worn a groove into whoever was doing it.

He felt the one who had shaped it: an archangel, unnamed even in the vision itself, hands steady despite everything, giving the spear its form as an act of justice he no longer believed he had any right left to carry out. Belial saw, in fragments too immediate to hold at any distance, verdicts this maker had delivered against those he loved, duty discharged long after the conviction behind it had worn through entirely. The weight of that was not glory. It was closer to erosion — a slow grinding down of something that had once, clearly, believed in what it was doing.

The sorrow in it was not ornamental. It was structural — a grief woven into the weapon's shape itself, not merely resting on its surface. Belial recognized it before he wanted to admit recognizing it — not as something foreign, but as the exact species of sacrifice he had spent his whole long service holding in contempt in lesser men, and now, for one unbearable instant, understood from the inside.

A voice reached him from somewhere inside the vision, belonging to no one he could name, carried by the weapon itself rather than by any single throat.

*Why do you want to carry this?*

"Because no one else dares," Belial answered, without hesitation, the words leaving him before he had time to weigh them.

The voice did not correct him. It did not offer comfort or challenge either. It simply repeated the question, once, in the exact same tone.

*Why do you want to carry this?*

Belial found, on the second asking, that his answer no longer arrived as quickly. He opened his mouth to repeat the same words and stopped himself. The question, he realized, had not actually been asking what he'd assumed. He said nothing further. The silence that followed felt like the only reply the spear meant to give him — and it left him with the uncomfortable sense that his first answer had already told the weapon everything it needed to know about him.

Something in that silence nearly succeeded where the guardian's blows had failed. Belial felt his grip loosen, fractionally, an instinct rising in him that he did not immediately recognize as fear — he hadn't felt anything close to fear in longer than he cared to measure. It arrived the way fear always arrives for men who have trained themselves out of feeling it directly: sideways, disguised as a reasonable question about whether this prize was worth its price.

He took one half-step back from the slab, spear still in hand but no longer fully claimed, something in him still weighing that question with genuine and uncomfortable seriousness. He thought, in that half-step, of Foras cultivating loyalty back home while he stood here bargaining with his own nerve, and of every commander he had ever watched hesitate at a threshold that would cost more than they'd budgeted for. In anyone under his own command, he would have called this weakness and ended the man's career over it without a second thought. He found the judgment harder to make stick against himself, standing inside the hesitation rather than watching it from a safe distance — a sympathy that changed nothing about how badly it still needed shaking off.

The hesitation lasted only a moment, brief enough that no one watching would have noticed it. It lasted long enough, though, that he would remember it afterward as the closest he came in this chamber to walking away with nothing.

He tightened his grip instead, both hands closing hard around the shaft, and let the old, familiar anger take over where his uncertainty had failed him. The sorrow pressed harder against him as he did, as though it understood exactly what he was refusing.

"I am Belial," he said aloud, the words landing flat and hard against the chamber's total silence. "My will is my own."

It was not victory, not the clean kind he had always imagined claiming a weapon like this would feel like. It was refusal — a man denying, by sheer force of repetition, a feeling he had already, unmistakably, felt.

He said it again, louder, as though volume alone might finish the work conviction should have done. "My will is my own." His voice echoed once against walls that had swallowed every other sound in this chamber. The sorrow did not retreat. It sat there, patient, unmoved by his insistence, as it had sat through every vision before this one — and Belial understood, dimly, that he was not actually arguing with the spear at all. He was arguing with himself, and losing every exchange, and refusing anyway to let the loss show on his face.

He did not surrender to the sorrow the way some other claimant might have. He simply refused to let it finish what it had started in him, burying the recognition under willpower alone, and wrenched the spear fully free of the slab with a motion that cost him far more than the muscle behind it. His hands shook, briefly, once the weapon came free — not from any weakness in his grip, but from the sheer effort of holding a door shut against something that had come very close to forcing its way through anyway.

The moment the spear left the stone, the chamber's grief shifted — not toward him with hostility, but with something that felt, uncomfortably, like recognition, as though the room had simply been waiting to see who would prove stubborn enough to claim what it guarded. The pale glow along the spear's edge brightened faintly in his hand, a small, private acknowledgment, then dimmed again to the same quiet light it had carried when he first found it.

The pressure in the air eased all at once, the total silence loosening into something almost ordinary again, and Belial found he could breathe without the effort it had cost him since he first crossed into this chamber. He flexed his free hand once, testing the return of sensation to fingers gone half-numb during the vision, and looked down at the empty slab where the spear had rested for however long — years, centuries, longer than he had any real way of estimating.

He did not linger to examine what had just changed, or why, or what it meant that a room built from someone else's grief had apparently decided he belonged here now. Some questions, he had learned across centuries of taking what he wanted and settling the accounting afterward, were better left unexamined once the taking was done. He turned from the empty slab, spear finally, fully his, and walked back out through the doorway without once looking behind him.

The channel beyond felt different beneath his boots on the way out, though nothing about the crystal itself had visibly changed — quieter, perhaps, or simply less inclined to test him now that he carried something the plane itself seemed to have already accepted. Even the low chiming that had followed him through every other stretch of this plane had softened to something barely audible. He did not slow his pace once to investigate. Whatever waited beyond the portal, in a Hell that had spent his absence quietly rearranging its loyalties without him, he intended to meet it carrying proof that the absence had not been wasted. He had crossed a plane no soldier of Hell had crossed before him, and bent something ancient and grieving to his own will rather than the other way around. He did not look back once — not at the tomb, not at the doorway, not at anything the chamber still held behind him — only forward, toward the channel, toward the plane's outer reaches, toward the portal that would carry him back to Hell.
