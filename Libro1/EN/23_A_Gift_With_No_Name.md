# Chapter 23 — A Gift With No Name

Corin Vasse had spent six years fixing other people's cars in the same cramped garage on the edge of town, and most days he counted himself lucky for it; it was steady work, an honest trade, and he had a boss who left him alone as long as the bay stayed clean and the customers stayed happy. He had a particular fondness for older engines, the kind that still rewarded patience and a careful ear. That same afternoon he'd finished adjusting the cylinder head on a decade-old sedan for a woman who had nearly given up on it. His friend Dez worked the counter at the hardware store two doors down from the garage, and over the years the two of them had fallen into an easy, undemanding friendship built mostly out of proximity and a shared tolerance for the same handful of bars in this part of town. Corin wiped his hands on a twill rag already soaked through with oil, feeling the day's metal-work fatigue settle into his back and studying the persistent grey film of grease that no stiff-bristled brush ever quite scrubbed out from under his nails.

Halloran's greeted them with the same lazy hum that seemed not to have changed since the winter before. The place smelled of damp sawdust, the tallow of the candles on the bar, and the cheap beer the bartender poured without needing to ask anyone's order. In one corner, a faded neon sign that had needed replacing for years, and probably never would, flickered with a dull, rhythmic click. Dez was halfway through a long story about a coworker's disastrous attempt at fixing his own plumbing, and Corin sipped a beer he'd been nursing for the better part of an hour, letting the usual Tuesday murmur pass over the rim of his thick glass. It was an ordinary night, one of a thousand he expected to live before anything in his life actually changed.

The argument at the next table started small enough that Corin barely registered it, two men trading the sort of sharp words that usually burn out before anyone has to step in. He caught only fragments over Dez's voice: something about money owed, or a favor never returned. A few regulars nearby had already gone quiet, watching instead of talking, spreading the hush through the room as more attention turned toward the corner. Then, suddenly, chairs scraped back against the linoleum floor. One man's hand closed around the neck of a thick glass bottle with a grip that changed the temperature of the room. The bartender started moving toward them, calling out something about taking it outside, and Corin felt his pulse quicken with the ordinary instinct to brace for trouble about to break loose in too narrow a space.

Dez put a hand on his shoulder, murmuring some half-formed warning about staying out of it, but Corin was already rising to his feet. A wordless certainty had risen in him that this needed to stop — a pull he hadn't chosen any more than he'd chosen his own heartbeat. In the instant just before it happened, he felt a strange heat move through his chest and out along both arms, gone again almost before he could register it as strange.

The room changed all at once. The anger that a heartbeat before had clenched both men's jaws dissolved, the raised voices dropped, and hands unclenched from bottles and chair backs with no memory at all of having gripped them in the first place. One of the men reached out and gripped the other's shoulder, a gesture that landed less like a challenge and more like an apology neither had words ready for yet. A thread of ordinary fondness that must have been sitting quietly underneath it all along — the memory of regulars who drank at the same bar and shared the same cold floor tile through whole winters — rose up and pushed the violence toward the corners of the room. Corin stood there in the middle of it, breathing hard, with no idea what he had just done or how.

The whole bar seemed to exhale at once. A woman near the bar caught his eye with an odd, searching expression, and crossed the room to whisper a "Thank you" at him that landed with an intensity heavier than anything he felt he'd earned. "I didn't do anything," Corin told her, and meant it, though the words left his mouth thin. The bartender gave him a small, grateful nod on his way back behind the counter.

Corin sat back down across from Dez, his hands not quite steady around his glass. He turned his palms over, studying them, and found only the ordinary marks of his trade: the same calluses from years of engine work and the small scar on one knuckle from a wrench that had slipped years before, nothing in them that could account for whatever had just moved through that room.

"What did you say to them?" Dez asked, leaning in with curiosity. "Looked like you lifted something off all of them at once."

Corin shrugged, taking a pull from his glass.

"I didn't say anything strange. I just... talked."

He walked the three blocks home slower than usual, hands buried in his jacket pockets against a cold he barely registered. Beneath the confusion of the walk, with the wet asphalt throwing back the flat reflection of the streetlights, a smaller and quieter fear settled in for which he still had no words: the fear of what that same nameless part of himself, which had just acted on its own without asking his permission, might decide to do next.
