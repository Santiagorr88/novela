Chapter 35. The Irreverent Judgment.

Gabriel found him in the armory before the first choir of dawn began its rehearsal, standing amid the dust of cold metal and the long shadows the empty breastplate racks threw across the floor, still working through the wreckage of the fractured council table days after the fact, and Miguel suspected from the set of his brother's shoulders what conversation he had come to have before either of them said a word. "Don't launch this," Gabriel said, no preamble, no softening. "Not for strategy. I've read the reports — the outpost is minor, the risk is negligible. That's not why I'm asking you not to go."

Miguel kept his eyes on Solmire's hilt, checking a fitting that needed no checking. "Then why."

"Because you're not chasing a victory," Gabriel said. "You're chasing something to prove to yourself, after what happened in front of the entire Council, and that's exactly the kind of decision that already went wrong once." Miguel felt the words land somewhere he had no intention of letting Gabriel see. "You don't know that." Even as he said it, he understood how little conviction the denial carried. Gabriel held his gaze a while longer, as though waiting for something Miguel wasn't prepared to give, then simply nodded — resignation, as though he'd already known, before he opened his mouth, exactly how this would end.

He said nothing further. The fractured table in the council chamber had already said it more plainly than either of them could manage in words, and Miguel understood, watching his brother turn to leave without another attempt at persuasion, that the authority Gabriel's counsel still carried was no longer enough to stop him today. He did not call after him. He finished checking the fitting instead, feeling the cold of the chainmail cling to his ribs, and told himself the silence between them was simply practical — nothing left worth saying, not grief over what it meant.

He thought, once Gabriel's footsteps had faded down the corridor, of every other time his brother had come to him carrying a warning shaped just like this one, and of how easily, once, he would have listened. There had been a version of himself, not so long ago, who trusted Gabriel's read on a situation over his own certainty without being asked twice. He didn't know precisely when that trust had eroded into this — a warning heard and set aside in the same breath, a brother's counsel weighed and found wanting before it had even finished being spoken. He didn't have the space left in him today to examine the loss closely. He set it aside instead, alongside everything else he had learned to set aside since the sword first came into his hand.

The armory around him had gone very quiet in Gabriel's absence, the ordinary clatter of the Bastion's morning business feeling, for a moment, too distant to reach him. He finished his preparations methodically, the way he always had, though the calm felt thinner today, stretched over something that hadn't settled underneath. He told himself the thinness didn't matter. He had a target, and a purpose, and the sword's familiar weight already settling into his grip, promising him the clarity he had come here looking for.

He found the outpost by midmorning, a minor demonic garrison holding ground that mattered to no one's larger strategy — the kind of target that would once have felt beneath his notice. Today it felt like exactly what he needed. He had not slept properly since the council chamber, had not managed a single clean moment since his own fist split marble in front of every commander he answered to, and something quieter than the anger still smarting from Gabriel's warning understood exactly why this target had drawn him here. He needed to feel Solmire's old, absolute purpose again — clean and simple, without the weight that had been crushing him since the vision of the hand, since the laughter, since everything that had followed.

He announced the attack himself rather than delegating it, gathering the assault force with a decisiveness he hadn't managed in days, and didn't once let himself circle back to the warning he had already decided to ignore. There was only the outpost ahead, the sword's familiar weight in his hand, and the desperate hope that the old certainty still lived somewhere inside both of them.

The soldiers he gathered fell into formation without question, the way they always did around him, and watching them assemble, Miguel felt envy — envious of whatever simple confidence let them follow him this readily. None of them had stood in that council chamber. None had watched the table crack beneath his fist, or felt the naked horror of an entire command staring at him in the silence afterward. To them, he imagined, he was still exactly what he had always been — Miguel, sword of Heaven, certain and unshakable. He wanted, badly, to feel like that man again, if only for the length of a single clean battle.

The march to the outpost took most of the morning, the terrain rough enough to demand real attention, and Miguel used the effort of it gratefully — glad of anything that occupied the part of his mind still circling Gabriel's warning. He didn't speak much along the way. Few of the soldiers expected him to. He had never been a commander who filled marches with unnecessary conversation, and today the silence suited him better than ever, a private space in which he didn't have to perform the certainty he no longer entirely felt.

The battle, when it came, was over almost before it began. The demons holding the outpost were undertrained, underprepared, unequipped for the force Miguel had brought against them, and within minutes their formation broke apart into open, disorganized flight. Angelic soldiers pressed the advantage on every side, driving the collapse further, and as the rout unfolded exactly as cleanly as he needed it to, Miguel felt the first genuine ease he'd felt in days settle into his chest. A conventional victory. Nothing complicated about it. Exactly what he had come here to find.

He moved through the collapsing formation himself, Solmire meeting every blade that dared rise against it and ending the exchange in a single stroke each time, the sword's familiar rhythm settling into his arm the way it had before everything in his life grew so complicated. For a few brief minutes, cutting his way through a genuinely resisting line, he felt like the man he had been before the council chamber, before the dream, before any of it. He let himself have those minutes without questioning them too closely. He had come here for this feeling, and he wasn't ready yet to ask how thin it might prove underneath.

The demons who could still fight fell quickly, one after another, and those who couldn't began breaking for any cover the terrain offered, the disciplined resistance of moments ago collapsing into a desperate, undignified scramble.

The enemy no longer fought; the survivors fled disarmed, the wounded dragging themselves toward the brush at the rear in a desperate stampede that no longer posed the Host any threat. Miguel made to lower the blade, the old military protocol demanding he grant the beaten the decency of being let go — and Solmire shifted in his hand.

It wasn't a surge of light. It was an eager vibration, icy and predatory, that climbed his forearm like a current of frozen water. His arm rose on its own, the hilt fitting itself to his fingers with an unbearable fixity that asked no permission to move.

"No," Miguel screamed, in the only voice left that still belonged entirely to him in the dimness of his own skull, but his flesh didn't hear it.

The sword dictated the verdict on its own. A beam of absolute light, flat and stripped of heat, lanced from Solmire's edge, arcing with surgical precision to hunt down the stragglers already gaining the treeline. The wounded, disarmed fugitives didn't fall to the steel; they simply stopped being present in the space, their shapes unmaking into a white, mute dust the valley wind carried off before it ever touched the ground.

Miguel stood amid the aftermath and felt nothing that resembled triumph, only a deep, spreading sickness that had nothing to do with exertion and everything to do with what he had just watched his own hand do without his permission. There had been no honor in it. It hadn't been a battle, not in any sense he recognized. It had been vermin cleared from a field, a methodical, perfect erasure that treated life with the same indifference an accountant shows striking the wrong mark from a serge-bound ledger.

He understood, standing there, what the laughter in his dream had meant. It wasn't cruelty. It was worse: indifference. For the sword, and for whatever hand had first shaped it, this had never been war — war implied an opponent worth the name, stakes that ran both directions. This was something else — the methodical work of clearing vermin from a field, precise and thorough, unburdened by anything so complicated as an enemy's right to survive the encounter. He had been the instrument of that work today, and hadn't even understood it was happening until it was already finished.

He thought of the disarmed figure he had watched running toward the treeline, nameless to him and now erased entirely — a man already beaten who counted for nothing against a blade that recognized no meaningful distinction between a threat and a target already fleeing. He had told himself, moments ago, that he still recognized the line between combat and slaughter, some old discipline from his training holding firm even now. Solmire, apparently, recognized no such line — or recognized it only well enough to erase it deliberately, the moment Miguel's own judgment tried to hold it in place. He didn't know which possibility frightened him more.

The company returned to the Bastion in a silence that felt heavier and more accusing than mourning a hard defeat. None of the soldiers dared cheer the triumph, and Miguel offered them no speech that might explain why the Host had just executed an enemy in flight. Several veterans' gazes drifted to his gauntlet with an unease they tried to paint over beneath the discipline of the marching step, and Miguel filed it away alongside everything else he had begun hiding from his brothers. He had no explanation to give that wouldn't require admitting, in front of soldiers who he imagined still assumed the weapon answered entirely to him, exactly how little control he now held over it.

He returned to the Bastion in silence, and for the first time since Solmire's forging, set the blade aside the moment he crossed his own threshold, unable to bear its weight against his palm any longer than the walk back had already forced. He did not sheath it carefully. He kicked it, hard, sending it skidding across the stone floor to rest against the far wall, and the sound of the impact rang through the empty room louder than it should have, echoing back to him twice before the silence reclaimed the room.

He stood there a long while, staring at the blade from across the room, unwilling yet to cross the distance and retrieve it, the silence of the Bastion pressing in around him with none of the comfort it usually offered after a long day's fighting. He had returned to this same room countless times before, after battles that had cost him far more than today's had, and had always found some measure of peace waiting within these walls. Tonight the walls offered him nothing of the kind. Touching the blade now felt closer to touching the hand of his own executioner than reaching for a weapon he had once trusted without question. He didn't know, standing there in the silence of his own empty quarters, how he was supposed to carry a sword tomorrow morning that he could no longer bear to even look at tonight. He only knew that some line inside himself, long fraying, had finally broken completely, and that no amount of Gabriel's warnings, or his own denials, was going to put it back together again.
