Chapter 20. The True Tomb.

The black crystal channel ended in a doorway that, by his tactical eye's reckoning, had not had any real existence in the world until the exact instant he stood before it. It was not carved into the masonry, nor did it show the bronze bolts of Dis's prisons; it was simply there, present and patient, where a heartbeat before there had been only a smooth wall of frozen crystal. Belial stopped a pace short, resting his left gauntlet's palm against the jamb to ease the searing pull his cracked ribs sent with every breath. He listened closely, hunting for the hum of some psychic ward or the vibration of a trap, but the Weeping Sepulcher had already taught him its hostility needed no such devices.

He crossed the threshold, and the chamber's air received him with a physical pressure that pressed on his chest, a mineral density that had nothing to do with the ordinary cold of the brine outside. Here, the tremulous chime of the black crystal flagstones vanished entirely. The room's silence was absolute, solid, thick enough to cling to his temples with the dry taste of spent lime and old copper. The chamber's walls carried none of the plane's bright patina; they were a grey so pale and worn they seemed to swallow the pale mist of his own breath without giving back a single reflection of lamplight. Belial went still in the gloom, feeling for the first time a physical reluctance, an old soldier's dread, to take the next step onto stone that refused to give back the echo of his boots.

At the center of the empty room stood a single slab of grey stone, free of any inscription, name, or ornament that might betray what it had guarded in the silence. Resting atop it lay the spear, and Belial felt the roof of his mouth fill with the bitter taste of spent lime at the plainness of the metal.

His first reaction was the quick, visceral, clean contempt of a general who tallies the cost of a campaign and finds the spoils lack the dignity of the mud it took to win them. He had sacrificed a Commander of the Legions to open this road; he had dragged his own guard into a slaughter rehearsed in shadow, and had crossed a plane of mourning and broken ribs only to find an unremarkable length of dark metal. The spear was simple. It gave off none of Solmire's radiance, bore no runes of power along its shaft; it looked, if anything, like an ordinary blade a border soldier might have carried in his kit and forgotten in some common trench.

And yet the contempt did not survive the instant Belial let his perception cross past the metal's surface. The spear made no threat, but it radiated an old, structural, bottomless sorrow that pressed against his ribs with the same persistence gravity uses to bend a willow's branches. It was not a mystical weakness; it was a restraint so immense and patient that Belial understood, with a familiar tightness in his chest, that this dark metal was the most dangerous thing he had ever encountered in his entire existence.

He took it anyway, because Belial's ambition had never once waited for his own flesh's consent before acting, and his hand closed around the shaft in a defensive grip.

The vision struck him at once, stripping away the chamber's stone and returning him to a slow, patient, deliberate forge. He felt the frigid water closing over a metal that writhed under the hammer, resisting shape, and felt the salt and the grief pressed into the steel's fiber one silent season at a time, as though the maker himself had wanted every strike to be an act of mourning. And then, he saw the artificer.

It was a celestial archon whose name had been erased from the Codex Mortalis before the Bastion's first scribe ever learned to hold a pen. Its hands, long and pale as a dead star, held the spear with a steadiness born not of faith, but of duty's pure inertia. Belial experienced, in fragments of a lacerating immediacy, the verdicts this maker had passed against those it loved; the wound of every word spoken to fulfill a law its soul had stopped believing in millennia before. The weight of that spear was not the golden glory Heaven's hymns sang to recruits; it was a subtle erosion, a slow crumbling of one's own righteousness under the weight of a grief that refused to let its executioner go. Belial recognized that emptiness as his own, recognizing in the nameless archon the same utilitarian detachment he himself had used to log the flesh of his legions, and the truth of that symmetry ached in his bones.

A voice reached him from somewhere deep in the vision, a dull vibration that seemed to come from the dark metal itself rather than any physical throat. It carried none of Gabriel's celestial music, none of Uriel's fire; it was a flat, patient, glacial tone.

Why do you want to carry this? it asked.

"Because no one else dares," Belial answered at once, and his voice came out thin, stripped of its old authority in the crypt's stillness.

The spear's voice did not correct him, nor did it hurl any warning of fire at him. It let a heartbeat pass, letting the stone's silence swallow his answer, before asking the question again in the same flat, notarial rigor.

Why do you want to carry this?

Belial found that, the second time, his pride's lie no longer reached his tongue with the same readiness. He opened his mouth to repeat the same words and stopped himself. The question, he realized, had not actually been asking what he'd assumed. He understood that the spear was not measuring his worth as a general, but weighing the cost of what he was willing to lose.

And the fear reached him.

It was not the common fear of the mud, the recruit's hesitation before an enemy's steel; it was a cold in his side that reminded him of Foras. He saw, with the clarity of brackish water, Foras quietly cultivating the captains of his own court in his absence, rearranging the loyalties of Dis while he stood here alone and diminished before a piece of grey stone. If he took this metal, the affliction of its mourning would settle beneath his mail forever, and Foras would return to find a general who no longer knew how to command without doubt. He weighed the hesitation in the chamber's silence, feeling that a single second of doubt at a threshold like this was the true weakness — the one that would cost him the throne of bones it had taken him so long to raise.

The doubt lasted only an instant, the span of a single held breath, but Belial knew he would remember it as the moment he came closest to biting the dust of his own defeat. He tightened the grip of both hands on the shaft, forcing the plates of his black armor to contract with a furious hiss, and let Dis's old, familiar rage sweep the vision's sorrow aside.

"I am Belial," he said aloud, the words falling hard and unadorned onto the chamber's mute marble. "My will is my own."

It was not a clean triumph; it was a mutual refusal. Belial refused to surrender to the spear's mourning, burying the revelation under the pure force of his military ego, and pulled upward with an effort that cost him more than any skirmish on the front ever had. His cracked ribs groaned under his iron breastplate, and the altar's stone finally gave way with a dull groan, a vibration of cold recognition running through the whole chamber as the dark metal came free in his fist.

The pressure in the air eased at once, letting him breathe without the effort the vision had forced on him, and he flexed the gloved fingers of his left hand, feeling sensation return slowly to flesh gone half-numb with the archon's mourning, and looked at the now-empty slab. He had won, he told himself, as he holstered the mace Dubium and let Lamentum settle against his hip. But as he started back through the crystal channel, the chime of the flagstones fading to a cold murmur at his passing, he understood that the spear would not be leaving his side again. The nameless archon's structural sorrow had settled beneath his skin like a patient tenant that had no intention of asking his permission to remember its name.
