Chapter 7. Hunter of Echoes.

Camael found him in the lower weapons yard, where the air still tasted of the cold lime of freshly whitewashed walls and the sour sweat of recruits gathering up the sparring boards. Camael had not removed his boiled-leather breastplate, and the runes across his bronzed torso pulsed with the lazy glow of an ember refusing to die under the ash.

Sariel did not look up from Penumbra. He ran a gray linen cloth along the spear's ash-wood shaft, a slow, circular, rhythmic motion he had repeated so many times across three ages that his hands no longer needed his eyes to find the wood's small imperfections.

"There's a demon masking itself in a mortal host, in a city called Navarion," Camael said. His voice was dry, military, stripped of the music the Council's heralds used to make orders sound like favors. "I want you on it."

Sariel stopped the cloth. The silence that followed was its own kind of question, a space Camael did not bother filling with unnecessary explanations.

"Why me?" Sariel asked at last. His voice came out economical, stripped of any excess inflection, a tool only unsheathed when the work required it.

"Because souls don't lie to you," Camael answered, already turning on his heel to head back toward the guard pit, as though the matter had settled itself in the air the instant the words left his mouth. "And this hunt needs someone who can't be talked into anything."

The mortal city surrounded him with garish colors and layers of sound he could not fully parse, the way it always did on the rare occasions his duty brought him to Earth. Sariel did not track his target by sight. He tracked it by scent — not smell in any sense a human would recognize, but a faint, specific wrongness beneath the ordinary noise of human souls, sulfur and spiritual rot masked under a thin veneer of borrowed humanity.

The doctrine of his office had said it since before Sariel existed: any soul with its account unsettled returns to life as many times as it takes, until the judgment balances. He had never needed to believe it or to doubt it. He only followed the trail.

He moved through the crowded streets unseen, invisible to every mortal eye by simple virtue of not choosing to be seen, a lone purpose walking quietly through a world that had no idea it was being walked through at all. Vendors called out prices he did not need and never would. A pair of lovers argued near a bus stop, oblivious to him passing within arm's reach of either of them. His want was uncomplicated: find the trail, follow it to its end, close the matter cleanly. He had done this a hundred times before. He expected no different tonight.

The trail led him past a market square filled with mortal commerce, past children chasing each other around a fountain, past an old man arguing cheerfully with a vendor over the price of fruit. A lone street musician played something slow and mournful near the square's edge, coins scattered thin in the open case at his feet, and a small crowd had gathered to listen despite the evening chill. None of them so much as glanced in Sariel's direction. None of them had any reason to suspect that the war that might determine their afterlives continued beyond their perception. It might also decide whether reality itself endured.

Sariel had walked among mortals often enough, over centuries of similar assignments, that their ignorance no longer struck him as remarkable. It struck him, tonight, as close to mercy. Whatever else this war cost, it had at least spared these particular strangers the burden of knowing what stood behind the sky. He passed the musician's case without dropping a coin into it, a small gesture that would have meant nothing to the man and cost Sariel nothing at all to make, and found himself, briefly, wondering why he had not made it anyway.

The mortal city pressed in around Sariel with a thick, disordered weight that made him narrow his eyes. To a hunter accustomed to the flat, shadowless light of the Bastion, the Earth was a maze of garish colors, currents of warm air that smelled of frying grease, and layers of sound he could not fully unravel. He crossed the Navarion campus unseen by the students, simply because he had decided there was no use in being seen.

Young men and women passed within arm's reach of him, their cheeks flushed with the evening chill, arguing with fierce enthusiasm about overdue assignments, spilled cups of tea, and the small, urgent tragedies of lives that would end, by Heaven's own reckoning, impossibly soon.

Sariel stopped beside the central fountain, watching a couple laughing beneath a lazy drizzle. He felt no contempt; he felt something older, close to envy or to pity. Those mortals did not know that the vault of the sky above their heads had cracked, or that the generals of the Heavenly Host had just signed their first retreat. Their ignorance, he thought as a student brushed past his shoulder without noticing him, was the only form of mercy that still functioned in the world.

The instrument in the basement archive had been humming at an unusual frequency for three days running, and Professor Calder had stopped trying to explain it to anyone who asked. The archive itself was little more than a small, converted storage room, tucked away beneath the main library where a previous generation of the physics department had installed equipment nobody currently on staff fully understood, and Calder had inherited responsibility for it the way one inherits an elderly relative's furniture — without being consulted, and without any real choice in the matter.

He was not, by his own admission, the sort of scholar equipped to interpret it properly — his specialty was pre-modern architecture, not whatever branch of physics this particular device had originally been built to measure, decades before his tenure, by a predecessor whose notes had long since gone missing. He only knew how to read the needle, and the needle had been climbing steadily since Tuesday, in patterns that did not match anything in the maintenance log left behind by the device's original custodian, a log itself half-illegible with age and written, apparently, in a private shorthand no one currently on staff could fully decipher.

He made a note of it, as he had every day this week, and filed the note away in a drawer that already held several unanswered questions about this building — a door that occasionally would not stay locked, a section of the archive that ran colder than the rest of the basement for no reason the maintenance staff could identify. Someone with the right expertise would eventually take an interest. It was not, he was fairly certain, anything that concerned him directly.

He straightened the stack of unfiled paperwork on the desk beside the instrument, a habit he kept more for his own peace of mind than for any real necessity, and glanced once more at the needle before switching off the lamp for the night, half-expecting, absurdly, that it might have settled back to normal in the time it took him to gather his coat. It had not. He closed the drawer, locked the door out of habit rather than any real sense of urgency, and climbed the basement stairs into a campus already quiet for the evening, going home telling himself, as he had every night this week, that it would surely resolve itself by morning.

Sariel was following the trail of spiritual rot — a thin thread of sulfur and burnt grease smell dragging toward the library — when the air of the quad changed all at once.

It was not a gust of wind, nor a drop in spiritual temperature, though it resembled both. The air of Navarion turned suddenly flat, glacial, and dense, as though the entire quad had become a block of black ice that refused to let any lie pass through it.

Sariel stopped dead, his hand closing around Penumbra's shaft in a defensive pull. His gaze was drawn, against his own discipline, to the man sitting alone on the low stone wall.

He looked like a retired mercenary, a man pushing forty in a worn canvas jacket, his black hair cut with the haste of someone who has no time for mirrors. A thin scar crossed his throat, a pale line disappearing beneath a dirty wool collar, and he sat in a particular way — not to rest, but watching the angle of the avenue with the economical stillness of an old soldier. He did nothing at all. But the density radiating from his mortal shape was an absolute Sariel had never felt outside the hall of Heaven's judges.

And then the guilt struck him.

It was not a thought; it was a physical wound opening in his chest. The memory of a minor infraction committed three thousand years ago — a sentinel who had fallen asleep at the Gate of Onyx, whom Sariel had chosen not to report out of nothing more than fraternal pity — rose to his throat with the dry taste of copper. He felt a violent, desperate, irrational urge to fall to his knees on the damp gravel of the quad, to confess to that stranger in the worn jacket every lie he had ever used to keep his record infallible. The dread of his own judgment closed around his chest.

Sariel forced himself to take one step forward, then another, dragging his boots as though the gravel were thick mud, refusing to kneel. When he finally crossed the library's threshold and the shadow of the archway swallowed him, the icy air let go of his lungs all at once. He leaned against the brick wall, trembling beneath his clean robe, understanding that he had just passed something Heaven had given him no name for.

He found his actual target in the library's lower stacks not long after, the demon's borrowed host slumped over a desk between two towers of unread textbooks, showing no sign that he knew a war was being waged for the seat of his own soul. Sariel approached without hesitation, grateful, in a distant and unexamined way, for a task simple enough to steady him after what had just passed on the quad. His touch was quick and silent, the spear's point finding the corrupted essence beneath the human shell and unmaking it cleanly, without spectacle. The host slumped further into an ordinary, dreamless sleep. If this cleansing followed the pattern Sariel had seen a hundred times before, the student would wake sometime in the morning with no memory of having carried anything but himself.

It should have felt like the end of the matter. It had, every other time, in every other city — a clean kill, a cleared assignment, nothing left to occupy him but the walk home. Sariel turned to leave the library and found his gaze drawn back, against his own intention, toward the quad and the empty stretch of wall where the young man had been sitting. He was already gone now. The unease he had left behind was not.

Sariel told himself, as he left the campus, that it had been some anomaly of the place itself — old buildings sometimes held old resonances, and universities built over centuries occasionally sat atop stranger foundations than their architects had ever documented. It was, he reasoned, the sort of place where a leyline might run close enough to the surface to unsettle even a seasoned hunter's instincts. He told himself this several times, in fact, on the long walk back to the place he would open a way home, reviewing the explanation as critically as he would a suspect report. He did not entirely believe it by the time he arrived.

The official report Sariel filed before the night was through was impeccable, written in the same crisp, unadorned hand Camael expected of his captains.

«Target located. Confirmed. Unmade without incident. Host unharmed.»

He wrote every word with a steady hand, feeling the honest scratch of the pen on parchment, but his mind stayed caught in the damp quad by the library, on the black-haired silhouette that had already left the wall by the time he stepped out. He did not mention the man with the scar. He did not write a single line about the wound that had opened in his own memory, nor about the taste of copper that still lingered bitter at the back of his tongue.

He told himself, as he put away the old horn inkwell, that the omission was reasonable. An unverified suspicion was not evidence, and Camael had no patience for a scout's hunches. But standing alone in the stillness of his chamber, Sariel understood that this justification was the cleanest lie of all. He had left out the man on the wall not to protect the report, but to protect himself — so he would not have to admit that his infallibility had cracked before the spear had even left its sheath.
