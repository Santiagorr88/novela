Chapter 40. Three Were Forged.

Iofiel held the brittle fragments of the Codex Mortalis suspended within a sphere of preservative light, each shard of ancient parchment turning slowly in place like something caught mid-thought. Her staff, Memnón, hummed low in her free hand, its ancient-memory working straining against pages that had not been meant for eyes like hers. She had spent three days coaxing the text toward legibility, and the effort had begun, by the second day, to cost her more than she had budgeted for.

The scriptorium around her had gone dark hours ago, its archivists dismissed for the night, leaving only the sphere's pale glow and the tall shelves of catalogued memory stretching up into shadow. She had chosen this hour deliberately — the Codex responded better, in her experience, to attention undivided by the ordinary business of a working scriptorium. The fragments would say what they had to say more readily to a room with nothing else asking for its attention.

The Codex resisted her the way old, stubborn things resisted anything that asked them to change. She could feel it in the staff's resonance each time she pressed further — a text this old, forged before whatever schism first split Heaven from what it had once shared with Hell, had never been written for a Guardian of Divine Memory to read aloud centuries later. She pressed anyway, matching her own patience against the parchment's.

She had catalogued fragments of far younger texts across her years in the archive, records that yielded their contents readily enough once properly coaxed. This one fought her at every stage, each small concession costing more than the last, until she had begun, by the third day, to wonder whether the resistance itself was a kind of message — whether the Codex withheld itself not from carelessness but from some older instinct toward self-preservation, a text that understood exactly how dangerous its own contents might prove to whoever finally read them whole.

The thought carried her, unbidden, back to a hymn filed months ago and not revisited since — a single stanza two of her archivists, Kurel and Daith, had returned to her marked illegible, the word doing exactly as much work as they'd needed it to and no more. She had pressed them once, lightly, and let the answer stand when it came back unchanged. She did not, sitting here now with her own stubborn fragment refusing to yield its meaning cleanly, regret the choice. But she recognized the particular weight of it in a way she hadn't quite managed to, months ago, from the other side of someone else's redaction — the private arithmetic of deciding how much of a half-understood thing belonged in a permanent record, and how much simply had to remain a gap, unresolved, with nothing more honest yet to put in its place. She set the memory aside without following it further, the way she had learned to set aside most things that offered comparison but no answer. The Codex in front of her had already asked her that same question twice this week. She doubted it would be satisfied with the same answer twice.

Her hands had begun, sometime in the last hour, to show the faint tremor she associated with a working carried past its comfortable limit. She noted it with a scholar's detachment, and pressed the sphere's light a fraction brighter to compensate. Whatever this text ultimately cost her, she had already decided, somewhere in the small hours of the first night, that the cost would be worth paying.

Gabriel found her there on the third evening, entering the scriptorium without announcement in a way she had long since stopped finding intrusive. He carried what looked to her like the same weight she had noticed settling onto him since Miguel's descent into Solmire's judgment and Belial's confirmed return with Lamentum — a weight that hadn't visibly lightened since, and showed no sign of lightening soon.

He crossed the scriptorium without his usual measured pace, and she noted the difference without remarking on it. He stopped a short distance from the sphere, close enough to see the fragments turning within it but not so close as to disturb the working — a courtesy she appreciated more than she said aloud.

He did not ask what she had found. He asked only whether she had eaten, whether she had slept, whether the strain in her posture had a limit she intended to respect before it became something worse than fatigue. She answered honestly enough — not yet, not truly, and no — and he did not press further, some old understanding between them holding that she would stop working when the work itself demanded it and not before.

"Send for me the instant it's ready to be read," he said, already turning back toward the door, his voice carrying less than its usual careful cadence. "Not before. I have nothing to offer this except my own exhaustion, and you have enough of that in the room already."

She studied him a moment before he reached the door, weighing whether to press him on whatever else had clearly followed him here tonight. She decided against it, some instinct telling her that what she took to be his burden seemed, for now, his alone to carry — the same way what the Codex was about to yield belonged, for now, to her.

He left before she had answered, before the text had yielded anything concrete enough to share, and the door closing behind him left Iofiel with an odd gratitude that he hadn't stayed to see what came next.

She returned to the sphere once the scriptorium had settled back into its familiar quiet, the staff's hum steadying into the rhythm she had spent three days building toward. The resistance began, gradually, to ease into cooperation, one line near the fragment's opening finally stabilizing into legible script, its letters glowing with a faint silver light that hadn't been there an hour earlier.

Before the Divine Word gave form to law—

She read the line twice before allowing herself to register what it implied. The phrasing alone unsettled her more than any single fact she had yet extracted from the Codex's stubborn pages. It gestured, plainly and without apology, toward a time before whatever authority currently governed Heaven's own law had existed to govern anything at all. She had spent centuries preserving memory for a living, and had never once encountered a claim quite this foundational, or quite this dangerous to have surfaced in a room with no one but herself to witness it.

She set the staff's working carefully, resisting the urge to rush the remaining fragments. The rest of it would reveal itself on its own terms or not at all — she had learned that lesson three times over already, each attempt to hurry the process costing her more strength than the gain in speed justified. She steadied her breathing and waited for the next line to surface.

The rest of the line surfaced in the minutes that followed, each word arriving with the same reluctant clarity as the first, until the full sentence finally settled, complete, into the space above the sphere.

Three were forged before time; one to judge, one to suffer, and one to create.

The words did not merely appear before her. They impressed themselves directly upon her mind with the force of thunder, each of the three verbs arriving in turn. Judge. Suffer. Create. She had translated prophecy before, had handled fragments of far darker implication across her long service to the Codex's slow reconstruction. Nothing had prepared her for how simply, how devastatingly plainly, this sentence chose to state itself.

She read it a third time, searching out of habit for the ambiguity that usually accompanied text this old — the alternate readings, the deliberately unstable grammar that let older prophecy mean several things at once. She found none here. The line read as though it wanted to be understood exactly as written, and that plainness troubled her nearly as much as the claim itself.

She understood the first two implications before she had even finished absorbing the sentence, the connections arriving with a speed that frightened her more than the prophecy's content alone would have. Solmire was the sword that judged — she needed no more than Miguel's own blade, wielded now with an irreverence that had already begun to trouble every angel who had witnessed it in Zaphor'el and beyond. Cassiel's report from Turein surfaced in her unbidden — a blade whose true origin predated Miguel's own claim to it, he had told her, unable to offer more than that fragment at the time. She had set the claim aside as an open question with no obvious answer. It had one now. Lamentum, the spear of suffering, could only be the weapon Belial had carried back through the portal he had torn open, radiating a pain that had announced itself to half of Heaven's command before anyone had known its name.

Two of the three, already loose in the world. Two of the three, already shaping the war Heaven and Hell were fighting, whether either side yet understood the shape of what they were actually fighting over. The third weapon — the one forged to create, whatever creation meant in the hands of something old enough to predate the Divine Word itself — remained entirely unaccounted for, and the forgers behind all three remained a silence the Codex had offered nothing yet to fill.

She turned the two confirmed connections over in her mind, testing them against everything she already knew of both weapons, searching for any detail that might weaken the association rather than strengthen it. She found none. Every report she had read of Miguel's blade in Zaphor'el, every account of the portal Belial had torn open, folded neatly into the prophecy's own language, judgment and suffering arriving in the world in exactly the shapes the Codex claimed they had been forged to take. She had hoped to find some flaw in her own reasoning. Instead she had only further confirmation, and confirmation, in this case, offered her far less comfort than doubt would have.

The knowledge settled into her with a weight she hadn't adequately prepared for, and she lowered herself slowly into the chair behind her, Memnón's hum finally quieting. Whatever strength she had spent across the last three days had been the smaller expense. This — sitting with what she now knew, unable to set it back down, unable to share its full weight with anyone who hadn't read it directly from the page themselves — was the larger one, and she had only just begun to pay it.

She thought of Gabriel's instruction to send for him the instant the text was ready, and hesitated over the decision in a way she rarely allowed herself to hesitate over anything. He would need to know soon, before this knowledge finished settling into shapes she could no longer control the telling of. But the third question the Codex had left her with — the identity of the weapon still unaccounted for, and of whoever had forged all three before time itself had fully taken hold — offered her nothing yet to hand him beyond the shape of the question itself.

She weighed, sitting there with the sphere's light dimming slowly around her, how much of what she now knew was hers to share tonight, and how much would only sharpen into something more dangerous with a few more hours of consideration first. Gabriel carried enough already, by his own account not an hour earlier. She would not add an unfinished revelation to that weight if a single night's further study might finish it properly instead.

She rose from the chair slowly, testing her strength before trusting it, and crossed to where the last unread fragments still waited within the sphere's dimming light. Whatever remained of tonight's working would go toward preparing herself for tomorrow's return to the text rather than forcing anything further from it now. She had learned that lesson once already this week, at some cost. She had no wish to relearn it twice.

And the third? The one that creates?
