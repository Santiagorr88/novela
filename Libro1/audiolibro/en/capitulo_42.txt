Chapter 42. The Master of Lamentum.

Belial drove the spear forward against his own lieutenant's guard and felt his knees buckle half a second before the blow landed — Lamentum's grief flooding up through his grip, a force that had nothing to do with the parry meeting it. He had trained against lesser weapons for centuries without once losing his footing mid-strike. The spear made a mockery of that history every time he raised her.

He forced himself upright, jaw locked against the sound he refused to let escape, and reset his stance. For days he had tried to wield her the way he wielded every weapon before her — force met with force, victory to whoever struck truer. The spear answered none of it. Every strike he landed sent some fraction of its grief back through his own arm, as if it resented being used for something as simple as combat.

Rovash — a lieutenant who had served him loyally since his earliest campaigns — pressed the advantage without hesitation. Belial's guard came a fraction late; only instinct built over centuries turned the blow onto his shoulder instead of his ribs, and even that mercy cost him a grunt he couldn't fully swallow.

The training hall was carved from the same black stone as the rest of his command, its high walls doing nothing to soften his own labored breathing as it echoed back at him. He had chosen this apartness deliberately, so that the spear's cost to him would play out where his enemies could not see it. He had not accounted for how many of his own demons would see it instead.

He reset his footing a third time, refusing the brief respite Rovash's fatigue offered him, and drove the spear forward again. Its grief answered as it always did — not the clean resistance of a poorly balanced weapon, but something that felt, each time, like an old wound reopening in a place he couldn't point to on his own body. He had faced physical pain without flinching more times than he cared to count. This was not that. This went past the body, straight for whatever still remained, under all his armor, capable of grieving.

By the third pass the training ground had gathered a crowd — demons of considerable standing pausing their own drills to watch their commander's spear falter against a lieutenant who would ordinarily have posed him no challenge at all. Belial felt their attention without looking up to confirm it, a familiar weight from centuries of command. Until recently it had carried more reverence than what he sensed gathering around him now.

He caught Rovash's expression shift mid-exchange, the lieutenant's usual deference thinning into something far more calculating, as blow after blow found its mark against a spear its wielder plainly could not control. Belial had built his own rise through exactly this kind of opening — weaker commanders faltering, someone quicker moving in to claim what the weakness left exposed. He recognized the same arithmetic forming behind Rovash's eyes, and behind the eyes of every demon watching from the ground's edge, and knew how little time he had before that recognition hardened into a certainty words wouldn't answer.

He had commanded ambitious subordinates for centuries and knew better than to mistake their loyalty for anything more permanent than the strength that justified it. He did not resent Rovash for the calculation forming behind his eyes — he would have run the same one himself, in Rovash's position, watching a commander falter this badly against a weapon he'd claimed as a triumph only weeks before. Resentment was an effort he had no surplus to spend. He filed the thought away with every other piece of intelligence a commander needed to survive his own ranks, and returned his attention to the spear still fighting him.

He went to one knee under the next exchange, Lamentum's grief surging through him with a force that filled his vision with an image he hadn't summoned and didn't welcome — a weeping archangel, vast and sorrowful, bent under a grief too large for any one frame to hold. It passed as quickly as it had come, leaving him gasping on the floor while his lieutenants watched in a silence louder than any jeer would have been.

He understood, kneeling there, what he had been doing wrong every session. He had spent his will trying to silence the spear's grief completely, treating it as an enemy to crush like any other. That approach cost him everything and returned nothing — every hour forcing the grief inward left him weaker for the next exchange, the spear's sorrow with nowhere to go but back through his own body.

He thought back over every session since the Sepulcher, cataloguing the pattern he had failed to notice until now. Each day he trained harder against the spear's grief, and each day it answered with greater force, as though his resistance fed whatever fueled it rather than wearing it down. Every other weapon of his long career had taught him that force applied consistently eventually broke what opposed it. This one had offered nothing but proof to the contrary, week after week, and he'd been too stubborn to read it until now, on his knees.

He did not need to stop resisting Lamentum. The thought arrived with a clarity that steadied him more than the pain had unsteadied him a moment before. He needed to stop spending that resistance on himself, and start aiming part of it outward. The grief was not a curse to make peace with. It was a weapon he hadn't learned to throw yet.

The vision of the weeping figure lingered at the edge of his thoughts even as the clarity settled into something useful, and he set it aside, unwilling to spend what remained of his concentration on an image he hadn't asked for and didn't need explained. Whatever it had actually been, it had arrived alongside the one insight days of fruitless repetition had failed to give him — and that, he judged, was the only part worth keeping tonight.

He rose without conceding a single measure of ground to the spear itself, his will locked against her as hard as it had been since the moment he claimed her from the Weeping Sepulcher. He would not surrender to it — surrender had never once been an option he was willing to entertain. What he opened instead, for the first time, was an outlet: a channel through which the grief he'd spent days containing could be aimed, precisely, at whoever stood opposite him.

He drove the next thrust forward with that intention behind it, and watched Rovash's guard collapse — not from the blow's force, but from something far worse arriving with it. The lieutenant went to his knees mid-parry, weeping openly, overwhelmed by what looked, to anyone watching, like the accumulated weight of every betrayal and failure his long service had ever cost him.

The cost remained his own to bear. Directing the grief outward cost him nearly as much as containing it had — a fresh wave of sorrow bleeding through his own chest with every ounce he redirected. He straightened anyway, jaw set against the strain, no closer to peace with the spear than he'd been at the start of the session, and a long stride closer to mastering what she could do to someone else.

He tested it again against a fresh opponent, wanting proof the first success hadn't simply been Rovash's own weakness catching him at a convenient moment. The result held. A second lieutenant crumpled under the same directed grief, then a third, each collapse costing him roughly the same toll — a consistency that told him he'd found something real, not something merely lucky. He filed the confirmation away with the same cold satisfaction he gave any tested strategy, and set aside, for now, the price each demonstration kept extracting from him.

Among the gathered legionnaires stood Foras, his multiple unblinking eyes fixed on the spectacle, giving away nothing of what moved behind them. Belial noticed him without turning his focus fully from Rovash — the habit of reading a room too deeply worn into him to switch off, even mid-working.

Foras's position had grown more precarious since Belial's return from the Weeping Sepulcher, and narrower still from the ground he'd already been losing to Vepar — a commander weakened on two fronts has little appetite for watching a rival grow more essential by the week. Watching Lamentum finally answer to its wielder gave Foras no new tool against him. It left him less room to maneuver than he'd had an hour earlier: a Belial who couldn't be questioned was a Belial who couldn't easily be replaced.

Belial held Foras's gaze a moment longer than necessary before returning his attention to the training ground — a small, deliberate cruelty he permitted himself without regret. Whatever calculations ran behind those unblinking eyes, Foras had likely already reached the same conclusion Belial would have in his place, and Belial felt no urge to make it easier for him.

He had never counted Foras among his allies, exactly, in all the years both had served the same cause — a useful subordinate, competent enough to leave to his own devices, but not a commander whose ambitions he'd mistaken for loyalty. He felt no particular satisfaction watching Foras's position narrow further tonight, only the same flat calculation he applied to anything that left his authority more secure than an hour before. A war on two fronts, Heaven and his own restless command, was one he intended to keep weighted in his favor on both counts.

He gathered his legions before the week was out, Lamentum raised overhead in a grip that no longer betrayed him with every movement, the spear's grief flowing outward through him now instead of pooling uselessly within. The ranks fell silent as he raised his voice, a cold despair radiating from the weapon in his hand and settling over the gathered demons before he spoke a single word.

He had spent the days since testing it against opponent after opponent, refining his aim the way he refined any weapon meant for a real campaign rather than a training hall. The cost hadn't lessened with practice, but he'd grown far more efficient at absorbing it, spending only what each demonstration required. He stood before his legions tonight with that efficiency tested, and no illusions left about what tomorrow's campaign would keep costing him, whatever victories it delivered against Heaven's ranks.

"We will not break their shields," he said, his voice carrying easily across the assembled ranks, Lamentum's edge catching what little light the fortress offered and turning it the color of old grief. "We will break their hearts."

The legions answered with a roar that shook the fortress walls. Belial felt, standing at their head with the spear finally answering his will, more satisfaction than peace — a war he hadn't yet finished fighting against the weapon in his own hand, folded now into the war he intended to win against everyone still foolish enough to stand against him.

He found Lucifer in the throne room rather than the library, a distinction worth noting before he crossed the threshold. His king received him among a scatter of courtiers too well trained to listen openly to anything they hadn't been invited to hear, seated with the loose, unbothered elegance of someone who had never once needed to sit up straighter to be taken seriously. The hall's ordinary murmur thinned to nothing the moment Belial's shadow crossed the doorway, courtiers finding somewhere else to look, the whole room settling into a stillness built entirely around the man on the dais.

Belial knelt and set Lamentum across both palms, point turned aside, haft toward his king.

Lucifer studied the spear a long moment before he studied its bearer. "The weapon that broke a dozen garrisons in a single night," he said, without a trace of mockery, which somehow made it worse, "and its master kneels to hand it away. Tell me why."

"It answers to Hell." Belial kept his eyes on the haft rather than the man above him. "It should answer to Hell's throne."

"You spent weeks learning to survive her. Weeks I watched you master a grief no other hand in my service could have carried an hour." Lucifer rose, and crossed the short distance between them unhurried, black wings pulling what little light the hall held toward him rather than away. "Ambitious men do not usually give away the one thing that finally made them irreplaceable."

Belial said nothing. There was no answer that wouldn't have cost him more than the silence did.

Lucifer's fingers closed around the haft, just above Belial's own, and did not immediately take the weight. "Loyal ones don't, either. Which leaves me curious which of the two I'm currently watching kneel in front of me." He lifted the spear free at last. Belial felt the absence of it before he registered that his own fingers had opened — a lightness where the grief-weight had sat for weeks, gone in a single motion, and beneath the relief, something that felt uncomfortably close to loss.

"I told you once there were old songs about weapons like this." Lucifer turned the spear in his own grip, testing a weight he had never carried before. "I find I would rather hold the answer than hear it secondhand. They will not expect me on that field, and that alone is worth more than one more Lord bleeding himself dry against Heaven's Champion for a victory the histories will forget by the second age." His gaze had already drifted past Belial, toward the doors. "You found her. You broke her to your will before I ever took an interest. That will not go unremembered, whatever field she ends this war on."

Belial bowed his head, and told himself the gesture was gratitude, and did not entirely believe it. He had spent weeks learning to survive the one weapon that had made him feared by his own officers, and had just knelt, of his own choosing, to hand the whole of that mastery to the one throne even his ambition had never once considered challenging. It was obedience, offered freely and without complaint, exactly as it always had been. It cost him more than any of the weeks he'd spent bleeding for her in the training hall, and he left the throne room telling himself the cost had been worth it — in the same tone he would later use, on a distant ash-grey plain, to tell himself he had won.
