Chapter 26. Two Offers.

The hunt had cost Sariel more time and more glory-stripped patience than any assignment his military memory could recall across three centuries of service. It had been weeks of slow, stubborn tracking, a trade of dust and wet cobblestone built on nothing more substantial than the tremulous resonance still clinging to his spear's edge, and a cold stubbornness that had refused to let the matter die in the liaison's records. The trail had brought him, finally, to this unremarkable alley on the outskirts, where walls of wet brick smelled of rancid kerosene and the old grease seeping from the garage's joints.

Sariel stopped at the corner of the block, hidden in the shadow of a cracked eave, and let his body take on that stone stillness the Bastion's recruits so often mistook for a lack of life. He was not alone. Near the garage's side gate, a second figure was talking with Corin Vasse, his posture so relaxed and so pleased with itself that Sariel's professional eye caught the anomaly before his mind could put a name to it. Vual wore a mortal's fine tailored clothes, but the systematic ease of his smile and the comfort with which he occupied a space that wasn't his gave away Hell's thick, greasy signature. The hunter weighed the scene with the coldness of a man auditing a casualty count: the confused civilian, the seducer weaving his net of favors, and the evening wind dragging coal dust past without anyone on the street noticing that the ground beneath their feet had already grown far too thin.

Sariel crossed the remaining stretch of street without hurrying, but with the coiled readiness of a soldier who has spent ages learning that a conversation that starts in an alley tends to end in a clean wound of steel. His boots made no sound against the wet cobblestones, but Vual turned before the first shadow touched his shoulder, and there was no surprise at all in that motion; it was the rehearsed gesture of a swindler who always waits to see who reaches the bargaining table first.

"Let him go, Vual," Sariel said, no preamble attached to the name, no room in his voice for anything but the demand itself.

"I don't have him tied to anything, Sariel," Vual answered, spreading his gloved hands in a gesture that managed to look both innocent and unbothered, in full view of the spear hanging at the hunter's hip. "We're only talking about engines. And about Tuesday night."

Corin Vasse looked between the two of them in turn, and the confusion darkening his face was the clearest proof that the civilian was beginning to understand that the conversation he had let his curiosity wander into had become a pit too deep to climb out of on his own.

"It's simple enough," Vual said, ignoring the spear Penumbra to pour all the warmth in his voice onto the mechanic. "You have something inside you that both of us want. He represents a court that wants to decide what to do with it on your behalf, filing you away in its census books. I only want you to decide for yourself, giving you everything you'd need to understand the dignity of your own inheritance."

Corin's gaze went to Sariel, searching the hunter for some handhold or explanation that the Host's protocol made no provision for giving mortals. Vual gave him no room to find it.

"And you should know," the demon added, sliding the threat out with the same smoothness a moneylender in Dis uses to review a grain contract, "that a man doesn't walk away from an offer like mine without someone at court eventually asking who else might be worth the trouble in his place. Your friend, for instance. Dez, isn't it? People who spend their lives that close to something like you tend to attract the mud's attention sooner or later, whether they want to or not."

At Vual's wrist, a thin, fine seam of chain — Deseum's mark — flared for an instant before fading back beneath his sleeve, and Sariel felt the pull of longing throb through the alley's air like a physical cold.

"Don't listen to him, Corin," Sariel said, his voice flat, stripped of any ornament of hope. "I can't tell you what you are. I can't promise you an answer, and I can't promise nothing else will come looking for you in the dark either. But what I can tell you is that this is still yours to decide. Not his. Not mine. Whatever you choose, choose it because it's the one possession that truly belongs to you."

It was, Sariel knew in the depths of his chest, a miserable trade against the sackcloth certainty Vual had just laid on the cracked kerosene counter. Truth against a comfortable lie; permission to keep searching against an answer already drafted by another's hand. But it was the only thing he had that was honestly his to give.

Corin Vasse stayed silent between the two of them, the grease rag still crumpled in his callused hand, and a stillness settled over his face that struck Sariel as uncomfortably old, a stone rigidity that didn't belong to a cornered civilian's fear. Something in the mechanic's chest answered on its own, a subtle pulse of affection and sovereignty that seemed to come from a wound far older than his own human life.

"I don't know what I am," Corin said, and the words came out hard, steady, surprising even himself with the lack of tremor in his throat. "But I know what it feels like to have somebody decide something's owed to me before I've even agreed to the price. The answer is no."

He wasn't looking at the demon, and he wasn't looking at the hunter either; his gaze stayed fixed on some point on the wet ground, as though he were reading a handwriting only his soul remembered. It was an exhausted refusal, cold at its center, born of that brine-grief of a commander who refused to forgive the men who decided his lines from the safety of a war table.

Vual's smile didn't break, but it shifted by a fraction of a millimeter toward a fixedness that betrayed the end of his patience.

"That's a hard word to mean tonight, Corin," Vual said, testing the refusal for any give it might still have.

"It's the only one I've got," the mechanic answered, and didn't take a step back.

Vual measured him a moment longer in the alley's silence, weighing the cost of forcing a chain that Corin's rejection had already made unstable, and finally inclined his head with the lazy elegance of a man who would rather postpone the collection than ruin the material.

"Another evening, then," Vual said, throwing Sariel one last look of professional courtesy. "I told you once already, Corin: I'm not in any hurry. And the net is already cast."

He turned and walked off down the street, his silhouette dissolving into the outskirts' grey haze with the slowness of a man who knows the fish has no other pond to flee to.

Sariel remained alone with the mechanic, studying him under the pale light of a streetlamp that had begun to flicker. Corin Vasse was not a disguised demon, nor a threat that Zadkiel's manual ordered him to eliminate with Penumbra's iron. There was no written protocol for a soul carrying a stable dual signature without having decided what it was. The deviation Sariel had begun weeks before, in withholding his report from Camael's liaison, was completing itself now in this solitary decision, made under his own authority and no one else's. His unfailing record was no longer clean, but looking at the mortal, he felt that the rust of that lie was a cost his own discipline was willing to pay.

"This isn't forgiveness, Corin," Sariel said, sheathing the spear with a dry rasp of leather. "It's borrowed time. Use it well."

He turned and left the way he had come, offering no further explanation, leaving the mechanic alone with the night settling back over the alley.

Corin stayed outside the garage a long while, feeling the cold drizzle wash the smell of kerosene and salt from the counter off him. He thought of Dez, of how easily Vual's threat had slid into the conversation like an idle aside, and understood that some things didn't survive translation into a half-finished beer at Halloran's. He wouldn't tell him anything. He went home in silence, hands buried in his jacket pockets, carrying the evening's strangeness the way he had learned to carry every engine nobody else in town knew how to fix. He didn't know what he was, or what price the choice he had just made without his own body's permission might eventually cost him. But as he closed the door of his dark bedroom, he felt beneath the exhaustion a small, unfamiliar steadiness, a narrow, cold certainty telling him that, at least for tonight, time was still his to decide.
