Chapter 18. Those Waiting for a Stumble.

Belial spent the first day back in his private chambers doing nothing but watch the slow crawl of shadows across the ceiling, which, for a general who had spent three ages measuring the world by the stride of his legions, turned out to be its own kind of sentence. His steward had drawn the heavy curtains of scorched velvet against the amber light of Dis without being asked, and left him in a gloom that smelled of cold tallow, archive wax, and the mineral dust of his own trophies.

The ribs the Crystal Guardian had cracked in the clearing were healing slowly, in the sluggish, dense way demonic flesh rebuilds itself under the weight of wounded pride. Every deep breath brought a searing pull along his left side, a dry pain that tasted of spent lime and reminded him, with a metronome's regularity, how easily he had been thrown onto the frozen flagstones.

He had twice refused the numbing draughts the citadel's healer had left outside his door. Belial knew that in the court of Dis, a healer's visit was an official dispatch Foras's secretaries would have on record before the wound had even finished closing. On the walls, the gold standards of the Host he had taken at Zaphor'el and the throne carved from his old rival's bones now watched him with a fixedness that felt almost like mockery. He had built his legend inch by inch, guard-post by guard-post, convincing the court that there was no steel in Heaven capable of making him look away. He was not going to let the Weeping Sepulcher's first lesson become the excuse his enemies were waiting for to declare his seat vacant.

By the second day, stillness had grown more painful than the pull of his ribs themselves, and he sent for the oldest texts in the Tower's upper archive, preferring to give his hands clean work to lean on before his mind started devouring itself in the silence.

The junior archivist who brought him the bundles waited near the threshold, shoulders drawn up under his cheap grey wool robe, trembling at the gloom of the chamber and at the glacial fixedness with which the general received him. Belial dismissed him with a single flat wave of his hand, without lifting his eyes from the first volume already resting on his knees.

Most of what the man had rescued from the high shelves was useless — inventories of dead sulfur caravans, half-legible correspondence between diplomats who had turned to ash before the first campaign, marginalia scrawled by hands whose names official history hadn't bothered to preserve. But in the third bundle, a single line written in pale ink that smelled of saltpeter stopped his attention, forcing him to read it three times before he let his fingers turn the page.

The fragment was a commentary attributed to a mystic of the first age, a scholar whose name time had erased from the records:

"Planes shaped by a single concentrated grief do not yield to conquest," the text read in a trembling hand, "because conquest itself is a concept foreign to a place built entirely of loss. Such planes can only be crossed, on terms the place itself sets, by those willing to arrive as something other than a conqueror."

Belial weighed the word crossed in his mind with the same caution a forger in Dis weighs a bronze coin he suspects is lead underneath. The Weeping Sepulcher was not a border fortress that could be besieged, nor a garrison whose supply of souls could be cut off. The guardian had not fought him; it had corrected him, returning the violence of his mace as an echo that refuses to acknowledge the haste of whoever sends it. He closed the bundle slowly, feeling the dry crackle of dead pages beneath his thumb, understanding that the wound had taught him a direction, and that direction demanded he break his own pride if he meant to see the end of the path.

...

Nyx had earned the right to deliver the week's dispatches specifically because none of the citadel's more senior couriers had the nerve to walk into a room where a humiliated general was digesting his own convalescence. She knew a wounded commander tended to treat the messenger as the first expendable variable in his own failure. While she waited outside the iron door, the cold leather case between her hands, Nyx ran back through the lines of her own report, deciding with the precision that had cost her two fingers of her left hand which parts of the truth needed to lead and which were better buried three items down the list.

She entered with her spine straight, keeping her eyes fixed on the case rather than on the general, whose silhouette cut against the window barely revealed the tired angle at which he favored his left side.

"The eastern border's supply counts are closed, my lord," Nyx began, her flat, procedural voice carrying no hurry at all. "The eastern sulfur quarries report a minor drift of three bundles, nothing the Council's secretaries can't smooth over before closing."

Belial nodded once, a flat gesture she took as permission to continue.

"The Third Cohort has completed its guard rotation at the docks," Nyx went on, turning to the second sheet of the bundle with a dry rasp of parchment. "But the real matter that requires your attention, general, concerns Foras."

Foras's name dropped into the silence of the room with the weight of a cold stone. Belial did not move from the window, but the hiss of his black armor seemed to slow, forcing her to sharpen her tone before continuing.

"In the weeks your... silence has lasted, Foras has been quietly cultivating the minor captains of his own line," she said. "He has settled boundary disputes in the eastern quarries in ways that leave the satisfied party feeling personally indebted to him, and not to the Council. Two of your own junior officers were seen in his court hall on Tuesday, laughing at some minor joke Foras offered with a warmth that caught my scouts' attention."

Nyx added no opinions of her own. She knew opinions were the most dangerous currency a subordinate could spend in Dis. She had delivered the fact with the same dryness she would have used reading the inventory of a coal caravan, and waited for his leave with her eyes fixed on the wood of the desk.

Belial listened to the report without interrupting her, his face set in the same unreadable mask Nyx had seen him wear through a hundred war councils.

"Anything else?" Belial asked at last, and his voice, low and stripped of warmth, chilled the air of the chamber.

"Nothing that can't wait for next moon's report, general."

"Then you're dismissed, Nyx. And make sure the archive secretary has your accounting before the afternoon is out."

...

Alone again, Belial let the two pieces of the day settle side by side in his mind with the coldness of two swords crossed over a Pact Table: the mystic's warning about planes that could only be crossed, and Foras's quiet, patient usurpation at his back.

Neither piece, on its own, offered him a plan. Together, they told him something his general's pride ached in the bone to swallow: he had less time than his vanity wanted to admit, and the method of mace and fire that had won him a kingdom was not going to buy him the Weeping Sepulcher's threshold on a second attempt through sheer stubbornness. What frightened him was not the spear of Lamentum; it was the certainty that Dis had already begun forgetting his name while he sat still in the dark of his chambers.

He returned to the Weeping Sepulcher on the third day, his ribs still tender beneath his armor plates and the mace Dubium at his hip more from old soldier's habit than any real expectation the iron would serve him for anything this time. He told no one where he was going, not even Nyx, and left explicit instructions that any further dispatches from the court would have to wait until his return — a small, deliberate withholding of power from those already beginning to count his legions in his absence.

The portal received him with the same mineral silence, the same black crystal chiming faintly beneath his sabatons, the same thick, salt-laden air clinging to his lungs with the dry taste of old copper. The clearing where the Crystal Guardian had broken him was visible in the distance, a monument of frozen stone that refused to return the echo of his steps, and Belial made himself look at it directly, without turning his eyes away out of shame, before angling his course toward the deeper passage.

This time he did not quicken his pace at the first sign of a mystic pull. He walked slower than his conqueror's instincts demanded, testing the firmness of every flagstone before letting his full weight fall onto the crystal. It felt strange, that slow, measured movement, as though the very air of the Sepulcher had turned to thick mud pushing back against him with every stride. And yet he found, with a discomfort that soured his breath, that patience cost him less than he had budgeted for. It was not surrender; he told himself so, firmly, as the grey haze of brine fogged his visor. It was, at most, the first small, grudging, costly concession of a commander who had finally run out of easy options, and who now had to learn to walk the path on terms the stone itself dictated.
