Chapter 13. The Devil's Gambit.

The adjutant turned the pages of the ledger with fingers stained by a cheap ink that smelled of vinegar and cold soot, and Belial listened to the rasp of the coarse parchment with the plain patience of a quartermaster. In the small side chamber of the war citadel, the air stood stale, thick with the mineral dust of supply reports no one had bothered to clean off in a decade.

"Aamon's Third Cohort," the adjutant read, his voice flat, stripped of any inflection that might suggest he was counting lives, "has seven hundred active blades. The officers of the eastern border are loyal to him by rank, not by blood. The ones from the west... well, they owe him the sulfur quarries' tithe."

Belial did not take the ledger. His mace Dubium rested against his thigh, the black plates of his living armor contracting with a metallic hiss as steady as his own breathing. He knew what the books would say before the ink dried. Aamon was no minor figure; he was Commander of the Legions, a real pillar in Dis's accounting of attrition, and his loss would leave a gap the court would need three seasons to paper over.

"Note the vanguard captains," Belial said, his voice low and unhurried. "I want to know which of them would eat from Aamon's hand if the ration thinned, and which simply stay still because the whip is close."

The sergeant noted the directive without looking up, taking it for the ordinary minutiae of a general preparing a rotation of posts. Belial let him believe it. Holding the lie was a simple discipline that required, above all, no hurry to see the end of it.

He did not hesitate over that decision, and did not revisit it. He thanked the adjutant, dismissed him, and sat alone afterward turning the plan over in his mind, checking it the way a soldier checks a blade before a fight he has already decided to win.

Aamon's hall was a lesson in pride written in black stone. Polished obsidian columns rose toward a vault the braziers' restless light never quite managed to clean, and the air seeping through the arrow-slits carried a constant moan, the hiss of a current that seemed to have forgotten how to leave.

Aamon listened standing, arms crossed over his old bronze breastplate, his silhouette cut against the coals' orange glow. Belial kept to the exact distance protocol demanded, his head slightly bowed, a deference that cost him nothing and let his commander believe the conversation still belonged to him.

"I've located a convoy on the eastern border, my lord," Belial said, his voice carrying the tone of a confidence won by accident. "An unguarded corridor that Heaven's patrols barely watch once every three moons."

He didn't hand over the finished map. He spread the parchment across the obsidian table, but withheld the details of the narrow pass, letting Aamon lean over the paper and complete the approach with his own dark-clawed finger. Belial watched his commander's shoulder flex, the glint of greed that lit in his eyes as he believed he was correcting his subordinate's plan. He had served enough vain lords to know that the surest way to sell a lie is to let the mark insist on putting his own signature on it before he signs his own sentence.

"We attack at the three-moon crossing," Aamon declared, his voice martial and pleased with itself, claiming the plan before Belial had even finished withdrawing his hand.

"You'll ride with the vanguard," Aamon said, already deciding every detail of the coming battle down to its smallest formation. "A commander who brings me a gift this good earns the right to watch it opened."

Belial inclined his head as though the offer were a courtesy rather than the exact concession his whole visit had been built to extract. Being near the front, close enough to see the coordinates for himself the moment the legion arrived, was not a reward he'd asked for outright — it was the one he had spent the whole conversation quietly arranging for Aamon to offer on his own.

...

Andras watched the exchange from the shadow of the third pillar, one hand resting on his dagger's pommel, still as the bronze of the moldings. His trade was infiltration, the wound that opened in the corridor before the victim could decide whether to be afraid, and his professional eye caught, at once, an uncomfortable symmetry in Belial's presentation.

The rhythm had been too clean. Belial's answers to Aamon's tactical questions had arrived with that suspicious readiness of ink left to dry before the book is even opened. It was not competence; it was rehearsal. Andras recognized the tone the way a forger recognizes another forger's stroke on a signature meant to imitate his own: the calligraphy was exact, but the writer's pulse lacked the natural hesitation of a first attempt.

He weighed the suspicion in his mind with the speed of a man who knows exactly what a mistake costs in Dis's court. He could step forward, accuse Belial of deceiving his commander, and risk his own rank on a corridor dispute. But Aamon was proud, and a lord shown to have been played by his own captain tends to punish the messenger before admitting his own stupidity. The math was simple: speaking would cost him his head; staying silent cost him nothing but a commander who, if he fell, would open three captaincies Andras himself could inherit come the next season.

Andras took his hand off the dagger, settled back into the shadow, and let the trap run its course in silence.

...

Belial spent the following day threading the rumor through the border's broken channels, with a leisureliness his rivals would have mistaken for carelessness. He needed Heaven to answer for real, not merely to find a lost map. A lie confirmed by an enemy's reaction is a truth in a suspicious commander's mind.

He handed a fragment of the route to a courier whose loyalty he knew wavered under a gambling debt, and a second fragment to a double scout who sold his secrets to the highest bidder on the sulfur docks. Neither knew the other existed, and neither held the whole picture. Information handed over whole wakes the scrutiny of Heaven's scribes; information gathered in pieces, discovered in two separate saddlebags after a minor skirmish, reads as a victory of one's own intelligence work and is swallowed without question.

The report came at dusk: the eastern border's celestial patrols had shifted their course, closing the corridor exactly where Belial had left room for them to do it. Aamon received the news as final proof of his own tactical cunning. Belial received it with a cold satisfaction: the last piece of his scaffolding had settled into place, and the Weeping Sepulcher already waited for the flesh it needed to open its door.

The legion formed before dawn, and the advance began with the dull thud of Aamon's banners snapping against the north's frozen wind. The soldiers traded jokes in the ranks, sharing that coarse, loud confidence of troops who believe they're marching toward an easy plunder, a skirmish where Heaven's steel will pull back the moment the vanguard falls.

Belial rode at Aamon's left, his face arranged into the mask of a loyal subordinate anticipating glory beside his lord. At his shoulder, the sergeant of the ledger kept pace in silence, the vanguard's accounting already settled in his mind. No one in the ranks knew the coordinates' true nature; none knew the road beneath their feet passed within a crossbow's shot of the Weeping Sepulcher's dead plain.

Belial listened to the soldiers' distant laughter, feeling the weight of the mace Dubium against his thigh, and spared them not one glance of charity. He had charted every step, every delay, every leak, and the vanguard would march headlong into the mud of the front at exactly midday. The gate's cost would be paid on the plain, and Belial had long since decided that debt would be settled by Aamon, in his own blood.
