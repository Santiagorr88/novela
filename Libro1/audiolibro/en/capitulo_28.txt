Chapter 28. What the Choir Won't Sing.

Daith had expected another routine translation when Captain Iofiel's summons reached the lower wing of the archive — another fragment of the Codex Mortalis destined to be catalogued, sealed, and buried on the cedar shelves alongside the thousands of concordat records that no longer mattered to anyone living. The scriptorium of the Silent Choir smelled the way it always did at this hour of dusk — old, sickly parchment, the cold tallow of candles burned through too many vigils, and that fine, mineral dust that seemed to shake loose from the high masonry itself no matter how often the aides ran their wool dusters over it. Iofiel received them without ceremony, folded into her narrow wool chair with the staff Memnon braced against her shoulder, her face carved by the same gray exhaustion that had settled over the whole officer corps since the shadow-plane campaign. She handed them a single sheet of hymn-script, its edges scorched by some old fire that smelled of brine and spent lime, its central stanzas legible only in ragged patches. "We need it translated in full," the captain told them, and the careful, almost reverent way she set the page down on the lectern warned them of the danger before her own words did. "Whatever it says, Herald Gabriel needs the complete text, Daith. Not a clean summary. Not a convenient reading. This particular folio matters more than most we've kept in this age."

Daith had already begun assessing the fragment's condition before she finished speaking, the ordinary professional habit of a man who had spent centuries reducing chaos like this into something a court of law could use. His eyes moved automatically across the scorched margins, cataloguing the damage by degree, by category, by what could reasonably be salvaged and what could not. Kurel, beside him, made no such immediate move. He only looked at the fragment a long moment — the particular stillness that came over him before he touched anything he considered significant, a stillness the archive had learned, over the centuries, to trust even when it slowed the work.

"You already don't like it," Daith said, more observation than question.

"I don't know what it is yet," Kurel said. "Give me the afternoon."

They worked side by side once Iofiel left them to it, and within the hour Daith understood exactly how differently they meant to approach the same page. He transcribed letter by letter, mechanical and precise, building the translation outward from certainty toward the gaps. Kurel read the hymn aloud instead, low, several times over, before he let his fingers touch the parchment at all — as though the words needed to be heard before they could safely be handled.

"You're wasting time." There was no edge in it, only the flat certainty Daith brought to most things. "The transcription won't move any faster for being recited first."

"It isn't about speed." Kurel didn't look up. "A name that resists being spoken doesn't reveal itself to a man in a hurry."

It was not an unfamiliar arrangement between them. They had worked side by side on dozens of fragments over the years, Daith's precision and Kurel's patience settling, more often than not, into a rhythm that produced work neither of them could have managed alone. Daith saw no reason today should behave any differently.

The difference stopped being merely stylistic within the hour. Daith reached a clean stretch of legible text near the hymn's middle and moved, without pausing to consult Kurel, to seal the section — standard procedure for any passage that met the archive's threshold. Kurel's hand closed over the parchment before the seal touched it.

"Not yet." Quiet, but immovable. "There's a name in there. I want to hear it again first."

"It's legible, Kurel. That's the entire threshold. Show me, then, if there's something here that actually merits holding it — instead of asking me to take it on faith."

Kurel obliged, turning the fragment so the disputed section faced them both and tracing the line with one careful finger. To Daith's eye it looked no different from the dozen surrounding lines he had already sealed without incident, the same liturgical hand, the same familiar cadence of praise. He said as much.

"Read it aloud, then. Not scanned. Spoken."

Daith obliged more out of exasperation than curiosity, reading the line at his usual brisk pace, and heard nothing unusual. "There. Nothing." Kurel only waited, patient in a manner that read, to Daith, as its own quiet form of stubbornness. "Again. Slower."

Daith read it again, forcing his throat to slow its pace. And then it happened. On the final syllable of the line's last word, the air in his mouth turned suddenly dense as lead, and his voice caught in a dry, involuntary, physical stumble that tasted of chalk in his throat. He felt the word lodge itself behind his teeth, as though the very concept refused to be expelled from his body. He read the line a third time to be certain, knuckles pressed white against the wood, and the stumble repeated in the exact same place — except this time the sound of the syllables seemed to shift beneath his own tongue, mutating from one pronunciation to another in a cold murmur that froze the spit in his mouth. He let the pen fall, his heart hammering in his temples, staring at the inert page with the disorientation of a man discovering that his own senses had just switched sides in the dark.

Kurel said nothing. He had heard the same shift on his own first pass through the stanza, hours before, and had said nothing of it then either.

"The past does not forget," Daith said, recovering his composure with more edge than certainty. "Neither do I. We transcribe what's here, entire, or we don't transcribe it at all."

"What's here includes a word that corrupts itself every time someone says it aloud. Are you transcribing that too, or pretending you didn't just hear it happen?"

Daith had no immediate answer. "Each name is a key," Kurel added, quieter now. "I don't know what door we're opening. Neither do you."

"You think I don't understand what's at stake in getting this wrong."

"I never said that." Kurel let the correction stand a moment before finishing it. "You're afraid of an incomplete record. I'm afraid of a complete one we don't understand yet. Those aren't the same fear."

Daith held his silence a moment longer before answering, ground given without the argument itself being conceded. "Then tell me what we do with a hymn that doesn't want to be read."

Neither of them had an answer ready. The scriptorium had gone quiet around them, Iofiel's other assistants long since gone for the day, the candlelight thinning toward the far shelves until the smell of wax was nearly the only thing left in the room besides the two of them and the fragment between them. "We have time before Gabriel needs this," Kurel said. "Not much. Enough to be careful instead of merely fast." Daith looked at the scorched edges for a while. "Then we use it." It was not agreement. It was the closest thing to it Kurel had gotten from him all evening.

...

Daith returned the next morning no closer to an answer; the argument itself had not moved overnight either. The hymn fragment sat exactly where they had left it, the disputed stanza bearing a faint discoloration where both their hands had rested during the worst of it. Only the final section remained untranscribed, the one containing the word that refused to hold its shape, sitting between them like a wound neither had managed to close.

He reached for it mid-morning, telling himself it was simply the next reasonable step in an assignment already running behind, and began transcribing it letter by letter — without waiting for Kurel's approval, without announcing that he had decided to proceed. Reaching for the stanza without asking was its own argument. Asking would have meant risking another refusal.

Kurel's hand came down over the parchment before Daith's pen reached the disputed word, the strike of it loud enough to set the bronze bottles ringing. "Move your hand, Daith," Kurel said, a rage in his tone Daith had not heard from him in centuries of shared service. "Not until you tell me why you're in such a hurry to commit this betrayal." He didn't move it.

"I'm doing my job."

"You're doing an end run around a disagreement you didn't want to keep having."

Daith opened his mouth to object; he had no rebuttal ready. It had, in fact, been exactly that. "We didn't agree it needed more attention, Kurel. You decided it did. I've given you the benefit of the doubt since yesterday. At some point that runs out." He pressed on. "There's a precedent for this. The Perren testimony — a covenant left ambiguous in the northern archives because someone decided caution outweighed completeness, and that lack of precision cost the Host three whole cohorts the following season. I've cited it to you before. I'm citing it now: an incomplete record always ends up failing the one who depends on it."

Kurel considered that longer than he considered most things. "Perren was finished too late to matter, Daith. This one would be finished too soon to be true. I'd rather wait than speak a name before either of us understands what answering it will cost us. You think thoroughness protects you, but you're only chasing a comfortable lie that lets you close the file and sleep tonight."

The accusation landed closer to true than Daith wanted to concede aloud, and he said nothing for a while, the silence doing more work than any further argument might have. Kurel did not press the advantage. He only waited, his patience unsettling Daith more than direct confrontation could have.

Daith withdrew his hand eventually, more from exhaustion than concession, and spent the rest of the afternoon on the hymn's safer stretches instead, cross-checking his own earlier work, letting the final stanza sit unfinished between them. He told himself he had simply chosen the more efficient use of his time. The explanation didn't sit right, and he knew it, but he kept the doubt to himself.

Alone that evening, walking the corridor back toward his own quarters, he let himself consider, briefly and privately, whether Kurel might actually be right. It was an uncomfortable thought precisely because dismissing it would have been so much easier than examining it. He had built his career on the principle that thoroughness protected people, that an incomplete record eventually failed someone who needed it whole. He tried, walking, to summon a single case in the other direction — a record finished too soon that had done comparable damage — and could not, and was not certain whether that was because no such case existed, or because he had simply never gone looking for one.

He did not reach a conclusion before he reached his own door. He only knew, letting himself inside, that the certainty he had walked into the scriptorium with had not survived the argument intact, whatever he had said to Kurel's face about it. Somewhere down the corridor behind him, faint enough that he could almost have imagined it, he thought he heard Kurel's voice, low and alone, reading the stanza to himself again — checking something, still, that would not stay checked.

Neither of them raised the subject again before the day ended. Kurel would not yield the seal; Daith would not concede the principle; the final stanza sat exactly where it had that morning, unfinished, its corrupted word still refusing to hold a single shape between readings. Something would eventually have to give — Gabriel's patience, if nothing else, or the deadline Iofiel had made clear was not indefinite. For now, the two of them worked around the wound in the hymn rather than through it, each waiting, Daith suspected, for the other to break first.
