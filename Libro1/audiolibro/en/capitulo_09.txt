Chapter 9. The Price of Knowledge.

The aide caught up to Belial on the third landing of the fifth level, and he did it with the sour gasp of a man who had dragged his own body up seven hundred iron steps with nothing to help him but urgency. His gray serge robes, filthy with soot and lamp grease, clung to his ribs with a thick sweat.

"Commander..." he managed, leaning against the rusted railing, which shuddered under his weight. "While you were... occupied here, Malgorath has been calling in favors across three separate courts. Building support. Promising tariffs on the eastern sulfur quarries."

Belial did not stop. His ascent continued at the same flat, economical pace he might have used to measure the distance of a guard trench, the living plates of his black armor contracting with a steady metallic hiss.

"Let him," Belial said, without granting the aide the charity of a glance. "Favors are flour coins, sergeant. They weigh nothing until someone tries to spend them, and by the time Malgorath tries to collect the first of his debts, I'll already be back with something that will make his little accounting of Dis look like a child's game in the mud."

The aide fell behind in the gloom of the landing, caught between the fear of being left alone in a tower that whispered and the dread of following a general who walked as though he had already decided half his own court was expendable. Belial kept climbing, letting the warning dissolve into the mineral dust and cold-ash smell of the closed walls.

The labyrinth of the ninth landing rearranged itself before him with the lazy creak of a damp bronze lock. Here the books were not bound in ordinary leather or in the parchment of Dis. Their pages, when Belial ran a gauntleted fingertip over them, had a disturbingly living texture: thin as the skin of a belly, warm to the touch, and so unwilling to lie flat on the lectern that they shrank faintly beneath his shadow, as though the paper still held the memory of the cold that had once parted it from flesh.

The second ward barred the passage three corridors further in. It had no locks; it was a circle of static light that throbbed with a slow pulse, a dull rhythm Belial felt in his own teeth before he heard it in the air.

He turned to the second minor demon following him. It was a gray, nameless creature, its shoulders bowed with the stupid docility of the lower ranks that only wait for the day to end without anyone noticing them. There was no struggle, none of the needless haste of cruelty. In the logic of Dis, the servant understood that its body was a tool, and that becoming the key to a forbidden ward was the closest it would ever come to its existence carrying any measure of worth.

Belial shoved it forward with a single flat, utilitarian motion. The ward took it instantly.

It was not hot fire that consumed it, but a pale, glacial, mineral light that climbed its joints, turning its flesh to translucent stone in a single heartbeat. Its shape froze mid-step, its mouth opening on a scream that never managed to produce a single physical sound in the stillness of the tower. Belial walked past the gray crystal statue without looking at it, his attention already fixed on the shelves opening beyond the threshold.

Then a voice tore through the corridor at his back. It came like a gust of icy wind that soured his throat with the taste of cold ash and rusted copper.

"Turn back," the voice said, in a language so old that the Dubium mace hummed at his hip in answer. "This is not a door built for the living."

Belial did not turn. He had heard the tower's ghosts on earlier campaigns and knew that the desperation of the dead rarely carried any real weight. He climbed the last stair, letting the warning thin out behind him until it became a murmur without consequence.

The volume he was looking for stood in the darkest corner of the landing, a tome whose iron spine groaned with a dry creak as he pulled it free. It was so damaged that half its pages disintegrated into gray dust that floated in his lamp's beam before settling on his boots.

The text spoke of the Primordials.

A name tried to take shape in his mind as he read, a vibration of fire and ancient law that struck against a seal of forgetting so dense that Belial felt a sharp pain behind his temples, a warning that pressing further would cost him a piece of his own memory. He pulled his attention back in time, keeping his caution, and turned to the next page.

There he read the description of Solmire. His golden eyes fixed on the exact words: *a blade forged from pure joy to deliver verdicts without sorrow.* He felt a familiar stiffness in his chest, recognizing in that phrase the survivor's report from Zaphor'el. But it was the next passage that made his breath stop cold:

*...and its counterpart, Lamentum, a spear forged in grief to deliver the truths that create it.*

The symmetry closed over him like an iron lock finally finding its key. Solmire was not some mystical anomaly of Heaven; it was only half of a broken song. And the other half, the spear born of pure grief, lay hidden in a place where the sorrow of creation had turned solid, beyond judgment's reach: the Weeping Sepulcher.

Belial studied the faded star-chart illustrating the margin, feeling pride and ambition silence whatever doubt Lucifer's warning had left in him back in the library. He had his target. He rolled the star-chart closed, careful not to break its brittle edge, and tucked it into his robes, a slow, satisfied smile curling at the corners of his mouth.

The busker had been playing in the square for most of the evening, and Yara, carefully wrapping the last flowers of the day before the cold finished wilting the petals, had stopped more than once to listen without quite meaning to.

He was an odd one. He went barefoot over the evening's chilled cobblestones, his guitar seeming to hold together on nothing but a couple of strips of gray tape and a patience that didn't belong to the season. His hair was too sun-bleached for the time of year, and when Yara looked closely, she found his eyes an odd mismatched pair — one the pale shade of old silver, the other a warm amber — that caught the lamplight strangely. His songs had no clear structure; they were verses that trailed off into lazy humming before finding their way back to a melody no one in the square could recall ever having heard before.

And yet, somehow, all of it worked.

The streetlamp above the fountain, which had buzzed with an irritating flicker for three nights running — a fault Yara herself had reported three times to the district office without any technician ever managing to fix it — steadied all at once into a warm, even glow partway through his second song. Yara noticed the coincidence and set it aside with the practical skepticism of someone who still had to count her coins for the walk home.

The busker told a joke between songs, something absurd about a fish that forgot how to swim and taught itself to fly instead, and he laughed with such genuine delight at his own clumsiness that Yara smiled despite herself. His laughter spread across the square. Near the fountain, a couple who had spent ten minutes arguing loudly over a missed date or money owed fell suddenly silent, caught by the busker's laugh, and ended up laughing along too, the urgency of the quarrel forgotten.

A stray dog with visible ribs, the same one that had spent the day growling at passersby from the mouth of the alley, wandered up to the busker during the third song, sniffed at his bare foot, and settled down beside him with a satisfied sigh, tail thumping once against the damp stone.

Yara finished closing her stall, tucking the flowers into the wooden crate. She was about to leave when, mid-chord, the busker stopped. His smile did not fade so much as simply pause, and his head tilted slightly toward the darkening sky, as though he had caught some sound beneath the square's ordinary breathing — a flat, tuneless chord with no echo that Yara could not hear.

The busker shook his head, a small, private gesture, and picked the melody back up exactly where he had left it, playing on unhurried as Yara turned the corner, letting the music follow her a few steps further before the city's dull noise swallowed it whole.
