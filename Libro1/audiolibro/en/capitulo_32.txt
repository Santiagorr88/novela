Chapter 32. What Neither of Them Keeps.

Cassiel found it at the center of the island's spiraling paths, in a clearing where bare stone broke through the growth — an echo carved into both rock and sound at once, unmistakably tied to Solmire, older than anything from his three prior visits. He knelt beside it a long while, letting the staff draw out what detail it could. No name surfaced. No face. What came through instead was a plain, negative certainty: whatever account Heaven held of the sword's origin, this resonance did not agree with it. One detail stood out once he isolated it — this echo predated Miguel's own claim to the blade, old enough that the sword had existed, and mattered to someone, long before Miguel ever touched it.

He sat with that certainty a long moment. This was his fourth crossing to Turein, and he still could not account for what had drawn him back after the first. Whatever he had expected to find here, a nameless contradiction was not it — and he made himself sit with the mismatch rather than smooth it into a tidier conclusion than the evidence supported.

He considered, kneeling there, how he would carry this back to anyone who needed to hear it. Iofiel would want specifics he didn't have — a name, a face, something solid enough to build an argument around. He had none of that, only a resonance that contradicted the official account. He had spent his whole service learning to trust a quiet certainty like this one over anything louder or easier to prove, and he wasn't going to abandon that now just because the finding amounted to so little.

He rose, memorizing the clearing's position the way he had on his first visit — the island's geography had cost him his way back once already. The resonance held steady under his renewed attention, unaffected by his leaving, asking nothing further of him.

He felt the presence behind him before he heard anything, and turned to find a demon standing at the clearing's edge, bow already in hand though not yet drawn. Cassiel rose slowly, the staff shifting into a ready grip without quite becoming a weapon raised in earnest. Neither spoke for a long moment. The demon's stance read more measured than aggressive — a hunter's readiness, not an immediate wish to strike, though Cassiel wasn't ready to stake anything on that yet.

He held his ground rather than retreat toward the resonance behind him — no reason to show the demon how much he might have to protect if this turned violent. Whatever the stranger already knew about the clearing, the sword, or anything Cassiel had spent four visits piecing together, giving away more than necessary was the wrong instinct to follow.

He studied the demon's bearing for anything worth reading — the way he held the bow, neither raised nor lowered, a stillness that suggested long practice rather than nerves. This didn't look like a soldier caught unprepared. No matter what had brought him here, he seemed to have arrived with a plan already formed for exactly this.

The silence stretched longer than Cassiel found comfortable, but he made no move to break it. Centuries of coaxing frightened souls back from panic had taught him that the first to speak into a tense silence usually surrendered more than the silence was worth. He held his ground and waited, the staff steady in his grip, trusting the demon's own reluctance to close the distance.

Selm studied the scout across the clearing, weighing the fight with the same flat calculation he gave any target he hadn't yet committed to taking. The angle was clean enough. The scout looked capable, but two days of watching had shown him a man not especially hungry for violence. A fight here would burn time neither of them had, and might scatter whatever this place held before either side understood it. A kill that cost the objective wasn't worth taking.

He ran the arithmetic the way he always did before violence: cost against benefit, certainty against risk, weighed against the plain satisfaction of an easy kill. Two days of watching had already taught him more about this quarry than a fight would reveal in its opening seconds — the man's caution, the care he brought to unfamiliar ground. Killing him now closed off a source that might still lead somewhere worth knowing. Selm didn't spend a source before it finished paying out.

There was also the matter of what waited behind the scout, in the clearing the man had finally reached after two days of searching. Selm had followed this trail because Vepar's apparatus wanted to understand what it was, not simply to remove whoever else was looking for it. A body answered none of the questions his orders required. Given the choice, he preferred to go home with something the mission could use over a kill that told Hell nothing beyond the fact that a rival scout had once existed.

He had already gathered one detail worth carrying home regardless of how the rest of this resolved: the scout's guarded stillness, holding his ground rather than retreating toward what lay behind him. A man didn't defend empty ground that way. Whatever sat in that clearing, the scout's posture said it mattered enough to risk a fight over — and Selm intended to report exactly that, plainly, without embellishment he hadn't earned.

He lowered the bow, not fully, only enough to signal the intention without committing to it as a promise. "I haven't come here to fight," he said, his voice carrying none of the urgency the moment might have demanded.

"Good," Cassiel said, the staff still held ready but no closer to becoming a weapon than the demon's own bow. "Because I don't have time to waste on this either — my own side already has enough weighing on it." Selm was silent briefly before answering in kind. "Mine as well." Neither offered anything further. The quiet that followed felt, to Cassiel's ear, less like hostility than a shared, unspoken understanding: whatever waited beyond this clearing mattered more than any satisfaction either of them might take from a fight neither could afford.

Cassiel studied the demon a moment longer, searching for any sign the restraint was tactical rather than genuine, and found nothing to point to either way. He decided it didn't especially matter. The outcome amounted to the mercy Cassiel needed — and, judging by the demon's continued stillness, might have served him too. For once, he was willing to accept an answer without needing to understand its cause first.

He allowed himself a small measure of professional curiosity about what had brought Hell's own hunters to this same remote clearing. Ground this far outside any charted route required more than luck to find — the demon's presence here seemed unlikely to be coincidence. Whatever had drawn Cassiel's own instincts here weeks ago, something comparable seemed to have reached the other side. That possibility alone was worth remembering.

He wondered, too, whether the demon carried a better answer than his own fragmentary reading — some fuller understanding of this place, bought through methods Heaven's scouts would never touch. He didn't ask. No matter what advantage either side held today, neither seemed inclined to trade it away for idle curiosity, and Cassiel wasn't about to be the one who broke that first.

Neither moved first for a long moment — the standoff settling into mutual assessment rather than active tension — until Selm finally stepped back toward the treeline, bow still lowered but not put away. "We both walk away with something," he said, leaving room for it to be contradicted. "We both walk away with something," Cassiel agreed, offering nothing further about what he'd found. Selm's expression gave away nothing in return, and Cassiel understood, as the demon retreated into the spiraling growth, that he would likely never know precisely what the demon had taken from this place — any more than the demon could know the full shape of what Cassiel now carried back with him.

Neither side had won cleanly. Cassiel left the clearing with the resonance's confirmation intact in memory, nothing physical to show for it, and no illusion that Hell's hunter had left empty-handed either. It wasn't the outcome he'd have hoped for on his first crossing to this island. Weighed against every alternative the confrontation might have produced, it was one he could accept without regret.

He made his way back across Turein's spiraling terrain at the same deliberate pace that had carried him through every stretch of it so far — the day's danger had passed, but the discipline that got him this far was not something to abandon now. The light overhead moved through its familiar strange colors as he walked, a phenomenon he no longer questioned, and the paths offered no further resistance on the way out. Whatever test the island had set him across four visits, he suspected he had finally passed it — or at least earned enough of its patience to be allowed to leave.

He thought, walking, of the demon's parting words — the shared acknowledgment that neither side had claimed the whole of what this place held. It was an uncomfortable kind of victory to carry home, incomplete in ways his service rarely tolerated. He carried it anyway, across open water and onto solid ground, already weighing how he'd explain an unfinished finding to a Council that preferred its evidence far more settled than this.

He brought his findings to Iofiel two days later, once the journey back and his other duties had given him a proper window to request her attention. He chose his words with the same restraint he'd held throughout the assignment, offering only what he could confirm. "Solmire's origin isn't what the Council currently believes," he told her, watching her expression shift from mild professional interest into something more guarded. "I don't have a name. I don't have a face. What I found predates Miguel's own claim to the blade — and it doesn't agree with the official account. That's all I can honestly confirm."

Iofiel said nothing for a while, turning the report over with the same care Cassiel recognized from her own work on the Codex Mortalis. "That's not nothing," she said finally. "It isn't evidence I can bring to Gabriel yet, either." Cassiel offered no further certainty than what he had — not even to someone he trusted with the rest of it.

"There's something else," he added, choosing the detail carefully. "I wasn't the only one interested in that particular stretch of water. Hell had someone searching that ground as well." Iofiel's expression sharpened at that — a different kind of attention than she'd given the resonance itself. "Did it come to a fight?" she asked. "No," Cassiel said. "We both left with something. Neither of us with everything." He didn't elaborate, and Iofiel, after a moment's consideration, didn't press him for more.

Whatever the resonance on Turein meant, he understood, as Iofiel set the report among her own unfinished work on the Codex, it would take more than one scout's testimony before the Council knew what to make of it. He had done what his service asked of him — found what could be found, reported what could honestly be confirmed, and left the rest for a wiser judgment to settle. It was, he thought, walking away, very nearly the whole of what his years under Orifiel's command had ever asked of him — not certainty, only the patient custody of whatever small true thing he could carry back intact.
