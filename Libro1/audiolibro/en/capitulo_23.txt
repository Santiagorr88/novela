Chapter 23. A Gift With No Name.

Corin Vasse had spent six years fixing other people's cars in the same cramped garage on the edge of town, and most days he counted himself lucky for it — steady work, an honest trade, a boss who left him alone as long as the bay stayed clean and the customers stayed happy. He had a fondness for older engines, the kind that still rewarded patience and a careful ear, and had spent that afternoon coaxing a decade-old sedan back to life for a woman who'd nearly given up on it — her relief worth more to him, most days, than the invoice.

Tonight wasn't a work night, which was why he'd let his friend Dez talk him into the corner booth at Halloran's instead of heading straight home. Dez worked the counter at the hardware store two doors down, and the two of them had fallen into an easy, undemanding friendship built mostly out of proximity and a shared tolerance for the same handful of bars in this part of town. He nursed a beer he'd been nursing for the better part of an hour, half-listening to the bar's usual Tuesday murmur. He liked this place because nothing ever happened in it worth remembering the next morning — the same regulars on the same stools, the same highlights looping low above the counter, the same bartender who knew his order without asking, the same faded neon sign that had needed replacing for years and probably never would.

Dez was midway through a long story about a coworker's disastrous attempt at fixing his own plumbing — the kind of story Corin had heard a dozen versions of from a dozen friends — and Corin let it wash over him without much real attention. It was, by every measure that mattered to him, an ordinary night, one of a thousand he expected to live before anything in his life actually changed.

The argument two tables over started small enough that Corin barely registered it — two men trading the kind of sharp words that usually burn out before anyone gets involved. He caught only fragments over Dez's story — something about money owed, or a favor not returned, the specifics less important than the rising edge in both men's voices. Neither looked like the type Corin would have pegged for trouble, which made it more unsettling to watch, not less. A few regulars nearby had already gone quiet, watching instead of talking, the hush of a room sensing something about to tip past the point where it can be pulled back.

It did not burn out. The volume climbed, a third voice joined in on one side, then a fourth on the other. Dez had stopped his own story; both of them now watched the same slow unraveling, conversations at nearby tables trailing off as more attention turned toward the corner. Chairs scraped back. Someone's hand closed hard enough around a bottle that the gesture alone changed the room. The bartender was already moving that way, calling out something about taking it outside that neither man seemed to hear. Corin felt his own pulse pick up — the ordinary instinct to brace for something about to go wrong in a space too small to contain it.

He wasn't sure, afterward, what he did. He remembered standing, a wordless certainty rising in him that this needed to stop — a pull he hadn't chosen any more than his own heartbeat — and remembered nothing about how it actually stopped. In the instant before it happened, he felt a strange heat move through his chest and out along both arms, gone again almost before he could register it. Dez said something as he rose, a half-formed warning about staying out of it, but Corin was already moving before the sentence finished.

The room changed. Whatever had been building toward a fight softened all at once — the raised voices dropping without either man seeming to notice, hands unclenching from bottles and chair backs with no memory, Corin guessed, of having clenched them at all. One of the two men reached out and gripped the other's shoulder, the gesture landing less like a challenge than an apology neither had words ready for yet. The anger hadn't vanished. Some thread of ordinary fondness that must have been sitting quietly underneath it all along — regulars who drank at the same bar most weeks, who probably knew each other's names and families — rose up and crowded it toward the edges of the room. The two men who had nearly come to blows started talking instead, low and almost friendly, like they'd just remembered how long they'd known each other. Corin stood there in the middle of it, breathing hard, with no idea what he had just done or how.

The whole bar seemed to exhale at once, conversations picking back up looser and easier than before. A woman near the bar caught his eye, an odd, searching look on her face, and crossed the room toward him with a directness that made Corin want to step back before she'd even opened her mouth.

"Thank you," she said, and the intensity in it landed strangely on him, heavier than anything he felt he'd earned. Her eyes were still faintly damp, and she seemed to be searching his face for some explanation he had no way of giving her. He hadn't thrown a punch, hadn't said anything he could remember beyond some half-formed word at the room, hadn't done anything at all as far as he could tell, beyond standing up and wanting the fight to stop. He tried for a moment to remember anything more specific — a gesture, a word, some concrete cause — and found nothing there but the wanting itself. "I didn't do anything," he told her, and meant it, though the words felt thin leaving his mouth. She looked at him a moment longer, then nodded and returned to her seat, leaving Corin standing there with a room full of strangers who all seemed to be looking at him differently than they had ten minutes earlier. Even the bartender, who'd been halfway to the corner ready to separate the two men, gave him a small, grateful nod on the way back behind the counter — the kind of acknowledgment usually reserved for regulars, not a man who mostly kept to his own corner booth.

He sat back down across from Dez, hands not quite steady around his glass, and said nothing for a while, running the last few minutes back through his head and finding no version that made ordinary sense. Dez had gone unusually quiet too, watching Corin with an expression still working its way toward some conclusion.

He had felt something leave him in that moment, the way a man feels a held breath finally go, though he couldn't have named what it was or where it came from. A quiet unease settled into him that had nothing to do with the argument and everything to do with the plain fact that some part of him, some capability he'd never suspected he carried, had just acted on its own. He'd spent his whole life being an unremarkable man — decent at his trade, forgettable in most rooms, never the one anybody depended on for anything more dramatic than a working transmission. Nothing in thirty-odd years had prepared him for being that person now, even for one strange minute, in a bar he'd chosen specifically because nothing here ever asked anything of him. He turned his hands over once, studying them, and found them as unremarkable as always — the same calluses from six years of engine work, the same small scar on one knuckle from a wrench that had slipped years ago, nothing in the ordinary shape of them that could account for whatever had just moved through that room.

"What did you say to them?" Dez asked, leaning in with the curiosity of a man who'd watched something happen and couldn't make the pieces fit. "Looked like you lifted something off all of them at once." He glanced back toward the corner, where the two men were still talking, easier now than either had probably been all night, then back to Corin with the same unresolved expression.

Corin shrugged, buying himself a second by taking another pull from his glass. "I didn't say anything strange. I just... talked." The words felt thin even as they left his mouth, and judging by how long Dez's eyebrows stayed raised, Dez wasn't buying it either. Neither pushed it further, settling into the kind of silence old friends could sit inside comfortably. Dez eventually picked his plumbing story back up, with noticeably less enthusiasm, and Corin let himself pretend to listen while the real weight of the evening sat elsewhere. He finished his beer in something closer to silence than conversation, the unease still sitting unresolved behind his ribs, and found, walking out into the cold a while later, that he still had no better answer than the one he'd given Dez.

He walked the three blocks home slower than usual, hands buried in his jacket pockets against a cold he barely registered, replaying the strange minute over and over without it landing any differently the second or third time. Underneath the confusion sat a smaller, quieter fear he didn't have words for either — not fear of what he'd done, but fear of what else, given the right room and the right moment, that same unnamed part of him might decide to do next.
