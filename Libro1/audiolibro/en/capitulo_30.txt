Chapter 30. The Island That Appears on No Map.

Cassiel found the wounded soul exactly where Silens told him it would be, curled at the base of a collapsed watchtower on a battlefield three days cold, its light guttering low but not yet gone. He knelt beside it the way the Order had taught him to kneel beside any soul Silens led him to — habit worn smooth by centuries of grief. Nothing about his own bearing hurried the process — an unremarkable build, a stillness he wore the way other scouts wore armor, an attention that reached the wound before the soul had found any way to name it. This one had been easy enough to find and far harder to steady, a soldier's soul so thoroughly undone by its own death that it took the better part of an hour of quiet, wordless attention before it settled enough to be guided onward. He did not begrudge the hour. It was simply what the work asked.

He rose from the watchtower's ruins once the soul had finally settled, brushing ash and old dust from his knees, and gave the quiet field around him a moment's witness before moving on. The staff hummed quietly against his palm as he walked, its steady pulse cataloguing the plane's remaining traces without urgency — finding what needed finding, at whatever pace the finding required.

He felt the anomaly on his way back toward the watch post, faint enough that a less attentive scout might have mistaken it for residue from the soul he had just guided onward. The staff pulsed differently for this, in a register Cassiel could not place against anything he had catalogued before — not grief, not fear, not the raw disorientation of a fresh death, but something closer to laughter caught and pressed flat in the fabric of the place, old and strange and unfamiliar. He walked a slow circle around the place where the reading had first reached him, testing whether it strengthened or faded with distance, and it did neither — only persisted, faint and unchanging, no matter which way he moved. A wounded soul had a center a scout could kneel beside. This reading seemed to belong to no single point at all, as though it were less a thing resting on the ground than an echo diffused through the ground itself.

He found Ophaniel filing her own routine report an hour later — she glanced up before he'd said a word, the reflex of anyone who'd worked alongside him long enough to know he rarely sought someone out empty-handed — and mentioned the anomaly in passing rather than as anything requiring escalation. "Anything I should send reinforcements for?" she asked, glancing up with mild, procedural curiosity. "Not yet... It might be nothing. I'd rather not waste anyone's time chasing a hunch," Cassiel said. She accepted the answer without further comment and returned to her ledger. Walking away, he considered whether he had been entirely honest in that answer, and could not quite decide. It was, at minimum, the strangest reading the staff had ever handed him, and he had decided he would rather carry the uncertainty alone a while longer than hand someone else a burden he couldn't yet describe clearly enough to justify.

He gathered what a solo scouting trip might reasonably require — provisions for several days, nothing that would draw notice — and set out the way he had set out a hundred times before. Only the reading itself, faint and unlocatable, made the walk feel different beneath his feet.

The trail took most of a day to follow, strengthening only marginally as he moved, offering nothing so simple as a straight line. By the time the coastline came into view, unlisted on any chart he carried, the crossing had already told him this was no ordinary assignment: the spray off the strait ran cold and sharp with salt in a way that clung to his robes long after he'd left the water behind. The island's own coastline shifted subtly enough between his approach and his landing that he began to doubt the maps were entirely at fault for missing it.

Turein's interior unsettled him the moment he crossed the treeline — vegetation growing in slow, deliberate spirals rather than any pattern he recognized, the wind folding back on its own passage in ways that left the light moving strangely across the canopy overhead. He felt, standing in the heart of it, some faint and unaccountable conviction that this place held something connected to Solmire — not a certainty he could trace to any single sensory detail, only a low recognition he could not yet name. He had felt nothing like it in all his years of scouting. He chose to trust it anyway.

He moved deeper with the same careful attention he brought to any unfamiliar ground, cataloguing details out of long habit: the spiraling growth repeating at every scale he examined, from the smallest curling fern to the largest tree's own twisting trunk. The light overhead moved oddly too, shifting angle and color in ways that did not track against the sun he occasionally glimpsed through gaps in the canopy.

The wrongness declared itself properly an hour later, at a fallen log crossing his path a second time, then a third, each encounter identical down to the angle of moss along its upper surface. Cassiel stopped and studied it a long moment, testing whether his own memory might simply be mistaken about how many times he had passed this point. It was not mistaken. He had walked, by his own careful count, in a complete circle, arriving back nearly where he had started despite having moved, as far as he could tell, in one consistent direction the whole time. He crouched and searched the log for some detail that might distinguish this encounter from the ones before it. The moss lay exactly as he remembered it from twenty minutes earlier, undisturbed by his own passage — as though his own footsteps had somehow failed to register at all.

He tried the path again, more from stubbornness than real expectation of a different outcome, and was back at the same log within a quarter hour — a fourth identical encounter, against a single unbroken line of travel. His pulse had climbed higher than he cared to admit; he steadied his breathing deliberately rather than let the fear compound whatever the island's geometry was already doing to his sense of direction. Whatever governed this ground plainly did not answer to simple persistence. He would need something other than the approach that had carried him through every other assignment of his long service, and he made himself admit that plainly rather than try the same failed method a fifth time out of pride.

He steadied himself instead by turning his attention to the staff rather than the shifting ground, and rose to try the path a final time — deliberately slower now, abandoning any effort to hold a straight bearing in favor of simply attending to what the island itself seemed to be doing around him. The spiraling growth, he noticed on this slower pass, followed a rhythm he could almost anticipate once he stopped trying to walk through it and let his own pace bend instead — not resistance exactly, but patient accommodation, the same quality of attention he brought to a soul too disoriented by its own death to be guided anywhere by force. Small variations he had missed entirely on his first rushed attempt now showed at the edges of his notice — a break in the undergrowth here, a shift in the light there — each one a small, quiet invitation rather than an obstacle. He followed them without hurrying, and for the first time since crossing the treeline, the ground seemed to be leading somewhere instead of circling back on itself.

He tested the idea once, deliberately, quickening his stride for no more than a dozen steps to see whether the ground would punish the impatience the way it had before. It did. The path ahead blurred, some quality he could not quite name suggesting he was about to lose his bearings again, and he slowed at once; the wrongness receded the moment he did. It occurred to him, walking the rest of the afternoon at exactly the pace the island seemed to ask of him, that his own temperament might be the reason it had not turned him away outright — the same unhurried attention his fellow Captains found faintly tedious in him, rewarded here with the same indifferent consistency a strict teacher might apply to a lesson everyone else had already learned.

He found it near the island's center, in a small clearing where the spiraling growth opened onto bare stone: a resonance unmistakably tied to Solmire, faint and old, carrying none of the sword's usual clean authority — closer to memory than to presence, as though the blade itself, or some echo of it, had passed through this exact place long before Cassiel had ever heard its name. He held the reading a long while, letting the staff confirm what his own instinct had already suspected since he first set foot on the island's shifting shore. He had been wrong about smaller things than this before. He preferred, where he could, to be wrong slowly.

Whatever this island was, whatever thread connected it to the sword now hanging at Miguel's side, Cassiel understood, kneeling over the resonance, that this was worth the trouble of returning for — and worth holding carefully, the way he held any soul not yet ready to be moved. He knelt there a while longer, memorizing every detail he could of the clearing's position relative to the coastline he had crossed that morning, unwilling to trust a return trip entirely to the staff's guidance a second time after everything the island's geography had already cost him once. For now he had exactly what he had come for: confirmation that Turein held something real, and a quiet, settled resolve — not excitement, nothing so unguarded as that — to keep following it, carefully, for however long it took to understand what it was.
