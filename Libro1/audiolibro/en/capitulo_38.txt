Chapter 38. A Truce Neither Will Admit To.

Thoria heard the commotion before she saw its source — a young demon soldier stumbling toward her patrol's position with her hands raised and empty, calling something urgent about a soldier of her own dying from no wound anyone could see. Thoria's own patrol raised weapons on reflex, and Thoria held up a hand to still them. The demon's panic looked genuine, and genuine panic, in Thoria's judgment, outweighed whatever hostility still lingered from the earlier standoff.

She followed the demon back at a measured pace, blade drawn and ready, and found what she expected — a demon lying rigid on the unstable ground, his presence fading in some way no ordinary wound could explain. Thoria recognized the shape of it — a soul-parasite bond settling its grip somewhere beyond the reach of blade or fist — the exact threat it had been forged to answer. She had cut connections like this more times than she could count, though never for a demon, and never at another demon's request, standing over him with something that looked, even now, like genuine fear.

She knelt closer, and the same faint, metallic taste the fissure's air had left on her tongue since the crossing sharpened here, close to Orwick, into something almost sweet — the way blood tastes, she thought, though nothing about him was bleeding. She studied the rigid soldier with the same unblinking attention she gave any working, cataloguing his stillness against every soul-parasite bond she had ever severed before. The pattern held familiar beneath the strangeness of the setting — a grip she couldn't see directly but could read through the blade's own resonance, sharpening the closer she drew. Whatever governed this fissure, it had at least produced a threat her training recognized.

The demon who had led her here hovered nearby, close enough that Thoria could feel the tension radiating off her without needing to look up from her work. She understood, distantly, that this soldier had crossed whatever line separated the two patrols to ask an enemy for help — a decision that likely cost her more than urgency alone accounted for. Thoria set the observation aside and returned her attention to the work.

She held her position a moment longer than the scene strictly allowed, some old resistance in her own nature recoiling from the idea of extending the blade's purpose toward a demon at all. She had built her entire service on a firm boundary between what she protected and what she opposed, had never had reason before now to question which side of that boundary any particular soul belonged on. Offering the one tool built to sever exactly this kind of threat to the side she had spent centuries standing against felt like a violation of something she could not immediately name.

She set the resistance aside deliberately, weighing it against the plainer fact in front of her — a soul in genuine danger, regardless of which side it had been born to, and a blade in her own hand built for precisely nothing else. Incorruptible had never meant rigid for its own sake. It meant holding to a purpose even when the purpose demanded something uncomfortable of her. She crossed the remaining distance and knelt beside the stricken demon.

She thought, kneeling there, of every doctrine she had ever been taught about the boundary separating Heaven's soldiers from Hell's, and how little of it had prepared her for this — a demon soldier dying of a threat neither side had caused, in a place neither side had chosen, with only her own gift standing between him and whatever this fissure intended. She had built her reputation on precisely the kind of unwavering conviction that made this decision feel, in the moment, like a betrayal of everything she stood for. She made the decision anyway, trusting the deeper purpose behind her service over a comfort she had never before needed to question.

The soldiers of her own patrol watched the exchange with visible unease, several of them exchanging glances she did not need to see directly to feel. She did not address their discomfort. There would be time enough later to explain the decision. Right now there was only the working in front of her, and the narrowing window in which it still had any chance of mattering.

Vem watched the angel work over Orwick with an unease he made no particular effort to hide, every instinct in him recoiling at the sight of her blade's edge so close to a soldier already beyond his own ability to help. He had asked for a second opinion. He had not fully accounted for what accepting one might look like — a Sentinel of Heaven kneeling over his own soldier with a blade built to sever the very bond currently unmaking him from the inside.

He forced himself to stay silent through the working itself, some rare discipline overriding his usual habit of filling any tense silence with commentary. The blade moved with a precision he grudgingly respected despite everything his instincts told him about the hand wielding it, a single, careful cut that seemed to loosen something in Orwick's rigid posture almost immediately. Vem didn't fully understand what he'd just watched happen. He understood well enough that it had worked, and that left him owing the angel a debt he had no comfortable way of processing yet.

He watched Orwick's breathing steady by careful degrees, color returning to features that had gone alarmingly slack only minutes earlier, and felt something in his own chest ease alongside it. He had spent the last several hours convinced that nothing in his own considerable arsenal could touch whatever had taken hold of his soldier. Watching a stranger's blade succeed where his own gift had failed left him with relief, and with something uncomfortably close to humility — an emotion he had no intention of examining while an angel still knelt within arm's reach.

Her work stirred every story he had ever heard about Sentinels of the In-Between, tales that painted them as humorless, unbending soldiers with no patience for anything as chaotic as his own temperament. The stories had not prepared him for the actual precision of her working, or for the focus she brought to a soldier who wasn't even her own to protect. None of the accounts he'd heard over the centuries had mentioned this kind of focus, which left him wondering how many other stories he took for granted about her kind were similarly incomplete. He tucked the thought away, unwilling to let his own surprise show any more plainly than it likely already had.

The tension between them eased only slightly once Orwick's condition began, visibly, to stabilize, and Vem, recovering some of his usual temperament now that the immediate crisis had passed, could not resist testing the strange new arrangement they had apparently fallen into. "Relax, sentinel," he said, watching her work with an ease that bordered deliberately on provocation. "I don't bite. Unless asked."

Thoria did not look up from the working. "One more comment like that," she said, flat and entirely without humor, "and I leave you here for the fissure to solve my problem for me." Vem raised both hands, smile still intact but noticeably more careful than it had been a moment earlier, and did not push the joke any further. Whatever else he had learned about her in the short time since first contact, he understood clearly enough that she was not joking.

He retreated a step regardless, an old reflex for self-preservation overriding whatever remained of his usual appetite for provocation. He had tested plenty of tempers over the centuries, angelic and demonic alike, and had learned to read which ones would follow through on a threat and which merely postured. Hers, judging by the flat, unbothered certainty with which she had delivered the line, belonged unmistakably to the first category. He decided, for the moment at least, that testing it further served no purpose worth the risk.

The rest of his own squad kept a careful distance from the exchange, several of them exchanging glances that suggested, to Vem's eye, a similar read on the situation. He did not comment on their evident amusement at his retreat. He had a reputation to maintain among them, and intended to recover at least some of it before the day was finished.

The fissure's next assault arrived without warning, a fresh instability tearing open beneath a cluster of soldiers from both patrols at once. Thoria identified the pull it was exerting on the nearest soul within reach, chose her point of severance, and brought her blade through it in one deliberate cut. Vem moved in the same instant, his staff driving a controlled collapse directly beneath the tear's leading edge, destabilizing the ground fast enough to buy Thoria the extra half-second her own working actually needed.

Neither move alone would have closed the threat in time. Together, staff and blade arriving at the same instability from two directions Thoria had not planned and Vem had not announced, the tear sealed itself as abruptly as it had opened, soldiers from both sides left standing, shaken but intact, on ground that had nearly claimed several of them a moment earlier. Thoria felt something settle in her own chest that she did not immediately have a name for as the demon beside her lowered his staff, his expression, for once, entirely without its usual performance.

She ran the exchange back through her mind in its immediate aftermath, cataloguing how the two workings had complemented each other — his staff buying her the fraction of a second her blade genuinely needed, timed with a precision she doubted had been fully deliberate and yet had arrived exactly when it needed to. She had coordinated with soldiers under her own command for centuries, had built entire tactical doctrines around the kind of complementary timing this exchange had just demonstrated without either of them planning it. She had never once expected to find it, unplanned and unannounced, in a demon she had been threatening to abandon to the fissure only minutes earlier.

Vem found himself replaying the exchange with something closer to genuine surprise than he cared to admit. He had thrown the working on instinct, trusting his own read of the collapsing ground more than any actual coordination with the angel working beside him. That instinct had happened to align, precisely and usefully, with whatever she'd been doing at the same moment. He did not know whether to call that coincidence or something else. She straightened from the now-sealed tear, and he decided he was not yet ready to examine the question too closely.

Neither of them remarked on what had just happened, the closest thing to genuine coordination either patrol had managed since falling into this fissure together. Vem returned to his own squad without comment, watching from the corner of his attention as Thoria did much the same on her own side, both of them settling back into the wary, separate postures that had defined every previous hour of this uneasy arrangement.

Something had shifted regardless, however little Vem intended to acknowledge it — and however little Thoria appeared inclined to do so either. More than once over the stretch that followed, Vem's attention drifted toward the Sentinel's position without the tactical situation strictly requiring it. Whatever this fissure eventually demanded of both of them, he judged, he would rather have her beside him than against him. He said nothing of it to anyone, least of all to her.

Thoria caught herself, later, tracking Vem as he directed his own soldiers through a stretch of unstable ground with a competence she had not expected from someone whose reputation, what little of it she knew, suggested chaos rather than genuine skill. She said nothing of the observation either, tucking it away alongside everything else about this fissure she had not expected to learn.

Orwick, recovering slowly a short distance away, watched the two of them settle into their separate, wary silences without any obvious sign that either understood what had just passed between them. Thoria noticed him watching and offered nothing by way of explanation. Whatever had shifted between her and the demon commander across the unstable ground, it was not, she judged, a thing either patrol needed spoken aloud. The fissure's strange light shifted overhead, indifferent to any of it, and both patrols returned to the same uneasy vigilance that had defined every hour of this arrangement since the moment they first fell into it together.
