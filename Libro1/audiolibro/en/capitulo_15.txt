Chapter 15. The Price of Truth.

The battle broke exactly where Belial had laid the lines, in a thick mud that the boots of both sides churned into a sticky sludge of blood, ash, and sulfur. Blades met in a constant metallic din, and the air filled with bursts of orange fire that lit the crossing at irregular intervals, answered at once by flares of celestial light. A wounded angel went down near Belial's boots, spilling an agonized glow across the mud before being trampled beneath the advancing line, but Belial did not spare him a second glance; none of it was real, not in the way that mattered. It was theater, a scaffolding of flesh and noise built to keep every eye fixed on the melee while the world's true wound was forged in silence.

Aamon fought at the center with the blind ferocity of an old butcher, wielding his heavy, serrated blade with a brutal precision that carved gaps of red light into the angelic line. His obsidian face was streaked with mud and greasy sweat, and he roared a wordless triumph every time his steel broke through one of the Host's shields. Not once did he look down at the ground he stood on, nor sideways at the commander riding beside him. Belial matched his pace with the mace Dubium still clean at his hip, measuring with a bookkeeper's patience the last minutes of a man who was already dead long before the first blow ever reached him.

...

The angelic captain leading the patrol had an eye too well trained by centuries on the frontier not to feel the dissonance in the formation attacking him. This was not the disordered chaos of an ordinary demonic ambush, that wave of rage and sulfur that crashes against shields by sheer inertia. This was too clean. There was a rehearsed rhythm in the spacing between officers and a systematic discipline in the flanks' fallback that tasted of chalk in his throat.

"Regroup!" he shouted, his command voice cutting through the din with a sharp edge of urgency. "Close the right flank before it stretches too thin!"

The nearest angels broke off combat at once, wings snapping open as they wheeled toward the named position, seeking a defensive line that, with a few more seconds, might have held. The captain allowed himself one moment of grim satisfaction at seeing his soldiers' discipline answer so readily. But then the front's angle shifted in a way his military training had never taught him to expect, and the order he had just given turned to ash before the first shield finished settling into place.

...

Belial's own honor guard did not press on against the angels; the instant their commander's signal reached them, they wheeled on their own heels in a single motion, drilled in secret for weeks, and drove their swords into the backs of Aamon's personal guard.

The betrayal took less than a heartbeat to complete, but the silence it left in its wake spread across the crossing with the weight of a block of frozen ice. For one suspended second, the whole skirmish seemed to hold its breath; the angels' blades froze mid-swing, and the Host's soldiers stood paralyzed in the mud, their eyes darting between two clusters of demons tearing each other apart with a savagery that belonged to none of the rules of their official war. Aamon's guard fell within moments beneath the steel of their own allies. One of Belial's lieutenants pinned three of Aamon's officers against a crumbling ridge, cutting their throats one after another in the mud before they could form a single order.

Belial advanced through the corridor of flesh opening before him with the metronomic stride of a man walking a path he has memorized in the dark. His boots trod the pale dust and black blood of his own legion without hurry, the mace Dubium still silent and clean at his hip, since none of that violence had required him to spend his own strength to clear the way.

Aamon staggered in the center of the mud, already wounded by his own soldiers' steel, black blood running down his arm, his obsidian face twisted with a confusion he still couldn't make sense of. Belial pushed him back, step by step, turning aside his furious lunges with dry, measured blocks of his mace, herding him without his commander noticing toward the stretch of ground marked weeks before.

Aamon glanced down for an instant and found the sigil's lines glowing faintly beneath the mud on his boots. His stance faltered for the first time.

"What is this?" he demanded, his voice cracking into a hoarse roar that betrayed, beneath the rage, the first pull of panic.

Belial began the invocation. The syllables of the forbidden ninth-tier text fell over the plaza with a physical weight, thickening the air until breathing itself became a cold effort. The ground answered at once: a sickly purple light climbed the sigil's lines, coiling around Aamon's legs like invisible threads of iron. The commander tried to step back, but his boots no longer answered him, anchored to stone that had decided not to obey.

"For power, Aamon," Belial said, low enough to make sure the words belonged to only the two of them. "The price must always be paid."

With a desperate roar, Aamon lunged forward, carving a savage arc with his serrated blade toward Belial's throat with all the strength his wounded body still held. Belial ducked beneath the swing, feeling the wind of the steel pass over his shoulder, and answered with a single downward blow. The heavy head of the mace Dubium connected with a dry crack against his commander's knee, shattering the bone and forcing him to collapse onto the glowing lines of the stone altar.

The seal opened with a sound that belonged not to metal but to suffering stone: a dull, drawn-out, wrenching wail, like a mourner's sob that snuffed out the carnage's noise all at once. Aamon's essence — that vast, dense military weight Belial had calculated with such care in the loyalty rolls — began to pour toward the crack in unspooling filaments of dark light drawn from his chest, sucked into a void that seemed to grow hungrier by the second. Belial raised the mace one last time and brought it down into the center of his commander's black heart. There was no blood; only a final burst of raw demonic power swallowed by the sigil, and Aamon's body went still atop the glass, his eyes fixed and empty, pointing at nothing.

The portal to the Weeping Sepulcher opened before him, a wound of cold purple light that pulsed once with a slow, patient rhythm. A current of cold air slid from its edge, carrying a thick taste of salt, old stone, and a grief older than the Fall itself, cooling against Belial's skin, still sticky with the sour sweat of the mud.

Around him, the surviving patrol of the Host began a hasty retreat. The angelic captain who minutes before had shouted his useless order dragged a wounded soldier toward the rear, his eyes crossing Belial's for one instant before he turned and fled; they had understood, too late, that what was opening beneath their feet was something far worse than a border ambush. The survivors of Aamon's legion were left behind, scattered and leaderless, some falling back in loose knots, others simply standing still, understanding in the silence that their commander would never return. Belial paid them no attention. He stood alone before the threshold of his long, costly scaffolding, the mace still in hand and the blood cooling on his fingers, contemplating at last the entrance to a darkness that had waited eras to be inherited.
