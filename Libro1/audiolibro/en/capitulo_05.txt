Chapter 5. Solmire's Judgment.

Joran had been fighting under Nael's banner for less than a year, and in that year he had learned to measure time by the taste of burnt grease and slaked lime that clung to the back of his throat. The crystalline towers of Zaphor'el were sick monuments now. The abyss's corruption had wrapped them like a hot gangrene, veins of black, pulsing flesh coiling through the crystal, weeping a thick mud that boiled where it touched the open air. To his left, Nael held the center of the line, her old-gold armor dented and filthy, wielding Audacia with a rhythm that was no longer heroic, only economical.

The war machine came from an angle no one had covered. Joran saw it a half-second before it struck the ground beside Nael, close enough that he felt the heat of it from three ranks back, and he was already shouting for her to fall back before the dust had settled.

"Fall back!" His voice cracked on it. "Nael, fall back!"

She did not. Blood ran from somewhere along her hairline, and she held the line anyway, her gauntlet still raised, still glowing. "If I fall back, they all fall back." Her eyes found Joran's for one moment, steady despite everything. "Not yet."

She fell three seconds later, cut down from the flank while her attention was still fixed on holding the center. She did not fall with a hymn's grace; she went down sideways, with a dry crack of broken bronze and flesh giving way. Joran did not see the blow land. He heard it, and turned in time to see her go down, and felt something in the line around him buckle at the sound of it — not a rout, not yet, but the particular silence that comes just before one. Around him, soldiers who had been holding formation on Nael's voice alone suddenly had nothing to hold it on, and Joran felt the gap where her banner had flown an instant ago like a wound of his own.

It was in that silence, that half-breath before the line could decide whether to break, that Joran first heard the horns.

He had no time to look for their source. The demonic war machine that had struck beside Nael was already grinding forward again, and Joran's body moved on instinct alone, dragging her back from the front rank while two others closed the gap she'd left. Around him the battle raged on in its old, familiar shape — angelic soldiers pinned against fortifications of bone and pulsing flesh, infernal machines too numerous to count, the crystalline ruins of a once-beautiful city serving now as cover for things that had no business sheltering in it.

He was still crouched over Nael, pressing what he could against the wound at her side, when the light reached him.

It came from behind the angelic line, and it did not arrive like reinforcement usually arrived — no horn answering horn, no fresh ranks pushing forward. It came instead as a single figure walking into a battle that should have killed anyone moving that slowly. Miguel held the sword aloft rather than swinging it, and with each step a wave of light rolled outward from the blade, and everything the light touched simply stopped being what it had been a moment before.

Solmire's tide of light rolled over Joran's trench like a gust of frozen air that stole his breath and left a flat, metallic taste on his tongue. The demonic war machines did not burst into flame; they simply lost their footing and crumbled into a pale, cold dust that the wind carried off within a second, leaving no body, no mark, no trace that blood had been spilled there a heartbeat before. The pulsing corruption sheathing the nearest tower peeled away like a shed skin, leaving crystal beneath it, whole and gleaming, untouched by whatever had grown over it for however many years. Joran had spent the morning watching soldiers die by inches for every foot of ground gained. He watched Miguel take that same ground in strides, and take it clean.

The soldiers around him had stopped fighting entirely now, watching the wave of light roll forward the way a person watches weather too large to argue with. No one cheered yet. It was too soon for that, and something in the silence of the advancing light made cheering feel, for the moment, beside the point.

He still had Nael's blood drying on his hands, and he did not know whether to call what he was watching a rescue. It felt, from where he knelt, closer to something out of scripture — the kind of story told to children, made suddenly, terribly real in front of him.

...

Mira had been hiding in the lower vaults of Zaphor'el for longer than she could accurately measure, ever since the corruption first took the upper city and made the streets unsafe for anything that still had a name to lose. She came up into the light only because the ground had stopped shaking, and the silence that replaced the shaking felt, cautiously, like it might mean something other than a new kind of danger.

The plaza she emerged into was not the plaza of her childhood. The corruption was gone, leaving the crystal clean and whole; but it was a dead beauty, the marble of the central fountain scrubbed so bare of any patina of time that it looked as though it had never once been touched.

An old man beside her reached out and touched the nearest wall, testing it as though it might not be solid. It was. He drew his hand back anyway, and said nothing, and Mira understood, watching his face, that whatever had won this battle for them had not left behind a version of home she recognized.

A child nearby began to cry, a sharp, frightened sound that the plaza's flat air seemed to muffle at once, denying it even an echo. Miguel's sword had not restored her home; it had torn out the entire page where her life was written, leaving in its place a blank space she did not know how to inhabit.

...

Raphael moved through the aftermath doing what he had always done, seeking out the wounded and offering what Veritas could give them. He found Nael first, propped against a fallen column with Joran still pressing a hand to her side, and knelt to lay his staff against the wound.

The staff answered with its warm, organic resonance of gold and green light, color returning to her cheeks with the slowness of life reclaiming its course. Nael's injury was honest, uncomplicated, the kind of wound Raphael had closed a thousand times without a second thought, and the young soldier who had been pressing a hand to her side finally allowed himself to sit back on his heels. But when Raphael drew his hand back from the wound and laid it flat against the crystal paving of the plaza, his spiritual perception froze.

He found none of the vibration of earth wounded by infernal fire, that hot ash which still holds the potential to shelter a seed once the storm passes. There was nothing here; an absolute void, flat and sterile, as if Solmire had erased not only the corruption, but the crystal's very capacity to register time or life.

He rose slowly and crossed the plaza to where Miguel stood, Solmire resting point-down against the crystal at his feet. "The ground here doesn't just lack corruption, Miguel," Raphael said, keeping his voice level. "It lacks everything. There's nothing left in it to grow back."

Miguel's expression did not change. His eyes, fixed on the legions singing hymns along the terraces, stayed as cold as the crystal beneath his boots. "Corruption is a weed, Raphael," Miguel answered, the certainty in his voice sounding metronomic, untouched by the weariness of the campaign. "To save the garden, you sometimes have to scorch the soil."

"A garden scorched by ordinary fire can still regrow, given time." Raphael's emerald eyes held steady on him. "This can't. I don't think it's meant to."

Miguel offered no response to that. He turned instead toward the cheering rising from the soldiers further down the plaza, and after a moment, so did Raphael, though the unease he carried did not lift with the sound. He glanced once more at the barren ground beneath Nael before he moved on to the next wounded soldier waiting for him.

...

The capital fell before nightfall, and by the time Miguel reached its center the fighting had already given way to celebration — soldiers embracing amid streets scoured clean of every trace of the corruption that had held this city for years. Word of Nael's survival had already spread through the ranks, passed soldier to soldier the way good news always found its way faster than orders did. Zaphor'el gleamed now, crystal towers catching the last of the daylight, pristine in a way that felt less like restoration than replacement.

Miguel climbed to a high balcony overlooking the plaza, ostensibly to survey the victory, though no one below seemed to need his presence for the celebration to carry on without him. He did not look at the celebration below. He looked instead at Solmire, turning the blade slightly in the fading light, feeling its low, constant hum against his palm more clearly than he felt anything rising from the crowd beneath him. The cheers reached him thin and distant, as though the city itself had muffled them on their way up.

He told himself this was exhaustion, the natural quiet that followed any hard-won victory. He did not fully believe it. Below him, someone began singing an old hymn of thanksgiving, and others joined in one by one until the sound filled the plaza, and Miguel found he could not make himself join them, could not find in himself whatever part of a soldier was supposed to answer that song. Something in him had already begun listening to the sword more than to the soldiers who had fought and bled beside him today, and he set that fact aside, as he had set aside so much else since Serephis, and did not examine it further.

...

Uriel found a quiet stretch of wall away from the celebration and, for the first time since the battle began, let his shoulders drop.

He had won his wager. Zaphor'el had fallen in a single afternoon, just as Miguel had promised at the war council, reading the sword's silent hum. Twenty silver coins weighed in his baldric, coins Camael hadn't dared refuse him.

He had backed this offensive from the first moment Miguel proposed it, had argued for it with more conviction than he usually bothered mustering for anything short of open war. He did not regret that now, exactly. He had wanted a swift end to a siege that had bled soldiers for years, and by any measure that could be written into a report, he had gotten one. But standing here, looking out over a city won in an afternoon at a cost he could not fully account for, he found the certainty that had carried him through the war council harder to locate than it had been that morning.

And yet, looking out over the gleaming city, Uriel felt a strange, cold nausea in his stomach. He thought of Raphael's words about the sterile ground. He thought of the inhuman speed of the erasure, a victory so cheap and effortless it seemed to mock the sweat and blood soldiers like Nael had spent holding the line before Miguel arrived. For a general of the Host, victory had always been a pact of blood and endurance, a physical fact that required the enemy have some chance to fight before being defeated. This did not feel like a battle won. It felt like a page torn out of history by an absolute that cared nothing for them, for their courage, or for their faith.

Uriel kept his silence, reaching into his baldric to make the twenty silver coins ring, but the sound of the metal suddenly struck him as out of tune, orphaned of the glory it was supposed to represent. There would be time enough for that later, he told himself, once the doubt had a clearer shape to it than unease standing quietly against a wall. For now, he pushed off from the stone and went to find the others, straightening his shoulders before he came back into view, and let the crack in his certainty close over, for the moment, unremarked.
