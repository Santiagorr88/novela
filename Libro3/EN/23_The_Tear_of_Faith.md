# Chapter 23 — The Tear of Faith

The private chamber Gabriel led them to after the council sat quiet and unadorned, a small room built for conversation rather than ceremony, its single window opening onto a stretch of garden Michael half remembered tending once, long ago, in some version of himself that felt increasingly distant with every hour spent back in this city. The council chamber's noise had followed them partway down the corridor before finally fading, replaced by the particular hush of a room built to hold difficult conversations rather than public spectacle.

Michael sat, finally allowing some of the tension he'd been carrying since the gates to ease out of his shoulders, and found the relief shorter-lived than he'd hoped for. He had barely settled into the room's stillness before a knock sounded at the door, brisk and economical, the kind of knock that belonged to a man who had never once wasted motion on anything unnecessary.

Camael entered without waiting for an invitation, closing the door behind him with the same efficient care. Michael braced himself for another confrontation, another version of the fury he'd already weathered once tonight, and found instead a face that held something considerably heavier than anger — a stillness in his eyes that reminded Michael of soldiers he'd once watched return from engagements too large for any debriefing to fully cover.

"I need to speak with you," Camael said, addressing Michael directly, his gaze flicking once toward Azael and Gabriel both before settling back. "Alone, if that's permitted. What I have to say isn't for the wider council yet."

---

Gabriel inclined his head and stepped toward the door without argument, Azael following a beat behind him, silent as always. Camael waited until the door had closed fully before he spoke again, his voice rough in a way Michael hadn't heard from him before, worn down by something that hadn't finished wearing yet, Michael guessed.

"I commanded the fleet at the In-Between," Camael said. "I saw our light and their fire swallowed by... nothing." He paused, jaw working once before he continued. "My faith is not in scripture or prophecy, Michael. My faith is in the shield wall. It is in the angel standing next to me."

Michael said nothing, letting the silence stretch rather than pressing Camael toward whatever came next. He remembered Camael from a hundred old campaigns, a commander who had never once wasted words on anything beyond what a briefing strictly required. This silence felt different from that old economy, heavier, and Michael found himself reading it as the particular quiet of a man still searching for words large enough to hold what he actually needed to say.

"I've heard the testimony you gave tonight," Camael continued. "Most of the council heard rumor and doubt in it. I heard something else. I've stood at the edge of that silence myself. I know what it costs a man to stand there and keep standing anyway." He met Michael's eyes directly. "My faith was never a promise that we'd win. It's a commitment to keep fighting regardless of whether winning is actually on offer. That's the only kind of faith I've ever trusted enough to build a command on."

"What did you see out there?" Michael asked, keeping the question plain rather than probing, hoping the plainness itself might leave Camael more room to answer than a sharper question would have. He had learned, across nine years spent teaching men and women who'd survived their own private horrors, that a question asked gently rarely got a smaller answer than one asked directly. It simply got a truer one.

Camael was quiet a moment before answering. "Legions on both sides, mine and theirs, throwing everything they had at something that simply didn't register the attack at all. Not resisted. Not absorbed. Simply unaffected, the way stone is unaffected by a man shouting at it." He shook his head once, a small, controlled motion. "I've fought losing battles before. I know what losing feels like. This wasn't that. This was closer to irrelevance, and I've never once found a tactic that answers irrelevance properly."

"I understand," Michael said. "More than I wish I did."

"I believe you do," Camael said. "That's precisely why I'm here." He straightened slightly, some old soldier's discipline reasserting itself in his posture. "I don't offer this lightly, Michael. I've never once offered anything lightly. But I've spent too many years commanding good soldiers to keep sending them at a wall that doesn't even notice it's being struck. If there's an actual answer to what we saw out there, I intend to be part of finding it."

---

Camael reached up and unclasped a plain steel locket from around his neck, turning it once in his fingers before he spoke again. "I forged this myself," he said. "By hand, during the long watch after the fleet came back from the In-Between. It wasn't planned. I needed something to do with my hands while my mind was still working through what we'd seen out there. Metalwork was the only thing that kept me from simply sitting in the dark replaying it."

He held the locket a moment longer before opening it, his eyes lingering on its plain steel casing longer than the object itself seemed to warrant. Michael watched the pause and read it, cautiously, as a man revisiting a memory he hadn't looked at quite this closely in some time. "I've made a few things with my hands over the centuries. Never anything quite like this one. I'm still not entirely sure what compelled me to keep it once it was finished, rather than simply setting it aside with everything else from that night."

He opened the locket carefully, revealing a single solidified tear resting against black velvet, glowing with a steady, silver light that held none of the flicker or waver Michael might have expected from something born of grief. Michael studied the small object a moment, struck by how plain it looked for something Camael was describing with this much weight behind it — no ornamentation, no ceremony, simply a working man's answer to a question that had apparently kept him awake through several long nights.

"This tear is not from my despair," Camael said. "It is from the faith that followed it." He held the locket out, offering it directly. "Take it. It's yours to carry, if it can actually help close whatever wound you're describing."

Michael accepted it with both hands, feeling the tear's quiet, steady warmth against his palms, a weight considerably lighter than he'd expected from something carrying this much significance. He studied the small light a moment before closing his fingers around it carefully, aware, holding it, of exactly how many things Camael could have chosen to do with a grief this large instead of shaping it into something worth handing to another man's cause.

---

"Thank you," Michael said, and found the words entirely insufficient for what he actually felt, though he had no better ones currently available to offer in their place. "This means more than you likely understand. Not just what it's meant to accomplish. What it cost you to make it worth accomplishing anything at all."

"I understand plenty," Camael said, something almost like a dry edge entering his flat, economical voice. "I understand that Uriel is a fool." He said it plainly, without heat, a simple statement of fact rather than an insult thrown for effect. "He fights for dogma. I fight for home. There's a difference, and it's one I've had considerably longer than tonight to think through properly."

He studied Michael a moment longer, something settling into his expression that Michael read, cautiously, as a decision already made rather than one still being weighed. "My legions will follow you, Michael," Camael said. "Not because the council votes to permit it. Because I've decided it's the right call, and my soldiers trust my calls." He said it without any of the ceremony the moment might have warranted, plain fact rather than pledge, the same economical certainty Michael remembered from every order he'd ever heard the man issue.

He reached out and gripped Michael's forearm, the gesture brief but absolute, carrying more weight in its plain simplicity than any formal oath the council might eventually manage to draft. Michael returned the grip, matching Camael's grasp with his own, and found the exchange said everything neither of them currently had the patience to put into longer words.

---

Michael held the locket carefully as Camael released his arm and stepped back, studying the steady silver light still glowing faintly through the small steel case. He thought of everything this single tear actually represented — not blind faith, not easy comfort, but a man's deliberate choice to keep believing in something worth defending even after watching that something nearly swallowed whole by a silence with no name. He understood, holding it, exactly how much this single object had actually cost the man standing in front of him to produce.

He thought, too, of the council chamber he'd just left, still ringing with Uriel's fury and the uncertain murmur that had followed Gabriel's declaration. Camael had offered none of that theater. He had simply walked into a private room, said what needed saying, and handed over the one piece of proof that actually mattered more than any argument Michael could have constructed on his own. There was something almost restful in the plainness of it, after everything the wider council had just put him through.

"I won't waste what this cost you," Michael said.

"See that you don't," Camael said, and for just a moment, something almost like warmth crossed his usually contained features before settling back into its familiar, economical stillness. "I've buried enough good soldiers already, Michael. I'd like the next batch of losses, if there have to be any, to at least mean something when they happen. I've spent enough of this war watching men die for reasons that never quite added up afterward. I won't spend what's left of it doing that again if I can help it."

He turned toward the door, pausing once with his hand on the handle. "Gabriel and the pale one are waiting outside. I imagine they'll want to know how this went." He glanced back, something dry returning to his expression. "Tell them it went well enough. I'm not in the habit of repeating myself twice in the same evening." He held that pause a moment longer, saying nothing further, his expression settling back into its usual, unreadable calm.

He left without waiting for a response, the door closing behind him with the same brisk economy he'd entered with. Michael stood alone a moment longer, the tear's steady light still glowing quietly in his cupped hands, and felt, for the first time since he'd walked through Heaven's gates, that the road ahead had grown one crucial step shorter.

One of the two elements Aetheris needed now rested safely in his keeping. Somewhere south, in a city he'd never visited, Thaeriel was hunting the other on his own separate road, no word yet of whether he'd actually found it. Two halves of an ancient promise, gathered — or still being gathered — by two very different roads, both converging now toward the same distant reforging. Michael closed his hand carefully around the locket, unwilling to risk even the smallest carelessness with something that had cost this much to obtain, and allowed himself, for the first time in longer than he could easily measure, a single quiet moment of something that felt almost like hope.

He crossed to the door and opened it, finding Gabriel and Azael exactly where Camael had said they'd be, standing quietly in the corridor beyond. Gabriel's eyes moved immediately to the small light still visible through Michael's closed fingers, and something in his expression eased, however slightly, at the sight of it.

"It went well enough," Michael said, echoing Camael's own dry phrasing without quite managing to keep the small smile out of his voice as he said it. He held up the locket, the tear's steady glow catching the corridor's dim light. "One piece down. One to go."

Azael said nothing, but his gaze settled on the small light a moment, and Michael found himself, standing there in the quiet corridor, allowing the first real relief of the entire long day to finally settle into his chest.
