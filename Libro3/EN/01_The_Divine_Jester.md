# Chapter 1 — The Divine Jester

The tavern smelled of spilled ale and old sawdust, and it took Mikel three full circuits of the room before he spotted the man he'd crossed two borders and a patient week of methodical work to find. The seven marks on his map had given him no pattern by themselves, exactly as they hadn't when he'd first drawn them — three centuries of footnotes still pointing at three different coastlines with equal confidence. What had actually closed the distance was an eighth thread, a folklore archive two towns over that had, buried in a decades-old newspaper clipping, a photograph of a street musician playing outside this exact building, described by the photographer as having "the strangest mismatched eyes." He'd worked outward from there through four bars and a music hall before someone finally pointed him toward this one, tucked down an alley too narrow for the address to have shown up on any map he'd have thought to check on his own. Milo Ray sat on a stool at the far end of the bar, a battered guitar across one knee, his sun-bleached hair loose and unbothered, playing to a crowd that seemed to have decided he belonged to them for the night. His feet were bare under the stool. Mikel noted that detail the way he noted most things lately — carefully, and without any longer being surprised by it.

He pushed through the crowd until he stood close enough to be heard over the noise, and waited for a gap between verses that never quite arrived. Milo's fingers kept moving, easy and unhurried, coaxing something bawdy and cheerful out of six worn strings while the room around him laughed at a joke Mikel had missed the setup for. The guitar itself looked older than anything else in the building, its varnish worn away at the edges where a thumb had rested a thousand times before, a crack running along one side that someone had mended with a thin strip of dark tape years ago and never bothered to fix properly since.

"I need to ask you about the echoes," Mikel said, pitching his voice to carry over the din.

Milo's eyes found him — one amber, one the pale silver of an old coin — and something in them sharpened for exactly as long as it took to register the question, then softened back into performance. He didn't stop playing. He didn't even slow down. He simply folded the words into the next line of the song, rhyming *echoes* with *depend on* and *far too many men have tried to bend on*, and the crowd roared with laughter at the sight of the serious man in the wrinkled coat trying to interrupt the music with something that sounded, in context, like the setup to a joke about a lover.

Mikel felt his face go hot. He had not crossed two borders for this. He'd pictured this meeting a dozen ways during the long drive here, most of them involving a quiet corner and a serious conversation, and none of them had involved becoming, however briefly, the punchline of a stranger's drinking song. A man near the bar clapped him on the shoulder on his way past, grinning, entirely unaware he was congratulating Mikel on a joke made at his own expense.

---

The song shifted without warning, the cheerful chord progression bending into something slower and more minor, and the crowd's attention thinned as half of them lost interest in a tune that no longer promised anything to dance to. Milo's voice dropped with it, quieter now, almost private despite the room around him.

*Three bright brothers, before the world had a name for morning,*
*One laughed the light into being, never asking, never warning.*
*One judged through fire and through the weight of every debt unpaid,*
*One waited in the silence, keeping the scales the other two had made.*

Mikel felt something cold settle behind his sternum. He recognized pieces of it without being able to say how — the judge through fire pulled at something in him that felt like Arin, like Thaeriel, a name he'd only recently learned to hold steady in his own head. The one who waited in silence pulled at something older and stranger, a corridor beneath a university, a figure he had met exactly once and had not stopped thinking about since.

*Their love became pride, and pride became a game,*
*and the game broke the first toy they were given — a world without a name.*

The verse ended there, unresolved, hanging on a chord Milo let ring out longer than it needed to. Mikel searched the words for himself and found nothing. Three brothers. Three roles, each one accounted for, each one recognizable in some dim, half-remembered way. He was not one of them. He ran through the verse a second time in his head, checking each line the way he'd have checked a translation against its source, in case he'd simply missed his own reflection somewhere in the words. He hadn't. He felt the absence of himself in that song more sharply than he'd felt anything the ballad had actually named, and he didn't understand why that should unsettle him more than the parts he did recognize, but it did.

"Who wrote that," Mikel asked, when the chord had finally faded. "Really. Not a folk tune. Not something you picked up on the road."

Milo's mismatched eyes held his a moment, weighing something. "I did," he said. "A long time before guitars existed to write it on. My name's Ereloth, if you want the one that actually matches the song. Milo's just the one that fits better at a bar."

He said it plainly, without ceremony, the way a man might correct a small mispronunciation of his own name rather than deliver a revelation. Mikel felt the word settle into him regardless, heavier than its plainness should have allowed.

---

Milo set the guitar down against the bar, unhurried, and the noise of the tavern seemed to fold away from the two of them without actually growing any quieter. He stepped closer, and for the first time since Mikel had walked in, the easy performer's smile went somewhere else entirely.

"After they broke it," Milo said, low enough that it felt aimed at Mikel alone, "somebody had to build a failsafe. Divine Law, they called it, dressed up nice so it would sound like justice instead of what it actually was — a cage. Reincarnation was the door on it. Revolving. Kept everybody inside cycling past each other, never quite touching, never quite remembering enough to finish the argument that started it all."

He reached out and touched two fingers to the center of Mikel's chest, light and deliberate, and held them there a moment longer than the gesture strictly required.

"You were the first and best part of that cage, big brother. The perfect warden. Because you believed in the bars."

Mikel didn't move. He had expected, on some level he hadn't examined closely enough to name, that finding Milo Ray would mean finding answers shaped like the ones he'd spent a month gathering — footnotes, dates, careful cross-references. He had not expected to be called a warden. He had not expected the words to land with the specific, sickening accuracy of something true. He thought, unwillingly, of nine years spent grading papers by a rulebook he'd never once questioned, of every argument he'd ever settled by pointing to a rule rather than asking whether the rule itself deserved the authority he'd given it. He had built an entire career on believing in bars of one kind or another. He had never once thought to ask who had built them, or why, or whether the order he'd enforced so carefully had ever actually been his to enforce.

---

"You're talking about the end of the world," Mikel said, "like it's a punchline."

Milo's head tipped back, and the laugh that came out of him was warm and terrible at once, a sound too large for the small space between them. Several heads turned. Nobody seemed to find it strange.

"Because it *is* a joke, Miki!" He wiped at one eye, still grinning, the grin not quite covering something sharper underneath it. "Your problem was never the fall. It was that you forgot how to fly, and you can't fly if you're afraid to laugh at the sky."

The sentence landed and then simply sat there, offering nothing further. Mikel opened his mouth to demand an explanation and found he didn't have one ready — not a question precise enough to deserve a real answer, not from a man who'd just told him, without flinching, that he'd spent some unknowable span of his existence guarding a prison he'd mistaken for order. He tried anyway. "Do you have any idea how long I've spent trying to piece together even the smallest fragment of this? Weeks. Months, if I'm honest about when it actually started." Milo only shrugged, unbothered, and reached for his drink.

---

He became aware, in the silence that followed, of the room continuing around them exactly as it had before. Nobody was staring. Nobody had gone quiet at the sound of a man laughing about the apocalypse two feet from a stranger's face. The bartender was still pulling the same pint he'd been pulling for the last five minutes. A pair of students near the door were still arguing, cheerfully, about something that had nothing to do with any of this. To every other person in the building, Mikel understood, this was still just a busker and an odd customer standing too close together after last call.

That should have made the moment feel smaller. It didn't. It made it feel entirely his, in a way nothing else in the last month had managed, not the archivist's careful questions, not the seven marks on his map, not even the corridor beneath Navarion. Whatever this was, it belonged to exactly two people in the room, and he was one of them. He thought of the archivist then, briefly, and of how she'd have looked at this scene — a busker and a professor, standing too close together after last call, the professor pale as a sheet for no reason anyone else in the room could name. She would have asked, dryly, whether he'd finally lost the plot entirely. He almost wished she were here to ask it.

---

"If I'm not one of the three," Mikel said finally, "then what am I?"

Milo studied him for a long moment, the performer's brightness gone out of his face entirely, replaced by something quieter and more careful than anything Mikel had seen from him yet.

"You're the one who carried the light without being born of it," he said. "That's not nothing, Miki. Sometimes it's harder."

Mikel weighed the sentence carefully, checking it against everything he already knew the way he'd have checked a difficult translation against its source, and found, to his own surprise, that it fit. His first instinct, hearing it, had been to assume that not belonging to the song meant he didn't matter to whatever story it was telling. He understood now that the opposite might be closer to true — that carrying something you hadn't made yourself, and had never fully understood, might be its own kind of weight, distinct from theirs but no lighter for the difference. He thought of every year he'd spent teaching other people's failures to students who hadn't lived through them, explaining what a commander should have done with a clarity he'd never once had to earn the hard way himself. Perhaps this was the same kind of work, in a shape he hadn't recognized yet. Carrying a light he hadn't forged, trying to do right by it anyway.

The name stayed with him after that, the way it had every time Milo used it. Not Mikel, which had always felt like a name borrowed for convenience. Not Michael, which felt like a name that belonged to someone else's memory of him, too large and too old to answer to comfortably. Miki sat somewhere between the two, small and specific, the kind of name only someone who actually knew you would bother using.

"Drink?" Milo asked, already reaching for the guitar again, the brightness sliding back over his face like a curtain drawn shut. "You look like a man who's had a long week finding a bar." He nodded toward the bartender, who was already pouring something without having been asked twice, the particular ease of a regular customer's unspoken order.

Mikel almost laughed. What actually came out was rougher than that, an unpracticed exhale that never quite finished becoming laughter, but it was closer to the real thing than anything he'd managed in longer than he wanted to admit.

"You have no idea," he said.

Milo grinned, picked the guitar back up, and struck a chord that pulled the whole room's attention back toward him inside of three notes, as though nothing at all had happened in the quiet space just before it. Somewhere behind the bar, a glass clinked against another, ordinary and unremarkable, and the low hum of a dozen unrelated conversations swallowed the last of the silence between them. Mikel watched him for a moment longer, still working through everything that had just been said, before he let it settle for the night. He found an empty stool and sat down to wait for whatever came next.
