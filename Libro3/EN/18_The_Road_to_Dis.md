# Chapter 18 — The Road to Dis

Thaeriel felt the legion before he saw it, a mass of coiled fury waiting somewhere ahead in the smoke-dark passage, hungry for a fight that had never once come to it on fair terms. He walked into their sight without slowing, and watched their ranks bristle at the sight of him, weapons rising, mouths already opening around threats he'd heard some version of a thousand times before in a thousand other mouths.

He did not raise a hand against them. He reached instead for the single thread that held their fury together, the raw, undirected rage that gave every one of them a reason to keep moving through an existence that had otherwise long since stopped offering them anything worth wanting. He found it, and pressed his judgment against it, patient and unrelenting, the way he'd learned to press against Malignus's iron lie.

The effort cost him immediately, a familiar ache settling low behind his ribs as the resistance pushed back against him. Fury this raw did not want to yield any more than a city's foundational lie had wanted to yield. He held his ground against it anyway, and felt, one demon at a time, the rage in their chests go quiet, replaced by nothing at all — no remorse, no clarity, simply an absence where the only thing sustaining them had been. They stood there afterward, weapons lowering on their own, faces gone slack with a confusion none of them had the anger left to voice.

He counted eleven of them before the last verdict landed, each one costing marginally more than the one before it, the cumulative weight settling into his shoulders the way a long march settles into a man's legs regardless of how evenly he paces himself. He had not expected, setting out this morning, that a single legion would test him this thoroughly. He understood now that every legion between here and Dis would likely test him the same way, and adjusted his expectations for the remaining distance accordingly.

He walked through the scattering ranks without another word, exhaustion already threading deeper into his limbs than he'd have liked this early in the day's march. He had crossed a dozen circles since Malignus, each one costing him some fresh measure of the reserve he'd once assumed was bottomless, and understood now, with a clarity that had taken real suffering to earn, exactly how mistaken that assumption had been. Every verdict he handed down drew from the same well. The well, he suspected, had a bottom somewhere, and he had not yet found it, but he no longer doubted it existed.

He kept walking regardless. Whatever the cost turned out to be, it had not yet asked more of him than he was willing to give.

---

A lone torturer crossed his path an hour later, working alone in a chamber lined with implements Thaeriel didn't let himself look at too closely. The creature turned at his approach, snarling something defiant, and reached for the nearest of his tools with a hand that trembled with more habit than actual conviction.

The instrument crumbled to dust between his fingers before he'd finished lifting it, followed by the next, and the next, each one dissolving the instant Thaeriel's attention settled on it. He did not attack the torturer directly. He simply looked at the purpose those tools had been forged to serve, and found it wanting on grounds too plain to need any further argument. The torturer stood amid the settling dust of his own life's work, staring at empty hands, and said nothing further as Thaeriel walked past him.

He was not a warrior, moving through Hell the way a blade moves through resistance. He understood that more clearly with every mile behind him. He was closer to a law given legs, implacable and exact, and the walking itself cost that law something real every time it found cause to speak. A blade grew dull with use and could be sharpened again by any competent hand. Whatever grew dull in him after a day like this one, he suspected, would need something considerably harder to earn back than a whetstone and a little patience.

He rested only briefly before continuing, unwilling to let the ache settle into something worse than it already was. The road ahead had not gotten any shorter for his stopping, and Dis was not going to wait any longer than it already had.

---

His path led him, some hours later, through a forgotten corner of Hell that smelled less of sulfur than of simple neglect, cracked stone and cold air replacing the usual furnace heat of the circles behind him. He found a small, misshapen demon crouched over an infernal beast twice its own size, the creature's flank torn open and weeping something dark, its low, pained sounds the only noise in the entire hollow.

The demon worked with clumsy, careful hands, packing the wound with whatever poor materials it had managed to gather, flinching each time the beast groaned beneath its touch but not once pulling away from the work. Thaeriel understood, watching a moment longer, that the beast belonged to this demon the way property belonged to an owner rather than the way a companion belonged to a friend — a selfish claim, not a generous one. He reached for judgment anyway, out of long habit, searching for the hook his power could set itself into.

He found nothing worth condemning. Whatever selfishness lived in that claim of ownership, it moved alongside something else entirely — a plain, wordless refusal to let a suffering thing suffer alone, motive murky and unremarkable and, in its own small way, entirely blameless. His judgment passed over the scene and found no purchase anywhere within it. He stood at the hollow's edge a moment longer than the verdict strictly required, watching the small demon work with a patience that owed nothing to anyone watching it. He had spent this entire march finding wrongness everywhere he looked. Finding none, for once, felt almost as strange as finding it had.

He walked on without a word, and the demon, glancing up too late to fully register what had almost happened, simply returned to its work, unharmed and none the wiser for how close a verdict had actually come.

---

In his private chamber deep within Dis, Belial watched Thaeriel's advance unfold through a drifting sheet of smoke that answered to his will alone, each new scene resolving out of the haze the instant he asked it to. Legion after legion, dismantled without a single blade crossing another. A torturer's tools reduced to dust by nothing more than a glance. Belial had commanded armies large enough to darken skies, had broken cities that thought themselves unbreakable, and found himself, watching this unhurried, unstoppable procession, unable to locate a single tactic in his considerable arsenal that might actually slow it down.

He had summoned three of his most capable generals earlier that same hour, had listened to each of them propose a defense built around numbers, terrain, the ordinary calculus of war he'd trusted his entire long command to. He had dismissed all three proposals within minutes of hearing them, not from arrogance but from a colder, more honest calculation he hadn't yet shared with anyone in this fortress. He had sent them out regardless, ordering the two nearest circles held at any cost, and had watched, through the same drifting smoke, as the general he'd trusted most across three centuries of service stood his ground in front of an entire fortified line and simply stopped mattering — his ranks scattering, his own weapon never once raised, the man himself left standing alone amid soldiers too confused to remember they'd been given an order to hold. Numbers had meant nothing to a force that dismantled legions without ever drawing a weapon. Terrain had meant nothing to something that walked through gates as though they were smoke.

He sat on the low stone seat that served him in place of a throne, Lament resting across his knees, its faint, familiar tremor running through the shaft beneath his hand. It had never once, in all the years he'd carried it, fully settled into his grip the way a weapon should settle into the hand that had earned it. He felt that same restless disagreement now, sharper than usual, the spear's grief pulsing at a rhythm that no longer matched anything close to his own.

He had told himself, across every one of those years, that the disagreement didn't matter. A weapon served the hand that held it, regardless of whether it approved of the arrangement, and he had made Lament serve him plenty well enough despite its constant, quiet resistance. He found that reasoning harder to hold onto now, watching the smoke resolve into yet another dismantled legion. Whatever Lament had actually been forged for, it seemed increasingly clear that his own grip on it had never once fully closed around the thing that made it worth carrying.

His fear, watching the smoke resolve into yet another dismantled legion, was not the ordinary fear of a commander facing defeat. He had planned for defeat before, had built contingencies around it the way any competent strategist built contingencies around every possible outcome. What settled into his chest now was colder than that, and considerably harder to plan around — the creeping, unwelcome suspicion that whatever he actually commanded here, however vast, might simply not matter to the thing currently walking toward his gates. He gripped Lament tighter, and felt, beneath his own hand, the spear's grief continue exactly as it always had, entirely unmoved by his need for it to feel like strength instead.

He dismissed the smoke at last, unwilling to watch any further, and sat alone in the sudden quiet of his chamber, Lament's tremor the only sound left to keep him company. Whatever waited for him at those gates, he intended to meet it standing, however little comfort that particular resolve currently offered him.

---

Thaeriel reached the gates of Dis as the smoke-dark sky above them deepened toward something that might have been evening, obsidian towers rising black and absolute against it, elite guards arrayed in ranks thick enough to suggest genuine confidence in the wall behind them. The gates themselves stood sealed, forged from black stone and older deceit, the kind of barrier that had clearly never once been tested by anything willing to actually test it.

He studied the ranks a moment before approaching, reading in their careful discipline a confidence he suspected had never once been asked to prove itself against a threat this size. They held their positions well, weapons steady, formation tight, the kind of discipline that spoke of long, comfortable years spent guarding a wall no one had ever seriously tested. None of it would matter to what he actually intended to do here.

He did not slow as he approached. He reached instead for the fear that held those gates together — the ordinary, borrowed fear of guards who had never questioned whether the walls behind them could actually withstand a serious challenge, layered over deceptions worked into the stone itself generations before any of them had been born to stand watch over it. He pressed his judgment against that foundation the way he'd pressed it against Malignus, patient, costly, unrelenting.

The gates gave way from the inside out, obsidian folding into fine black dust that drifted rather than crashed, guards scattering before the collapse reached them, weapons forgotten in their hands. This effort cost him more than any single verdict he'd handed down all day, an old gate's worth of accumulated deceit unwilling to yield without taking something real from him in exchange. He held his ground against it anyway, patient, until the last of the black dust finally settled.

Thaeriel walked through the settling ruin into a city gone entirely silent around him, exhaustion sitting heavier in his limbs than it had all day, and found Belial waiting for him in the central plaza beyond, Lament gripped tightly in one hand, the spear's faint tremor visible even from this distance. The city around them held its breath, guards and citizens alike vanished from every window and doorway he passed, as though Dis itself understood that whatever was about to happen here belonged to only the two of them.

Neither of them spoke yet. Thaeriel simply walked forward, unhurried, toward the one confrontation this entire long road had actually been leading him toward.
