# Chapter 22 — A Council of War and Faith

The council chamber held every Archangel Heaven still counted among its ranks, arranged in a wide circle beneath a ceiling that opened onto stars no mortal astronomer had ever charted. Ereloth had stayed behind at the gates, unwilling, he'd said, to spend his considerable charm on an audience that hadn't actually asked for it yet, content to wait and see what this particular room decided before he bothered introducing himself to it. Michael stood at its center instead, Azael a silent, unmoving presence just behind his left shoulder, and let his gaze move once across the assembled faces before he began — Gabriel, composed but watchful; Uriel, arms crossed, Ignis Lux resting unlit against one shoulder; Raphael, hands folded, expression carefully unreadable; a dozen others whose names Michael had once known as easily as his own.

He had stood in this chamber more times than he could easily count, in ages that no longer felt entirely his to claim, and had never once stood here as anything other than certain. The uncertainty now was its own strange weight, unfamiliar even after everything the last month had taught him to carry. He thought, briefly, of every lecture he'd delivered to a room full of skeptical students who had no idea what they were actually looking at, and found the memory steadied him more than he'd expected it to. He set the uncertainty aside as best he could and began.

He told them everything. He spoke of the Forgotten — Ereloth, Thaeriel, Azael, never himself among their number, a distinction he made plain and repeated twice to ensure no one in the chamber mistook the shape of it. He spoke of his own long role as guardian and wildcard within the reincarnation cycle, a piece that had never quite belonged to either side of the war cleanly. He spoke of the cycle's true purpose, a cage built with care rather than cruelty, meant to contain rather than punish. And he spoke, last and most carefully, of Thamorak — the echo of an ancient failure he had helped contain but never once caused, now waking hungry after an age of patient dormancy.

He kept the telling as plain as he could manage, resisting the urge to soften any part of it for easier hearing. He had learned, across nine years of standing in front of skeptical students, that a difficult truth delivered honestly landed better, in the end, than the same truth wrapped in careful diplomatic padding. He offered this council the same honesty now, whatever the immediate cost of it turned out to be, and found the old lecture-hall discipline of holding a room's attention through sheer clarity carried over more naturally than he'd expected it to.

Azael said nothing throughout the telling. He simply stood, silent and imposing, his presence alone lending weight to a story that would have sounded, from Michael's voice alone, like the ravings of a man too long removed from sanity to trust. Michael found himself grateful for that silence more than he'd expected to be, a steadying anchor at his back while he laid out a history far larger and stranger than anything this chamber had likely prepared itself to hear.

He watched the council's faces as he spoke, tracking the shift from polite attention to something considerably more uneasy the further into the history he went. He had rehearsed this testimony a dozen times on the road here, in his own head, and found now that none of the rehearsals had adequately prepared him for how it actually felt to say all of it aloud, in this exact room, to these exact faces.

---

"Thamorak cannot be fought with judgment or with fire," Michael said, once the full shape of the history had been laid out before them. "It can only be contained by restoring the balance we shattered." He explained the plan as plainly as he could manage — Aetheris, broken since the First Sundering, needing two components to be remade whole: a tear of faith drawn from Heaven's own heart, and a spark of defiance drawn from Hell's. Thaeriel had gone south to secure the second, he told them, already deep into Hell's own territory on the same errand Michael now carried here, a fact he offered as proof that this plan had already begun moving forward regardless of whatever this chamber decided about the rest of it.

He watched the chamber take that in, a dozen expressions shifting through disbelief and calculation in equal measure, before he continued toward the part he understood would land hardest. He had debated, across the long walk here, whether to soften the request before delivering it, to ease the council toward it in smaller pieces rather than laying the whole weight down at once. He had decided against it somewhere along that road. A truth this large deserved to be spoken plainly, whatever the cost of the plainness turned out to be.

"I need a tear of your purest faith," he said. "And I need you to prepare your armies to fight alongside the legions of Hell."

The silence that followed lasted only a moment before it broke entirely. Michael had expected the reaction. He had rehearsed several versions of the argument he'd need to make in its aftermath, on the long walk here, weighing which order might land the request most gently. None of the rehearsed versions survived contact with the actual noise now filling the chamber around him.

---

"Blasphemy!" Uriel's voice cracked across the chamber like a physical blow, his fist coming down hard against the council table, Ignis Lux flaring to life in his other hand before the echo of the word had finished ringing off the walls. "This is a demonic trick! You are not Michael — you are his shell, animated by a lie from the Pit!"

A dozen angels near him rose at the same instant, weapons kindling one after another in a ragged spread outward from where Uriel stood. Michael did not move to defend himself. He held his ground, watching the chamber tip toward open conflict with a speed that left no room for a careful counterargument to land before the first blow could.

"I understand why you'd believe that," Michael said, keeping his voice level against the rising noise. "I would have believed the same, standing where you're standing. I fought this war for longer than most of you have existed to remember it clearly. I know exactly what Hell has cost this Host. I am not asking you to forget a single hour of it."

"Save your understanding," Uriel snapped, flame climbing higher along the halberd's shaft. "Ally with Hell? You ask us to fight beside the very legions that murdered our brothers, that corrupted Lament, that have spent an age trying to unmake everything we've ever built. I will not stand here and listen to a demon-wrought puppet ask this council to hand Hell our trust on a silver plate."

Several of the angels arrayed near him voiced their agreement, a low rumble of assent spreading through that portion of the chamber, weapons still raised and ready. Michael counted them without meaning to, an old habit from centuries of reading a room's actual temperature rather than simply its loudest voice, and found the numbers less overwhelming than the noise itself suggested. Uriel's faction was real, and vocal, and dangerous in this exact moment. It was not, from what Michael could see, the whole of the chamber. A considerable portion of the council still stood with weapons lowered, silent, their attention moving back and forth between Uriel and Michael without settling firmly on either.

---

The chamber held on the edge of something Michael had no clean way to stop, a dozen weapons lit and ready, Uriel's faction drawing tighter around him with every passing second. Michael kept his own hands away from Solmire, unwilling to give the moment a shape that ended in violence if there remained any other path through it. He had not walked this far, through Hell's circles and a duel he'd carried no part in and a road home he'd barely dared hope would still be open to him, only to let it end in a chamber full of angels burning weapons at each other over a truth that hadn't yet finished being weighed properly.

He looked past the immediate danger of it, searching the wider circle for whatever ground still remained to stand on, and found every gaze in the chamber had turned, almost as one, toward a single figure who had not yet spoken a word since Michael's testimony ended.

Gabriel stood very still, his gaze fixed entirely on Michael's face, no part of him turning toward the fire and fury spreading through the chamber around him. Michael held that gaze and offered nothing but the plain, unguarded truth of everything he'd just told this room, nothing performed, nothing softened for easier swallowing.

"He speaks the truth," Gabriel said finally, his voice carrying clearly through the noise despite its quiet, even register. "I believe him."

The words landed in the chamber with a weight Michael hadn't fully anticipated. Several of the raised weapons hesitated mid-motion, flame guttering low along more than one blade as the angels holding them stood still, watching Gabriel rather than Uriel now.

---

The chamber did not fall silent all at once. It simply began to, weapon by weapon, flame after flame guttering low as Gabriel's words moved through the assembled ranks. Michael had watched this same council defer to Gabriel's judgment on smaller matters more times than he could count, and found the pattern holding true even now. Uriel did not lower Ignis Lux. He turned instead toward Gabriel, something raw crossing his features for the length of a single breath before his jaw set again, the familiar fury reasserting itself across his face.

"You would take his word over the evidence of your own eyes?" Uriel said. "Over everything we've fought for? Over every soldier we've buried believing the line between Heaven and Hell meant something worth dying for?"

"I would take my own eyes as the evidence," Gabriel said, "because I have looked into them, and I have found nothing in them that resembles a lie." He turned, addressing the wider chamber now rather than Uriel alone. "I am not asking anyone here to simply trust blindly. I am asking this council to weigh what we've just heard honestly, the way we would weigh any testimony brought before us, rather than dismissing it because the shape of the truth happens to be uncomfortable."

He paused, letting that settle before continuing. "I prayed for my brother's return every day of this war. I never once expected the answer to arrive shaped like this. But an answer that arrives in a shape you didn't expect is not, by that fact alone, a lie. Sometimes it is simply larger than the question you thought you were asking, and it falls to the rest of us to grow large enough to actually receive it."

Michael watched the chamber's tension shift, not vanishing entirely but loosening by a fraction, enough weapons lowering partway that the immediate threat of open conflict had, for the moment, passed. Uriel remained standing, flame still lit, his gaze cutting once more toward Michael with a fury that had not fully burned itself out. But he did not raise the halberd further.

Raphael was the first to speak into the settling quiet, his voice measured in a way that carried its own particular authority. "If even half of what's been said here is true, this council has larger concerns tonight than settling old scores over who fell and who stayed." He looked toward Michael, something careful and evaluating in his expression, though he offered nothing further to soften or sharpen it. "Let the chamber breathe before it decides anything further. A threat this size deserves more than a shouting match to answer it properly."

A murmur of agreement moved through the chamber, quieter than the fury that had preceded it but no less real for that. Michael felt something in his own chest ease slightly, not victory, not yet, but the first real sign that this council might actually finish hearing him out before deciding what to do with everything he'd brought them. In the careful quiet that followed, he understood he had, for now, been granted exactly the space he'd come here asking for — nothing more, and nothing guaranteed beyond this single, fragile moment of the council actually willing to listen.
