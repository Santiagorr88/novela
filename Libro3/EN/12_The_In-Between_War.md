# Chapter 12 — The In-Between War

Camael held the vanguard at the edge of the In-Between, where the light of Heaven's border thinned into something closer to dusk than day, and watched the demonic fleet assemble across the gap in ranks precise enough to suggest a marshal who valued discipline over spectacle. He had commanded engagements longer than he cared to number, in stretches of contested space uglier than this one, and had long ago stopped expecting any of them to feel like anything but work. He checked his own formation once, found no fault in it, and allowed himself exactly the amount of satisfaction the moment actually warranted — which was, by his own long-held standard, very little.

"Numbers?" he asked, not looking away from the gap.

"Matched, near enough," his aide answered. "Their marshal's holding position too. Waiting on us as much as we're waiting on him."

Camael had fought a dozen commanders across that same gap over the centuries, most of them content to charge the instant pride allowed it. This one held his line with a patience Camael recognized, uncomfortably, as a mirror of his own. He filed the observation away rather than dwelling on it, the way he filed away every detail that might matter later and none that mattered now, and waited for the order that would end the waiting.

He had lost soldiers on this exact stretch of ground before, names he still carried without needing to be reminded of them, and had learned across those losses to trust the plain arithmetic of preparation over anything resembling hope. He ran through the formation again in his head, checked the reserve lines, confirmed the healers were positioned where a long engagement would actually need them rather than where custom simply expected them to stand. None of it was inspiring work. He had never once needed it to be. It was, simply, the work that kept the fewest of his soldiers from becoming names on a list he already carried too many of.

"They're not testing us," his aide added, watching the same still ranks Camael was watching. "That marshal's not the type."

"No," Camael agreed. "He isn't." He had read enough reports on Hell's current marshal to know that much, at least. Whatever else waited across that gap, it would not be carelessness.

He thought, briefly, of the survivor's report that had reached his own desk some days back — a border sentry named Lyra, the only witness left standing after whatever had taken the Inverted Valley, her account too strange to fully credit until he'd read it a third time. He hadn't connected it to this crossing when the order came down to hold the vanguard here. He found himself connecting it now, watching the still air across the gap for a flicker that resembled anything she'd described.

---

Vepar watched the celestial fleet take shape across the gap, gold ranks catching a light that had no true source in this particular stretch of the In-Between, and felt none of the appetite for spectacle he suspected his soldiers expected of him. Word had reached him some hours ago, secondhand and thin on detail, that the throne wanted every front quieted while it weighed some fresh report from the war's edge. His own fleet had already been committed to this crossing a full day before that word arrived, too deep into the standoff to withdraw without inviting exactly the kind of opportunistic strike a retreat always invited. He intended to hold position and explain the delay afterward, assuming there was an afterward left to explain it in. He had heard the stories they told about this crossing — glory, honor, the kind of language that made for good songs and poor tactics. He had never once found a battle that improved for being sung about beforehand.

"Their commander hasn't moved in a quarter hour," his second reported, trident-bearers arrayed behind him in the loose, disciplined order Vepar had spent decades drilling into legions that preferred chaos by instinct. "Holding for something."

"So am I," Vepar said, and let the silence between the fleets stretch a while longer, unbothered by it. He had learned long ago that the commander who charged first was rarely the one who walked away from the field afterward. He would give this angel exactly as much patience as the angel gave him, and not one measure more.

His trident, Marea, rested across one forearm, its length catching what little light reached this stretch of the border and throwing it back in a rippling, unsteady shimmer that matched the movement of his own translucent skin. He had carried it into more engagements across the In-Between than he cared to count, had watched it turn tides both literal and otherwise more times than any song about him would ever bother to mention accurately. He trusted it the way he trusted very little else in his long command — completely, and without needing to examine why.

"You've fought this one before?" his second asked, nodding toward the gold ranks across the gap.

"Twice," Vepar said. "He didn't win either time, and he didn't lose cleanly either. That's the kind of enemy worth actually watching, instead of simply charging at." He kept his eyes on the angel's still formation, patient, unhurried, and waited for whichever of them finally ran short of that patience first.

---

The gap between the two fleets did not announce what came next with any warning either commander would later be able to name. Camael felt it first as a wrongness at the edge of his vision, a smear where the dusk-light of the border should have held its shape and instead simply didn't. He turned toward it in time to watch a hole open in the space between the two waiting armies — vast, sudden, and utterly without sound, larger already than anything his scouts had ever reported from the smaller incidents scattered across the last several months.

"Report," he said, and heard his own voice come out steadier than the ground currently felt beneath him.

"I don't — sir, it's not registering on anything. It's just not there."

He had no order ready for a threat with no shape, no numbers, no visible weapon. He had spent his whole long career building a mind built to classify exactly this kind of gap and found it, for one genuinely unfamiliar moment, offering him nothing at all to work with.

---

Vepar saw it from his own side of the gap in the same instant, and understood, watching it spread, that whatever this was had not chosen a side. It grew directly between the two fleets, indifferent to gold ranks or trident-bearers alike, expanding at a pace that made the earlier reports he'd dismissed as exaggeration look, in retrospect, almost restrained.

"Marshal," his second said, voice tight, "orders?"

He had none ready. He had built his entire long command on the certainty that any threat, properly measured, yielded eventually to enough force applied with enough patience. He held that certainty now, out of old habit, and felt it fail to find any purchase at all against the silent, spreading absence in front of him.

"Hold position," he said, because it was the only order he currently trusted himself to give.

---

Camael broke first, unable to simply watch it grow unanswered a moment longer. "Concentrated volley," he ordered. "Full width, on my mark." Holy light gathered along his ranks and loosed itself in a single disciplined wave, gold fire crossing the gap toward the spreading wrongness with the clean, practiced precision of soldiers who had never once needed to doubt what their own light could accomplish.

The wave reached the edge of the hole and simply ended there, extinguished without impact, without resistance, without anything he could later describe as a result at all. No flare. No scatter. The light did not fail the way light failed against a shield or a ward. It stopped existing, the instant it touched that boundary, as cleanly as if it had never been loosed in the first place.

He had commanded soldiers through losses he still carried the weight of, had watched formations break against odds that should have held. He had never once, until this exact moment, watched an attack simply cease to have happened. Along his own line, he heard the ranks behind him fall into a silence heavier than any order could have produced, soldiers who had trusted their own light without question their entire existence now watching it fail to leave so much as a mark on the thing they'd aimed it at.

"Again?" his aide asked, voice tight enough to betray exactly how little confidence sat behind the question.

"No," Camael said. "Not yet." He had learned, across centuries of engagements that had actually made sense, never to repeat a failed tactic simply because he had no better one ready. He held his ranks instead, watching the hole continue its patient, wordless growth, and made himself keep looking at it rather than looking away.

---

Vepar answered a heartbeat later with hellfire of his own, a black-red wave loosed from his gathered legions with the same clean discipline Camael's had carried, and watched it die at the same silent boundary, extinguished as completely and as impossibly as the light that had preceded it. He had built his entire reputation on the certainty that fire answered every obstacle eventually, given enough of it. He watched that certainty fail for the first time in a command that had never once, in all his long centuries leading it, failed to produce some result worth measuring.

"Marshal," his second said again, and this time there was no tactical question left in the word at all, only the plain, unhidden fear of a soldier watching his commander run out of answers in real time.

Vepar did not have an answer to give him. He understood, watching the hole continue its patient, indifferent growth, that neither fire nor light had ever been the right kind of weapon for whatever this actually was. His legions held their formation behind him without needing to be told to, the same discipline he'd spent decades drilling into them now serving a purpose he'd never once trained them for — standing still, weapons lowered, in front of an enemy that refused to be an enemy in any shape a soldier could actually fight.

He thought, briefly, of every campaign he'd fought and won by simple, patient attrition, wearing down whatever stood in front of him until it finally yielded. He understood, watching the silent absence continue its unhurried spread, that attrition itself meant nothing to a thing that consumed the very attempt to wear it down.

"Fall back one full length," he said finally, "and hold there. No further volleys until I say otherwise." It was not a victory. It was, at least, an order he could stand behind with some confidence, and for the moment, that would have to be enough.

---

Camael looked across the widening gap and found Vepar looking back at him from the far side of it, close enough now, with the hole's growth eating the distance between their two fleets, that he could read the other commander's face without needing any instrument to help him. Whatever he'd expected to see there — triumph, contempt, the particular satisfaction of an enemy watching him fail — none of it was present. What he saw instead looked, as plainly as any expression he'd ever learned to read across a battlefield, like fear.

He thought of every briefing he'd sat through on Hell's current marshal, every report that had painted the man as calculating, ruthless, entirely comfortable trading soldiers for advantage. None of those reports had prepared him for a face this openly unsettled, and he found, studying it, that the discovery unsettled him considerably more than any tactical surprise the marshal could have sprung on him instead. He did not give the order to charge. He held his ranks where they stood, watching the silent absence grow, and found he no longer had any ready answer for which direction the real danger was actually coming from.

---

Vepar held Camael's gaze across the shrinking distance and read there, in the stillness of a face he'd spent this entire engagement expecting to see set in triumph, something closer to his own unshaped dread than to any satisfaction an enemy commander should have been showing him. He had fought this angel twice before, had studied his tactics closely enough to predict most of what the man was likely to do in any given engagement. Nothing in either of those studies had prepared him for this particular stillness, and he understood, watching it hold steady across the widening gap, that whatever passed between them now belonged to neither of their old, familiar wars.

Neither fleet had advanced. His own legions held the single length he'd pulled them back to, and the angel's held their ground in answer, both commanders keeping their soldiers back from a wound that answered to neither gold light nor black fire. Vepar found, for the first time in longer than he could name, that the shape on the far side of the gap no longer looked like the thing he'd come here to fight.
