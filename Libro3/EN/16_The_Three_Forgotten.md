# Chapter 16 — The Three Forgotten

The valley held a silence unlike anything Michael had walked through on the way here, a stillness so complete it felt less like the absence of sound than like sound itself had agreed to wait somewhere just out of reach. Beneath an ancient oak, its trunk wide enough that several men might not have circled it hand to hand, a solitary figure sat cross-legged in the grass, robes the color of weathered stone settled around him in folds that hadn't stirred in longer than Michael could easily guess.

He did not look up as they approached. He did not need to. Michael understood that plainly enough from twenty paces out, the particular quality of a stillness that already knew exactly who was walking toward it and had simply been waiting, unbothered, for the walking to finish. The grass around the tree grew undisturbed in every direction, no worn path marking any prior visitor's approach, as though the valley itself had simply held this one clearing in reserve for however long it took the right three travelers to finally arrive at it.

Michael found himself slowing without quite deciding to, matching his pace to the valley's own unhurried patience rather than his own eagerness to close the distance. Ereloth beside him had gone quieter with every step, the easy conversation of the last two days thinning into something closer to reverence the nearer they drew to the tree.

"There he is," Ereloth said, quiet in a way Michael hadn't yet heard from him, all his usual performance stripped away and left somewhere back along the trail. "Just like I remembered him. Give or take an age or two."

They stopped a respectful distance from the tree, and only then did the seated figure open his eyes — one lit from within by a warm, familiar gold, the other holding a quiet, star-flecked dark that seemed to swallow the valley's soft afternoon light without dimming anything around it.

---

Ereloth swept into a bow theatrical enough to belong on a stage rather than in a silent valley, one arm sweeping wide with a flourish that looked, to Michael's eye, like it had been rehearsed across several centuries specifically for this exact reunion. "Azael," he said, straightening with a grin that didn't quite manage to hide something rawer sitting just beneath it. "You're looking well. Suspiciously unchanged, actually. Have you simply been sitting here this entire time?"

"Long enough to notice the grass hasn't needed cutting," Azael said, the dry economy of it landing with an ease that suggested old, well-worn banter rather than a fresh joke between strangers.

Michael offered a plainer greeting, inclining his head with the particular respect he'd learned to give beings whose age he had no real way of measuring. "Azael," he said. "Thank you for waiting."

Azael's gaze moved to him briefly, unhurried, taking his measure with none of the wariness Michael had half expected from a being who had spent this long apart from anyone resembling him. Whatever Azael found in that brief study seemed to satisfy him. He said nothing further to Michael directly, but the silence carried no dismissal in it either, only the plain, patient acknowledgment of one more presence the valley had been expecting all along.

Azael studied them both a long moment before he finally spoke, his voice arriving low and even, carrying none of the urgency the words themselves suggested. "You are late," he said. "The wound has festered." He paused, and something in his expression softened by a fraction too small to name precisely. "But you are here now. That is what matters."

He did not rise to greet them. Michael understood, watching the stillness that held around him even now, that rising had never been the point of this meeting, and that whatever authority Azael carried had never depended on standing to assert it.

Michael studied him a moment longer, taking in details he hadn't been able to gather from twenty paces out — the weathered stone-gray of his robes, worn soft by an age he couldn't begin to calculate, and the perfect balance of the two colors in his eyes, gold and star-dark holding equal weight without either one straining to dominate the other. He had met beings across his long service who wore power like armor, loud and unmistakable from any distance. Azael's power sat differently, folded so deeply into his stillness that a careless observer might have walked past him entirely without ever suspecting what actually waited beneath that quiet.

---

The air shifted before either of them could answer him, a change Michael felt first as pressure behind his own eyes, the same warning note Solmire had been carrying in its hilt since the canyon. A portal opened at the valley's edge, fire the color of frost and starlight tearing a seam into the afternoon air, silent even as it widened into something large enough to walk through.

Thaeriel stepped out of it without ceremony, and Michael felt the contrast land in his chest before his mind had finished registering the shape of it — Azael's profound, settled peace on one side of the valley, and on the other, a figure carrying visible exhaustion in every line of his posture, dust from some distant road still clinging to him, his eyes holding the particular flatness of a man who had spent recent days learning things he would rather not have learned. He carried no spear. He carried no weapon of any visible kind at all — only the bearing of someone who had walked a long way on will alone.

He said nothing. He simply stood at the portal's collapsing edge, studying the tree and the two figures waiting beneath it, his gaze moving last, and longest, to Azael.

Michael watched him with the particular attention he'd learned to give reports rather than rumors, comparing the man standing here against every account he'd ever read of the Judge's long and solitary career. None of those accounts had prepared him for the plain exhaustion currently written across every line of his posture — shoulders held with visible effort rather than natural ease, a stillness that looked, up close, less like discipline than like a man conserving whatever strength he had left rather than spending it on anything unnecessary. Whatever this valley's pull had interrupted, it had clearly pulled him away from something that was already costing him dearly.

The portal closed behind him without a sound, frost-fire folding back into the ordinary afternoon air as though it had never opened at all.

---

No one spoke for a long moment after that, and Michael found himself holding perfectly still, aware without needing to be told that whatever was happening in the space between the three of them belonged to a history he could only ever stand beside rather than inside. Thaeriel crossed the remaining distance slowly, each step deliberate, and stopped an arm's length from the tree, close enough now that Michael could see the toll Hell had already taken on him — not defeat, nothing that resembled defeat, but the particular wear of a man who had spent every reserve he carried and kept walking regardless.

Ereloth's usual performance had gone entirely still beside him, his earlier grin folded away somewhere Michael couldn't see. Azael rose at last, unhurried, and the three of them stood together beneath the oak in a configuration Michael understood, without needing the fact stated aloud, had not held this exact shape since the First Sundering itself — since the single accidental instant, however long ago that had actually been, when their three weapons had sounded together once and torn the world in the doing. Whatever had kept them apart since that moment, Michael had no way of knowing for certain. He could only watch the three of them now and read, in the careful distance each still held from the others, that the parting had cost all three of them something they had never fully finished paying.

He found himself looking between them and seeing, for the first time laid out plainly in front of him, the shape of the old stories he'd only ever heard in fragments before this — Thaeriel, pale and unyielding, judgment made into a standing man. Ereloth, still and quiet now in a way that only sharpened how much life usually moved through him, joy's own forger caught between one performance and the next. Azael, upright at last beneath his oak, balance given a body and a patient, watchful face.

He felt it building before he understood what he was feeling — a pressure gathering in the still valley air, warm gold and quiet dark and something colder threading between them, three signatures recognizing each other after a silence Michael could not begin to guess the true weight of. He thought of the accidental chord Solmire itself had shown him when its own memories first returned, three weapons sounding together once and tearing the world in the process. He understood, standing close enough now to feel the edges of that old resonance stirring faintly even unplayed, exactly how much restraint it was currently taking all three of them simply to stand this close without letting it happen again.

Solmire had gone still at his side, its earlier pull settled now into something closer to attention, as though the blade itself understood it was standing at the edge of a conversation that predated it by an age it had no way of measuring. He kept one hand near the hilt anyway, less from any expectation of danger than from the simple, steadying comfort of having something familiar to hold onto while three far older presences worked through whatever came next between them.

No words passed between the three of them. Ereloth reached out first, wordless, and gripped Thaeriel's shoulder, the gesture carrying more genuine feeling than any of his earlier theater had managed all morning. Thaeriel accepted it without flinching, though Michael noticed the faint, involuntary shift of weight that suggested even that small contact currently cost him something to hold steady through. Azael watched the two of them without moving from where he stood, an expression Michael couldn't fully read settling across that mismatched gaze — something that looked, as closely as Michael could judge it from the outside, like old grief finally permitted to sit beside something warmer.

---

Michael stayed a full step back from the three of them, watching a reunion that belonged, at its center, to a bond forged in an age before he had existed to share in anything at all. He felt no bitterness in the distance, only the plain, settled understanding of a man standing exactly where he currently belonged. This moment was not his to stand at the center of. What came after it would be, just as fully — he had felt that certainty in Solmire's hilt every step of the way here, and felt it now, standing in the gathering weight of three signatures finally reunited since the First Sundering. His place in what came next was every bit as necessary as theirs, even if this particular moment, rightly, was not his to claim.

He thought, watching them, of every council he'd ever sat through as Heaven's Commander, rooms full of voices competing to be heard first, loudest, most convincingly. Nothing here resembled that. Three beings who had once torn the world apart entirely by accident now stood close enough to do it again, and instead simply stood still, weighing the plain fact of each other's presence before risking a single unnecessary word. He understood, watching that restraint hold, exactly how much practice it must have taken each of them individually to arrive at a stillness this complete.

No one spoke again for a long while after that. The valley held the same charged stillness that had greeted Michael and Ereloth's arrival, deepened now into something that hummed faintly at the edge of hearing, three primordial presences standing close enough together, for the first time since the First Sundering, that the air around them seemed to carry the pressure of it.

Michael let the silence hold as long as it needed to, unwilling to be the one who broke it first. Whatever words eventually came next belonged, by every right he understood, to the three beings who had waited this long to say them, and he found, standing quietly at their edge with Solmire's warmth steady beneath his palm, that he was entirely content to wait alongside them for however long that turned out to take.
